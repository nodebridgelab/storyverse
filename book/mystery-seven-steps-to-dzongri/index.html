Seven Steps to Dzongri

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

A small group trekking toward Dzongri in West Sikkim wakes to find the group map missing and one hiker gone before dawn. Guide Pema Bhutia reads the trail itself to learn what really happened.

Chapter 1: The Missing Map

By the third morning of the trek, the mist lay so thick in the Prek valley that Pema Bhutia could not see his own boots. It did not worry him. He had guided people to Dzongri for nineteen years, and he knew the rhododendron forest as he knew the creaks of his own kitchen.

What worried him was the tent. The red one, at the end of the line, stood open and empty.

"Where is Mr. Karan?" he asked the five people stamping their feet around the fire.

Karan Malhotra, a talkative software engineer from Gurgaon, had joined the trek on the second day by way of a bus mix-up and had been charming ever since. He had also been carrying the group's laminated trail map, because he had insisted on being "the navigator."

"His sleeping bag is gone," said Ruchi, a retired schoolteacher from Pune. "And so is the map. He must have started early to take photographs."

"In this fog?" said Pema.

The others looked at each other, uneasy. A man who walks alone into fog, on a path that skirts a deep gorge, is a man someone ought to worry about. Pema divided the group into two. The porters, Tashi and Dawa, would stay with the fire and the trekkers. He and the youngest hiker, an eager college student named Ananya, would search ahead.

The path from camp rose through moss-hung trees. Pema walked with his head bent, reading the ground as another man might read a newspaper.

"What do we look for?" Ananya whispered.

"Stones and moss," said Pema. "A man may lie with his words, but mud and moss cannot lie."

Chapter 2: Reading the Trail

They found the first print after ten minutes, a boot with a zigzag sole pressed deep into black mud. Karan's boots, for Pema had seen them cleaned by the fire the evening before. A second print followed a few strides later, and a third, each one clear.

"He walked steadily," said Pema. "He did not run. There is no hurry in the stride."

At a fallen birch the prints turned sharply off the main trail. Pema crouched. Beside the zigzag marks were new ones, smaller, with a worn heel. "Someone was waiting here," he said quietly. "Standing a long time. See how the moss is flattened."

Ananya swallowed. "A stranger?"

"Or one of us."

They followed the two sets of prints for fifteen minutes, climbing, until the trees opened onto a stone ledge above the gorge. The mist was thinning now, and below them the river hammered white. On the ledge lay a crumpled laminated map, weighted with a flat grey stone.

Pema picked it up. Someone had drawn on it in red pen. A circle around a small blue lake on the far side of the valley, and beneath it, in a neat hand, the words: Meet me. Alone. 6 a.m.

Ananya read it twice. "This isn't Karan's handwriting. His notes in the group chat are sloppy."

"No," said Pema. "This is the schoolteacher's hand. Ruchi taught for thirty years. Nobody writes capital letters like that unless they have drawn a thousand on a blackboard."

Chapter 3: What the Moss Remembered

They found Karan beside the blue lake an hour later, sitting on a boulder with a thermos in his hands, perfectly alive and rather sheepish. And beside him, since she had hurried by a shortcut that only a teacher with decades of walking could know, stood Ruchi.

"I should have said something sooner," she told Pema. "Karan is my nephew."

"I was going to tell you all," Karan added. "Aunt Ruchi told me she wanted to scatter my uncle's ashes at Dzongri. He loved this valley, and she did not want a crowd. She asked me to meet her at six, at the lake, because the legend says the water listens. I took the map so she could mark the spot."

Pema sighed, and then laughed, a deep, helpless laugh that went on for some time. "Madam, I would have walked you here myself. You frightened a whole camp for nothing."

"I know. I am sorry." Ruchi's eyes were bright and wet. "I thought you would say the lake was too far and the weather too poor, and I did not want to be told no."

Pema looked out over the water, so still and blue under the clearing sky. "The lake is not far for anyone who has come this long," he said. "We will go back and tell the others, and we will all come, if you wish. Not as a crowd. As friends who walked with you."

They returned at noon, and the whole group climbed to the lake in the afternoon light, and stood in a circle while Ruchi poured the ashes into the clear cold water. The mist did not come back. When Pema checked the trail the next morning, the prints had already filled with dew, as if the valley itself were keeping the secret.
