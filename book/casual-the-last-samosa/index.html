The Last Samosa

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

In a Delhi office, a single leftover samosa starts a cold war between two colleagues who have never exchanged more than a nod. Settling it takes a very unusual peace treaty.

Chapter 1: The Plate on Pantry Table

Every Friday at four, Mr. Bedi from Accounts brought a box of samosas to the fourth floor of Lakshmi Towers in Delhi, and every Friday the box was empty by four-ten. It was the one tradition the company had never needed a memo for.

This Friday, Meenakshi from Compliance arrived at the pantry at four-eleven, a mistake of exactly one minute caused by a client who could not stop saying "just one last point." The box lay open. Inside, wrapped in a paper napkin like a small golden treasure, sat a single samosa.

She reached for it. So did a hand from the other side.

Tarun from IT stood there holding his empty mug, looking at her the way a man looks at a stranger who is about to take his seat on a train. They had worked on the same floor for three years. They had nodded at each other perhaps four hundred times.

"Please, you take it," said Tarun.

"No, no, you take it," said Meenakshi.

"I insist."

"I insist more."

Neither of them moved the samosa. It sat between them, cooling, as the pantry slowly filled with colleagues who sensed that something worth watching was happening.

Chapter 2: The Cold War

By Monday the samosa was gone, but the argument was not. Someone had told someone that Tarun had said he was being generous and Meenakshi had been rude. Someone else had told someone else that Meenakshi had actually given way first and Tarun had grabbed it. Neither claim was true, because nobody had eaten the samosa at all. Mr. Bedi's peon, Raghu, had quietly thrown it away on Saturday morning.

Still, a floor needs a story, and this one now had two sides. The Compliance people took Meenakshi's side and stopped lending Tarun their stapler. The IT people took Tarun's side and began taking an extra long time to fix the Compliance printers.

"My printer says paper jam," Meenakshi told Tarun on Tuesday, standing at his desk with her arms crossed.

"Is there paper jammed in it?"

"There is no paper in it at all."

"Then it is not a jam, madam, it is a philosophical problem." He smiled for half a second, and then remembered the war and stopped.

On Wednesday, the war reached the water cooler. On Thursday, a sticky note appeared on the pantry fridge that said, in capital letters, WHOEVER TOOK MY CURD KNOWS WHAT THEY DID. Nobody had taken anyone's curd. But the floor was now nervous, and a nervous floor sees thieves everywhere.

By Thursday evening Meenakshi was tired. She did not enjoy being at war. She preferred spreadsheets, which never took sides.

Chapter 3: Peace, With Chutney

On Friday at three-fifty, Meenakshi did something unplanned. She walked down to the street stall behind the building, where a man named Shankar fried samosas in a pan the size of a cartwheel, and she bought two. Then she stood in the pantry and waited.

Tarun came in at four-oh-two, perfectly on time, with the face of a man ready for combat.

"Before you say anything," said Meenakshi, "I want it noted that I am a very reasonable person."

"I have no doubt."

She held out a paper plate. On it sat two samosas, each with a spoonful of green chutney. "One is yours. One is mine. We will eat them at exactly the same time so nobody can claim anything."

Tarun looked at the plate. Then, slowly, he took one. "On three?"

"On three."

They counted together and bit down together, and the whole pantry watched in silence, as if a treaty were being signed in oil and potato.

"It is not as good as Mr. Bedi's," Tarun admitted, chewing.

"It is far worse," said Meenakshi. "Shankar uses too much ajwain."

They both laughed so hard that Raghu came running with a glass of water. And when Mr. Bedi arrived with his box at four-fifteen, he found the fourth floor sitting together on pantry chairs, Compliance and IT mixed up like cards in a deck.

"What happened here?" he asked.

"Nothing, sir," said Meenakshi. "We just settled a small matter."

Mr. Bedi shrugged and counted the box. Then he did something he had never done in eleven years. He went back down and bought a second box.
