The Exit Interview

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

An HR manager sits across from the company's best employee and hears things she cannot un-hear.

Chapter 1: The Form

The exit interview form had seventeen questions. Lata had conducted forty-three exit interviews in three years as HR head at Meridian Technologies, and she had long since stopped believing that the form produced useful data. People said what they calculated was safe to say. They gave the company a six out of ten when they meant a three. They wrote 'new opportunities' when they meant 'I cannot work another day under my current manager.'

She still ran the interviews. They were required by policy, they created a paper trail, and once in a while someone said something that made a difference.

Vijay Krishnamurthy was the company's best analyst. Technically the best—she had seen his work outputs and they were genuinely exceptional—and also the best in the harder way, the way that meant people in other departments voluntarily included him in projects that weren't his responsibility because having him in the room made the room smarter. He had been at Meridian for three years. He was leaving for a fintech start-up that was, as far as anyone could tell, paying him more and giving him a smaller title, which was unusual enough that it had been the subject of significant discussion in the management hallway.

Lata had scheduled an hour for this interview. She expected to fill forty minutes of it.

Vijay arrived at ten past four, apologised for being two minutes late (he was not two minutes late; she was), and sat down with a glass of water. He was thirty-one, quiet in the way that meant he was thinking rather than shy, and he had the particular quality of giving you his full attention when you spoke that made you feel simultaneously respected and slightly nervous.

"So," Lata said, settling into the standard opening. "I want to start by saying this is entirely confidential—your responses go into an aggregate report and aren't attributable to you individually, unless you give specific permission to share something."

"I know," Vijay said. He was looking at her in a way that suggested he found the disclaimer slightly redundant. "I've spoken to three other people who did exit interviews with you. The confidentiality is real. I know."

She made a note. She asked the first question: what was his primary reason for leaving?

"The work is excellent," he said. "I mean the actual work—the problems, the data, the analytical challenges. I've had more interesting problems here than I've had anywhere else. That's not the reason I'm leaving."

"Okay. What is?"

"The way the company treats its knowledge," he said. And then he started talking.

Chapter 2: What He Knew

He was precise, which was what she should have expected. He didn't have grievances—or if he had them, he had already processed them into observations, which was more useful and also harder to receive.

The first thing he said was that Meridian systematically prevented knowledge from moving across teams. Not intentionally—there was no policy that said 'information should be siloed'—but the incentive structure rewarded individual team outputs without any mechanism for cross-team collaboration. He had spent six months trying to get a data-sharing protocol established between the analytics team and the product team, because they were solving overlapping problems with different datasets and both reaching worse conclusions than they would have reached together. The protocol had died in three separate approval queues. Nobody had killed it; it had simply never accumulated enough priority to reach the top of anyone's list.

"So the company is producing worse outcomes than it's capable of," he said, "and has no structural interest in fixing that, because the teams are measured on their own performance and their own performance is fine."

Lata wrote this down. She had heard some version of this complaint before, but not at this level of articulation.

"What else?"

He paused. Not a long pause—he wasn't gathering courage; he was choosing precision.

"I want to say this carefully because it's about a specific person."

"It stays here," she said.

"Rohan."

Rowhan was the head of analytics. Vijay's direct manager. A fifteen-year company veteran who was, in the conventional assessment, an asset—stable, client-trusted, consistent.

"He's not a bad manager," Vijay said. "He doesn't do anything wrong, exactly. But he manages people by making sure no one on the team becomes more visible than he is. Not obviously. He does it by CC'ing himself on all external communications. By giving feedback in a way that requires sign-off from him before anything goes to leadership. By being the only person from the team who presents in cross-functional meetings."

Lata kept her expression neutral. She was thinking about the last six months of skip-level feedback, which had been uniformly positive for Rohan's team in ways that were, in retrospect, slightly undifferentiated. Everyone had said good things. No one had said great things. No one had said anything specific.

"He's not doing it maliciously," Vijay said. "I genuinely believe that. I think he's just protecting his position the way he knows how. But the effect is that no one on the team has grown in terms of organisational visibility. I've been the highest performer by any metric you could name for three years and I have a lower profile inside this company than analysts I started with who moved into other teams."

Lata put her pen down. She said: "How many other people on Rohan's team know this?"

"All of them," Vijay said immediately. "None of them will say it in this interview."

Chapter 3: After

The interview lasted ninety minutes.

Lata had filled four pages of notes, which was three and a half more than her average. On the walk back to her desk she was thinking about what she was going to do with what she had heard, which was a question she hadn't had to seriously ask in a long time.

The aggregate report was due to the CHRO at the end of the month. It would go into a document that the CHRO would scan and file. The exit interview data sat in a spreadsheet that had never, in Lata's three years, been directly cited in a management decision. She knew this because she had watched it happen. Or not happen.

Vijay's feedback would, in the standard process, become a percentage point in a category called 'growth and visibility concerns' that would sit at thirty-two percent for this quarter as opposed to twenty-eight percent last quarter, and someone might note the uptick and attribute it to industry-wide trends.

The alternative was to write a separate memo. Not a complaint—Vijay had not asked her to take action; he had asked for nothing, actually, which was the mark of someone who had given up on the system producing change. She could write a memo to the CHRO that said: I conducted an exit interview with our highest-performing analyst and he articulated a structural problem with team incentives and a specific management behaviour that is suppressing the visibility of everyone on one team. I believe this is a retention risk.

The memo would either be acted on or not acted on. If acted on, it might help the people still on Rohan's team who would not say in exit interviews what Vijay had said. If not acted on, it would at least exist somewhere in writing, which was its own form of honesty.

She wrote the memo. She was careful to frame it structurally—the incentive problem rather than the Rohan problem, which was the version that had a chance of being received as information rather than accusation. She sent it to the CHRO on Thursday.

The CHRO responded on Friday. He said: 'Thanks for flagging—let me look at the data.'

Lata did not know what would come of it. She suspected less than was needed and more than nothing, which was the realistic range for this kind of thing.

Vijay's last day was a Wednesday. She sent him a LinkedIn connection request on the day he left, which she did not do with everyone who exited. He accepted the same evening and sent a message that said: 'Thank you for the conversation. I don't know if it changes anything but I thought it was worth saying.'

She wrote back: 'It was. Safe travels.'

She sat at her desk for a while after that, looking at the exit interview form with its seventeen questions, thinking that the form was not the problem and the form was also not enough.
