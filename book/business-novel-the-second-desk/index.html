The Second Desk

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 11 chapters · Completed

Meena joins a twelve-person start-up as employee thirteen and watches it grow to eighty, losing something small and important at every funding round.

Chapter 1: Thirteen

The offer letter said 'Senior Associate, Product Operations' and the salary was modest and the equity was in the form of a ESOP grant that Meena had looked up how to value and then stopped looking because it was too complicated and probably didn't matter. She had taken the job because of a thirty-minute conversation with Aditi Sharma, the founder, in which Aditi had explained what Basecamp—a workflow tool for small professional services firms—was trying to do, and why it was harder than it looked, and what she personally was getting wrong, and Meena had thought: this is the first honest founder conversation I've had in three months of interviews.

She was the thirteenth employee. The company was in a rented floor of a mixed-use building in HSR Layout—the kind of space that had probably been a textile showroom in a previous decade and still had a certain echo in the corners that furniture had not quite eliminated. The team was twelve people when she arrived, and they had twelve desks arranged in a loose cluster near the windows, and there was a second cluster of desks at the back of the room that were empty and waiting.

Aditi had given her a desk in the empty cluster. 'You can move it wherever you like,' she'd said. 'We'll fill in around you.'

Meena had left the desk where it was. It felt presumptuous to rearrange furniture on the first day.

She spent the first week understanding what product operations actually meant at Basecamps's current scale, which was: everything that didn't fit cleanly into engineering or sales. Onboarding workflows. Integration documentation. The customer feedback pipeline that existed in three different Airtable tabs and a shared folder and Aditi's email drafts, and that Meena spent her second week consolidating into something she could read without translating.

Aditi had hired her because the company needed someone who could think across functions. Meena discovered quickly that thinking across functions was another way of saying: see the problems that no one's job title requires them to solve. She found six of these in the first month. She fixed four of them without mentioning it and mentioned two of them in the Monday standup as 'things I changed that someone should know about.'

Aditi noticed this and said nothing at the time. A month later, she mentioned it in Meena's first one-on-one: 'You fix things before you tell me, which is what I need and also occasionally makes me nervous. I'm not asking you to change it. I just want you to know I see it.'

Meena thought about this assessment for several days. It was the most accurate description of how she worked that anyone had ever given her.

Chapter 2: Seed Round

The seed round closed in March. Seventy lakhs from two angels and a small institutional fund. Aditi told the team in a Monday all-hands—they all fit in the main room, all sixteen of them by then, with coffee—and there was genuine celebration. Someone bought mango ice cream from the shop below, which was not seasonal but was enthusiastically consumed.

Meena watched the celebration and felt the rightness of it. The money was real. It meant six months of runway plus some breathing room. It meant they could make two hires they'd been delaying.

She also noticed, in herself, a small and probably unreasonable feeling that she filed under 'things I should not say out loud.' The feeling was something like: the company has a different weight now. Before the round it was Aditi's idea, funded by Aditi's belief and her team's work. After the round it was Aditi's idea plus two angels and a fund, funded by their belief and a set of expectations that lived in a term sheet.

This was not a bad thing. She knew it was not a bad thing. The company needed capital to do the thing it was trying to do.

She said none of this.

The two new hires arrived in April—a senior engineer named Roshan and a customer success manager named Pooja. Both excellent. Both hired fast, which was the pace the seed round made possible. Meena had been part of the hiring conversations for both of them, which was one of the things she liked about the company at this stage: the team was small enough that everyone had a hand in who joined it.

She had lunch with Pooja on her second day. They talked for ninety minutes, which was thirty more than they'd planned, about what the product was missing and what the customers were saying and what the gap between those two things looked like from the inside. It was the kind of conversation that produced three action items and one good idea, which Pooja then built into a workflow that cut customer onboarding time by thirty percent.

Meena wrote in her personal notes that night: 'This is what it's supposed to feel like. Someone new arrives and makes something better immediately and no one has to tell them to. Remember this.'

She did not know, writing it, that she was keeping a record.

Chapter 3: The First Process

By June there were twenty-two people.

The scaling was fast enough to be exhilarating and slow enough that you could watch individual days change. The rented floor had filled. They'd taken the floor above, which was connected by an interior staircase that everyone used as a de facto meeting point—conversations happened on those stairs that wouldn't have happened in a conference room.

Aditi asked Meena to build an onboarding process for new hires. Not just documentation—a structured experience, the first two weeks systematised so that new people were productive faster and had context without needing to absorb it all from ad hoc conversations.

'I don't want it to feel corporate,' Aditi said. 'I want it to feel like Basecamps.'

Meena built it. She spent three weeks on it, which was more than she'd planned, because the hard part was not the process itself—that was straightforward—but the question of what 'feeling like Basecamps' actually meant.

She had been here for ten months. She knew what it felt like from the inside. It felt like: your ideas get listened to faster than you expect. Problems that belong to everyone get fixed by whoever sees them first. Aditi is usually right and says so without making you feel she was waiting for you to be wrong. The team eats together on Thursdays not because it's mandated but because Shivani from design started bringing lunch for two people and it grew.

None of these things were processes. They were conditions. You couldn't put them in an onboarding document.

She put in the onboarding document what she could: the product glossary, the customer segments, the decision-making norms, the tools, the channels, the ways different teams preferred to receive information. She added a section called 'how we talk to each other' that tried to capture the communication norms without making them sound like rules.

Aditi read it and said: 'This is very good. The 'how we talk to each other' section especially.'

Meena said: 'I'm worried the things that make this place work aren't capturable in a document.'

Aditi looked at her. 'You're right. That's why we keep making the hires carefully.' She paused. 'We'll keep making the hires carefully.'

This was, Meena reflected later, both a promise and a hope, and the two things were not the same at forty people as they had been at twenty.

Chapter 4: Pre-Series A

The Series A process started in October and ran through February.

Meena was not in the investor meetings. This was correct—she was an operations person, not a founder, and investor meetings were not where she was supposed to be. But she was in the building for many of the preparation sessions, and she helped build the data room, and she heard enough of the conversations in fragments—on stairs, in the kitchen, during the moments when Aditi stopped performing and talked to the core team—to understand what the process was doing to the company's internal temperature.

The temperature was running high. Not dangerously—no one was having open conflicts, no one was leaving—but there was a quality to the working days that hadn't been there before. Decisions that had previously moved in a week were now taking three, because Aditi was in calls, because the leadership team was aligned toward external presentations rather than internal ones, because the fundraise was the most important thing happening and everything else was the second most important thing.

Meena wrote in her notes: 'The company is learning to perform itself. This is maybe necessary. I'm not sure it's cost-free.'

The round closed in February. Four point five crore. A Bengaluru VC as lead, two follow-ons. The team had grown to thirty-one people by close.

The all-hands this time was different. Not bad—it was still genuine, still warm, still Aditi being honest with the room about where they were and what came next. But the room itself was larger. You couldn't see everyone's face at once. Meena sat in the second row and noticed that three people in the back were people she didn't know well yet—hired in November, hired in December—and she felt the gap where easy recognition had been.

Afterward there was champagne, which was new. At the seed round it had been mango ice cream. At this round it was champagne from a box. Someone made a joke about it being 'the real start-up experience now.'

Meena laughed. She also filed it.

Chapter 5: The Manager

In April, after the Series A closed and the expansion plan was in motion, Aditi told Meena she was being promoted.

Head of Product Operations. Direct reports: two coordinators and eventually a fourth. Budget: real, actual budget, more than she'd ever managed before. A seat in the leadership team meetings, which meant weekly sessions with Aditi and the heads of engineering, design, sales, and the new VP of Growth who had joined in March from a larger company.

Meena accepted. She was glad. She was also, very quietly, slightly worried.

The worry was specific: she was good at doing things across functions because she could move across functions without authority, fixing things because she saw them and because no one would object to her fixing them. Authority changed the geometry of that. People behaved differently toward someone with a title.

She started managing Divya and Krishnan, the two coordinators, in May. Divya was quick, ambitious, occasionally impatient. Krishnan was thorough, careful, and had the quality of being the person everyone trusted to check their work before it went to a client. Meena had liked both of them as peers and was learning to like them as direct reports, which was different.

The first thing she noticed was that they asked her permission for things she had previously just done. Divya asked before sending a customer communication that, in Meena's pre-title life, would have gone out with a Slack notification at most. Krishnan wrote a decision memo for a process change that he had the authority and knowledge to make himself.

She sat down with both of them individually and said: I hired you because you're good at your jobs. The title difference means I need to know what you're doing but it doesn't mean you need my approval to do it. Here are the things I need to know about before they happen. Here are the things I just need to know happened. Here are the things you can handle entirely without me.

This helped. It also took three more months before the adjustment was complete, because org charts had gravity even when you tried to make them lighter.

The leadership meetings were a different education. Five people around a table, all heads of function, all talking about the same company from different angles that did not always point the same direction. The VP of Growth—his name was Karan, he was from a company with six hundred employees—spoke in a vocabulary that was slightly adjacent to the vocabulary the founding team had developed over two years, and the slight adjacency created small frictions that no one flagged as frictions but that Meena could see accumulating.

Chapter 6: Karan

Karan was not wrong about most things.

This was, in some ways, the heart of the problem. If he had been wrong, it would have been easy. He had good instincts for growth strategy, had managed scale before, and brought a pattern-matching that the company genuinely needed. The friction was not about competence. It was about orientation.

The founding team—Aditi, the original heads of engineering and design, Meena, Roshan who had been promoted from senior engineer to engineering lead—oriented instinctively toward the question 'what does this do to how we work?' Karan oriented toward 'what does this produce?' Both orientations were correct and necessary. They were not always compatible in the same meeting.

Karan proposed a tiered customer communication model in June. The logic was sound: segment the customer base by revenue contribution, allocate customer success resources proportionally, ensure the highest-value customers had the highest-touch experience. Standard SaaS playbook. Meena had seen versions of it in three companies she'd worked at before Basecamps.

Pooja, who had been running customer success since her second day and had built the thirty-percent-onboarding-improvement workflow, raised a concern. 'Our smaller customers are often the ones who give us our best product feedback. The tiered model might mean we hear less from them.'

Karan said: 'We can build structured feedback channels that don't require CS time to maintain.'

Pooja said: 'The feedback we actually use isn't structured. It comes from relationships.'

The discussion continued for twenty-five minutes. It ended without resolution, which was appropriate—it was a hard problem—but with an undertone of incompleteness that Meena thought about afterward.

She spoke to Aditi that evening. Not about Karan specifically—that wasn't the conversation. About the way the company was making decisions now versus how it made them a year ago.

Aditi listened carefully. She said: 'I know. I watch it too. I don't always know how to fix it and also build.'

Meena said: 'I don't think it needs to be fixed. I think it needs to be named.'

Aditi asked what she meant.

'When we disagree now, we resolve it by going to the deck and looking at what the data supports,' Meena said. 'That's useful. But we used to resolve it by staying in the room until everyone had said what they actually thought. Those are different processes and they produce different outcomes.'

Aditi didn't say anything for a moment. Then: 'Can you write this up? Not as a critique. As an observation. Something I can bring to the leadership team.'

Meena wrote it up. She didn't know if anything came of it. Some things did; some didn't. That was the condition of writing things up.

Chapter 7: Forty People

In September they crossed forty employees and Meena stopped knowing everyone's name.

This is not a dramatic statement. It happened gradually, the way most meaningful thresholds do. New people joined in twos and threes. She met some of them in orientation sessions, some in passing, some she knew by Slack handle before she knew their faces. By October she could look across the expanded floor—they had taken a third space in the building, connected by a fire door that was propped open permanently—and see people she recognised as Basecamps employees without knowing exactly who they were.

She remembered when she had known everyone's lunch preference.

This was not something she should mourn, and she tried not to. Scale was the point. The company was doing the thing it had set out to do, and doing it by growing, and growing meant more people, and more people meant the specific intimacy of a small team was no longer available. This was not a failure. It was a consequence.

But she noticed what changed. The Thursday lunches that Shivani had started became a formal 'team lunch' on the calendar, catered, organised. The result was attendance—everyone came, which was good—and the conversations that happened were different from the ones that had happened when it was Shivani bringing an extra portion and whoever was nearby coming to join. Organised inclusion had a different texture than organic inclusion. Both were real.

The Monday standups, which had been a genuine update session when there were sixteen people, now happened by team with a written summary compiled for the leadership layer. This was correct—no one wanted a forty-person standup—and it meant that Meena learned about whole categories of things second-hand that she had previously known first-hand.

She had a conversation with Roshan on the stairs in November—the staircase conversations had reduced but not disappeared, which felt important—about whether they were the same company they had been at seed.

Roshan thought about it. 'Same DNA,' he said. 'Different body.'

'Is that okay?' Meena said.

'I think it's what happens.' He paused. 'I think the question is whether the DNA is actually in the body, or whether it's in the stories the founders tell about what they used to be.'

This was the most precise thing anyone had said to her about the year, and she thought about it for weeks.

Chapter 8: The Second Series

The Series B process started in January.

Meena was in some of the investor meetings this time. Not all—the lead partner meetings were Aditi and the CFO who had joined in September—but the operational due diligence sessions, where the investors' teams reviewed process documentation and talked to department heads about how the company actually worked, were partly hers.

She spent two weeks preparing for a two-day review with the lead investor's team. The questions they asked were good questions. They wanted to know about the decision-making structure, the communication architecture, how problems surfaced from individual contributors to leadership, what the retention patterns looked like broken down by hire year. They were professional and thorough and they genuinely wanted to understand what they were investing in.

Meena answered their questions. She was honest.

At one point the lead analyst, a woman named Swati who was perhaps twenty-six and had the focused energy of someone who has internalised very young that preparation is the job, asked: 'Is there anything about the company's current operating model that you think presents risk that the financial data doesn't capture?'

Meena thought about this for a moment.

'We're at a scale where the informal culture that made us effective at twenty people is no longer informal,' she said carefully. 'We're in the process of institutionalising things that used to just exist. That transition is real and I think we're managing it thoughtfully. But there's a risk that we institutionalise the form of what made us work rather than the function.'

Swati made a note. 'Can you give me an example?'

'We have a Thursday team lunch. It started as a spontaneous habit and it's now a line item in the culture budget with a catering contract. More people attend than ever did when it was informal. But the quality of the conversations—the lateral connections, the serendipitous things that came from people eating with whoever happened to be around—that's changed.'

Swati looked up from her notes. 'That's interesting. Is it fixable?'

'I don't know,' Meena said. 'I think it might just be a cost of scale that you pay and then you build other things.'

The Series B closed at fourteen crore. A larger VC out of Mumbai as lead. The terms were complex in ways that Meena didn't fully understand—the CFO summarised them at the all-hands and the summary was clear but the detail was not all visible. This was reasonable. Not everything needed to be visible to everyone.

She noticed the change in what was visible.

Chapter 9: Sixty

At sixty employees they hired an HR head, which was the right decision and which changed Meena's relationship to the hiring process.

For two years, she had been part of every significant hire. Not formally—it wasn't her function—but because the team was small enough that every hiring decision touched product operations in some way, and because Aditi had made a habit of including her. She had been in the room for Roshan's final interview, for Pooja's, for the six engineers hired in the run-up to the Series A.

She was not in the room for most of the hires that happened in the six months after sixty. This was correct. The HR head—Vishal, who came from a fintech company and was meticulous—had a structured process that he had built carefully and that worked well by the metrics she could see. The time-to-hire was faster. The quality of documentation was better. The new employees arrived with clearer expectations and more structured first weeks.

She did not know the new employees the way she had known the earlier ones.

She thought about what this meant. It was not that the new employees were less good—many of them were exceptional—or that the company was less interesting. It was that she had lost something she didn't have a name for. The feeling of knowing the company by knowing the people, rather than knowing the company by knowing the organisation.

She raised a version of this with Aditi in their monthly one-on-one. She was careful to frame it as observation rather than complaint.

Aditi said: 'What would help?'

'I don't know if help is the right frame,' Meena said. 'I think I'm describing something that happens and that I need to adjust to rather than something that should be adjusted.'

Aditi looked at her. 'That's a very mature thing to say.'

'It doesn't feel mature,' Meena said. 'It feels like loss.'

Aditi was quiet for a moment. 'I feel it too. I felt it the first time we closed a round—like the company had a new shareholder and I was no longer the only person who owned the direction. I felt it when we hired Karan. I felt it when we got the first month where I didn't personally know every customer interaction.' She paused. 'I don't think feeling it means something went wrong.'

'No,' Meena said. 'I don't think so either.'

They sat with that for a moment, which was the kind of conversation that didn't need a resolution to be useful.

Chapter 10: The Staircase

In June, the company expanded to a fourth floor.

The fourth floor was connected by elevator rather than staircase. The old floors—one, two, three—still had the staircase between them, still had the conversations on the stairs, still had the texture of the place Meena had joined. The fourth floor had new furniture, a better HVAC system, and a meeting room configuration designed by someone who had read extensively about productive workspace design.

The people on the fourth floor were mainly the new growth-team hires: performance marketers, SEO specialists, data analysts with backgrounds in larger companies. They were good. They ate lunch at different times from the product and engineering teams on the lower floors, which was not a deliberate separation but was a practical consequence of team rhythms.

Meena noticed the floors had different cultures.

She walked through the fourth floor on a Thursday in July and felt, clearly and strangely, like a visitor. Not because anyone was unwelcoming—the people she passed nodded or waved, she had met most of them at some point. But the floor had its own energy that was organised around different work, different priorities, different vocabulary.

She had felt this way, she remembered, the first time she had walked through the Nexus building when she had gone to a partner meeting two years earlier. Nexus had six hundred employees and fourteen floors and the sense of walking through something large and differentiated. She had walked out of the Nexus building that day thinking: glad I'm not there.

Now she was on a fourth floor that felt like the beginning of a Nexus building, and the company was only eighty people.

She did not say this to Aditi. It was not the kind of observation that had a useful output. She wrote it in her notes.

She had been keeping the notes for three years. They ran to sixty-seven pages. They were private—not a diary exactly, but a record of observations that she thought were worth keeping. The early pages were sparse, the middle pages denser, the recent pages more uncertain in their conclusions.

She read back through them sometimes. The first entries were about the product, the customers, the processes she was building. The later entries were about the shape of the company, which was not the same thing but was increasingly what she thought about.

Chapter 11: Year Three

In August, Aditi offered Meena a new role.

VP of Operations. The entire operations function: product ops, customer success, office infrastructure, the new people-ops partnership with Vishal. A seat at the senior leadership table rather than the leadership team. Board observer rights, which was not the same as a board seat but was its own kind of signal.

Meena said she needed a week to think.

She spent the week thinking about two things.

The first was what she actually wanted. She had joined Basecamps because a founder had been honest with her in a thirty-minute conversation and she had felt the honesty as an invitation to a certain kind of work. The work she had done in three years was exactly that kind. She had built things, fixed things, kept track of things, noticed things. The VP role would change the proportion of those activities. She would do more coordinating and less building. She would manage more and notice less, or notice the same amount and have less time to act on what she noticed.

The second was what she owed the company.

This was a harder question. She had grown here. She had been given more than she'd had anywhere else—more responsibility, more trust, more access to the way a company actually worked rather than the version of it that was presented to people at her level in other organisations. That generosity had a claim on her, not in the form of debt—Aditi was not the kind of person who would frame it that way—but in the form of continuity. The company needed someone in the VP of Operations role who understood it from the inside, and she was that person.

She accepted the role.

On the day she told Aditi, she also asked something she had been sitting with for months.

'Do you still know what it felt like at twelve people?'

Aditi understood the question. She thought about it.

'Not the same way,' she said. 'I know it the way I know what it felt like before my daughter could walk. Vividly in memory, not directly anymore. The company isn't that and I'm not that, and I think I'd be getting in the way of what we're building now if I pretended otherwise.'

'But you think about it,' Meena said.

'I think about it the way I think about the first customer who paid us. As the thing that showed me what was possible. Not as the thing to go back to.'

Meena sat with this.

She thought about the desk she had left in the second cluster on her first day. Her desk now was in a different position, in the new space on the second floor, with a door she could close when she needed to think. It was a better desk by any functional measure.

She thought about what the first desk had given her that this one couldn't, and whether that was the desk's fault or her own distance from beginnings.

She thought that the company she was in was not the company she had joined, and that the company she had joined was not the company that could have built what this one had built, and that both of these things were true at the same time.

She opened her laptop. There were forty-seven unread messages and a calendar blocked solid until six. She started at the top and worked her way down, the same way she always had, moving through problems with the patient attention she had developed in the first year and had not, despite everything, managed to lose.
