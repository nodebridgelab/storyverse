The Witness Tree

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

A Mysore archivist races to prove a 200-year-old banyan tree is legally bound to a property dispute still unresolved.

Chapter 1: Notice of Removal

The notice appeared on a Tuesday, stapled to the bark of the banyan tree itself, which struck Amrita Pillai as either deeply ironic or deeply cruel, possibly both.

The banyan was on the corner of Devaraja Urs Road and a smaller lane called Ashoka Marg, and it was the kind of tree that cities grow around rather than remove: its main trunk was nearly four metres in circumference, its aerial roots had long since thickened into secondary trunks that supported a canopy covering the better part of the intersection, and its age was a matter of neighbourhood consensus rather than recorded fact, somewhere between one hundred and fifty and two hundred and twenty years, depending on whom you asked and how they felt about the tree.

Amrita asked around. She asked because the notice said the tree was to be felled in twenty-one days to allow for road widening, and because she was sixty-two years old and had walked past this tree every morning for the forty years she had lived in this part of Mysore, and because she was by profession an archivist at the regional records office, and archivists are, by training and temperament, constitutionally opposed to the permanent deletion of things that have been present for a long time.

The road widening project was real — she confirmed this at the municipal office in a conversation with a junior engineer named Prakash who was apologetic in the way of someone carrying out instructions he had not designed. The tree was in the footprint of the proposed expansion. The expansion had been approved. The order had been issued.

'Twenty-one days,' Amrita said.

'Twenty-one days,' Prakash confirmed. He offered her chai, which she accepted. 'You could file an objection. People do.'

'On what grounds?'

He listed the standard grounds: ecological significance, heritage status, religious importance. The tree was a banyan, a species that attracted automatic consideration. But it was not formally registered as a heritage tree, which would require a specific designation that had not been applied for, and its religious significance was informal — people lit lamps under it, as they did under most old trees in Mysore, but there was no formal structure, no temple designation.

Amrita returned home and sat at her desk, which was covered in the particular organized chaos of someone who processed large quantities of paper and had developed systems for it that looked like disorder to outsiders. She thought about the tree. She thought about trees in legal records. She had been an archivist for thirty-five years and she had read a great many things.

The thought arrived slowly, the way that important thoughts often do — not as a revelation but as a small nudging certainty. She had, perhaps fifteen years ago, been cataloguing a collection of documents from the late Wodeyar administrative period and had come across a deposition — a formal legal statement used in a property dispute — in which a banyan tree on Devaraja Urs Road had been described. As a landmark. As a fixed point of reference for a boundary.

She went to the records office the following morning, which was her day off, and began to look.

The argument over the banyan had been going on for eleven years. The municipality wanted the footpath widened; the footpath could not be widened without the tree coming down. The tree, for its part, stood where it had always stood: rooted, indifferent, covered in monsoon moss and the small brass nails that devotees drove in every Tuesday.

Kalyani did not believe in the tree's sacredness, not exactly. She believed in its age. Two hundred years meant the tree had stood through the founding of the municipality itself, through the district collector's office moving twice, through the fire of 1923 that took the eastern row of houses, through Partition, through four name changes for the street it grew beside. A thing that had survived all that was not simply a tree. It was a record. And you did not demolish records without reading them first.

She found the first reference in the quarterly proceedings of the Mysore Revenue Board, 1887. A dispute over the inheritance of a walled garden. The presiding officer had noted, without further explanation: 'The boundary is established by the banyan at the northwest corner, which all parties acknowledge.' All parties. Three families, two of which no longer existed by name, one of which she was not certain about. She wrote down the case number and kept going.

Chapter 2: The Deposition of 1887

The records room where she worked was a long, cool space lined with steel shelving, climate-controlled at some expense by the municipality, which understood that paper in Karnataka's humidity required specific conditions to survive. Amrita had spent much of her professional life in this room. She knew it the way a person knows a house they have lived in for decades — not every detail held consciously, but available when needed.

The documents she was looking for were in the Wodeyar civil records, which ran from the mid-nineteenth century to the early twentieth and were indexed by a system she had herself developed and implemented fifteen years ago. She pulled the relevant boxes and began to read.

She found the deposition on the second day of looking.

It was from 1887, a formal legal document in Kannada with some sections in English, relating to a dispute between two families — the Iyengars and the Nanjundappahs — over a piece of land in what was then the outer residential area of Mysore city. The land in question was approximately two acres, and the dispute concerned its eastern boundary. The Iyengars claimed the boundary ran to the east of a large banyan tree at the road junction. The Nanjundappahs claimed it ran to the west of the same tree.

The deposition was from a land surveyor named Gopalakrishna, who had been commissioned to assess the boundary in 1887. He described his methodology and his measurements, and then he wrote something that Amrita read three times to be certain she had understood it: 'In the absence of a stone marker at the eastern boundary point, I have designated the banyan tree at the junction of Devaraja Urs Road and the lane now called Ashoka Marg as the boundary marker for this claim. This tree, being of great age and fixed position and known to the residents of the surrounding area as a permanent feature of the landscape, I find to be a suitable and reliable marker. Its removal or significant alteration would constitute interference with the established boundary.'

Amrita sat back in her chair.

The tree had been designated, in 1887, as a legal boundary marker in a property dispute. Its removal, the surveyor had specifically noted, would constitute interference with that boundary. And the dispute — she turned the pages of the document — had it been resolved? She read on.

The case had not been resolved in 1887. It had been referred to a higher authority, then delayed by various administrative events, then apparently set aside. She found a reference to it in an 1894 document, still unresolved. Then nothing.

She went to the civil court records, which were kept in a different building, and spent an afternoon with a very patient young clerk named Suma who helped her trace the case through the system. The case of Iyengar vs. Nanjundappah had been filed, deferred, partially heard, deferred again, and then — in 1921, during a period of significant administrative reorganization — marked as 'pending further evidence' and essentially forgotten. It had never been dismissed. It had never been resolved. It existed, technically, in a state of legal suspension.

She called her nephew Karthik that evening, who was a lawyer in Bangalore specializing in property law.

'A tree designated as a boundary marker in an 1887 legal deposition, in a case that was never resolved,' she said. 'If I can establish that the case is still technically active, does the tree's status as a boundary marker have any legal force today?'

There was a long pause.

'In theory,' Karthik said carefully. 'A designated boundary marker in a live case — even a suspended case — has legal standing. Removing it without the court's permission would be a contempt issue, potentially.' Another pause. 'But you'd need to formally revive the case. Which means finding the successors to the original parties, which after a hundred and forty years is going to be extremely complicated.'

'Twenty-one days,' Amrita said.

'Athai,' said Karthik, using the respectful word for a paternal aunt, which was what she was to him, 'I'm going to need to move some things around.'

Chapter 3: What the Tree Witnessed

Finding the successors took eleven days.

Amrita handled the archival side. She was systematic and she was thorough and she had spent a career tracing document trails, which is essentially what genealogy is. Karthik handled the legal side, working from Bangalore via phone calls that started early in the morning and sometimes continued past ten at night.

The Iyengar family was easier. A descendant named Srikanth Iyengar had lived in Mysore his entire life, was a retired schoolteacher, and when contacted knew immediately what Amrita was referring to: his grandmother had spoken of the disputed land, of which their family now occupied roughly half in the form of an old house and its compound. The Nanjundappahs were harder — the name had changed through a marriage early in the twentieth century — but Amrita found a reference in a 1938 property transaction to a Kamala Nanjundappah who had married a man named Gopalswamy, and traced the family from there to a woman named Hema Gopalswamy who lived in a retirement community near Ooty and was, she said when Karthik called her, 'one hundred percent not surprised' that this was coming up.

'My mother talked about it,' Hema said on the phone, in a voice that suggested she had been a woman of opinions for a very long time and saw no reason to stop now. 'She said the tree was the whole point. Who owns the tree means who owns the land beside it. Is the tree still there?'

'For eleven more days,' Amrita said, from the other end of the conference call.

'Then someone should do something,' said Hema.

Karthik filed a motion in the Mysore district civil court on day thirteen, submitting copies of the 1887 deposition, the surveyor's designation of the tree as a boundary marker, and documentation of the case's suspension rather than dismissal. He argued that the tree's removal would interfere with a pending case and should be stayed until the matter was resolved.

The judge assigned to the motion was a methodical woman named Justice Revathi who had, she told the courtroom during the brief hearing on day fifteen, seen a fair number of unusual property matters in her career, but not a boundary dispute that hinged on a living tree.

She granted a temporary stay.

The notice was removed from the tree's bark on day sixteen. Prakash, the junior engineer, called Amrita to let her know. He sounded, she thought, somewhat relieved.

The substantive case — what the land dispute actually meant a hundred and forty years later, when both families occupied different portions of the contested area, when roads had been built and houses erected and the landscape rearranged by a century of life — took eighteen months to resolve. Srikanth Iyengar and Hema Gopalswamy, who had spoken for the first time on Karthik's conference call and had subsequently become friends in the unsentimental way of old people who have discovered unexpected common ground, agreed to a settlement that acknowledged the historical claim while accounting for actual occupation. The road widening project was rerouted along an alignment that the municipal engineers had initially considered less convenient but which turned out, when the final design was complete, to be marginally better from a traffic-flow perspective.

The banyan tree remained.

Amrita was asked, by a journalist who wrote about the case for a heritage magazine, whether she had expected to succeed.

'I'm an archivist,' she said. 'I don't expect things. I look for what exists and try to understand it. The tree existed. The record of what it witnessed existed. That was enough to start.'

'The tree witnessed a property dispute,' the journalist said. 'That's not usually what we mean by witness.'

'A marker is a witness,' Amrita said. 'Something that holds its position over time and tells you what was true when it was placed. That's all a witness is, really. Standing in one place long enough to be evidence.'

She still walked past the tree every morning. The aerial roots had continued their slow work, thickening, reaching toward the ground, building the next generation of support. In a hundred years, if the city continued to grow around it as cities do, it would be larger still, its canopy wider, its roots deeper into the ground where the old boundary line ran.

Amrita occasionally placed her hand on the main trunk as she passed, briefly, the way you might acknowledge someone you had known for a long time. The bark was warm in the morning sun, rough and deeply ridged, the texture of extreme age.

The tree, as it always had, said nothing. It simply remained.
