The Borrower

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

Kavitha borrows everything from everyone — until the day she finally has something to give back.

Chapter 1: The List of What She Had Taken

If Kavitha sat down and made an honest accounting — which she had never done but which she sometimes assembled involuntarily at three AM — the list would begin with small things. Sugar, at least fifteen times from her neighbour Janaki, because she always underestimated how much she needed and the shop closed early on Sundays. Her colleague Deepa's phone charger so regularly that Deepa had simply started leaving a spare at Kavitha's desk as a fixed arrangement. An umbrella from Srinivas at the office which had not been returned for eight months because she kept forgetting and he kept not mentioning it in a way that was beginning to feel pointed.

Then the medium things. Her sister's mixer-grinder for a week that had become three weeks. The vacation guidebook from her friend Nandini that she had not read and had left on a flight to Bangalore. The recipe notebook from her mother, borrowed to cook for a dinner party, still on her kitchen counter a year later.

Then — and this was the part that caught at her at three AM — the intangible things. Her college roommate Shruti's evenings, consumed regularly by Kavitha's romantic crises. Her husband Vikram's patience, borrowed in long quiet withdrawals that she did not always notice until she saw the shape of his silence. Time. Other people's bandwidth. The emotional equivalent of phone chargers, needed in sudden urgent moments and returned — if at all — imperfectly and late.

She was not a bad person. This was important to understand and she understood it. She was just someone who had grown up in a house where borrowing was the texture of daily life — her mother borrowed from the neighbours, her neighbours borrowed back, it circulated — and she had never quite developed the equivalent reflex in the other direction.

The thing that changed it was not a confrontation. Nobody confronted Kavitha — she was warm and funny and the sort of person you wanted to stay in favour with, and also people were not sure how to bring up borrowed umbrellas without it becoming a thing. What changed it was a Sunday when Janaki knocked on her door and instead of asking for sugar — which Kavitha had steeled herself for, having used the last of hers in chai that morning — Janaki stood in the doorway looking pale and said, "I got some bad news. My mother. Can I sit here for a bit?"

Kavitha opened the door wider. "Come in," she said. "I'll make tea."

Janaki sat. Kavitha made tea. She did not speak. She did not offer advice. She sat across from her neighbour and let the silence be what it needed to be, which was something she had learned from Shruti years ago, absorbed without knowing it.

Janaki cried for a little while. Kavitha handed her a tissue from a box she had borrowed from Deepa's desk two weeks ago and hadn't returned.

Chapter 2: What She Had That She Did Not Know

Her friend Nandini called the following Thursday, which was Nandini's lunch break and Kavitha's work from home day, and they talked the way they had been talking for fifteen years — in overlapping sentences, with sudden subject changes, covering four topics at once. Nandini worked in HR and had a theory about everything and was usually right.

"You're doing that thing," Nandini said, approximately twenty minutes in.

"What thing."

"Keeping score against yourself. The borrower list. I can hear it."

Kavitha had not mentioned the three AM accounting. Nandini had a way of hearing things she had not said, which was either a gift or slightly unsettling, depending on the day. "I was just thinking," Kavitha said, which meant yes.

"The thing about you," Nandini said, in the tone of someone who had prepared this, "is that you borrow tangible things because you're a disaster with planning. Fine. But what you actually give is intangible things. Which is harder to inventory."

Kavitha looked at the ceiling of her flat. "That sounds like something you'd put in a LinkedIn post."

"It's still true. You sat with Shruti the whole night before her board exam because she was panicking. You researched Deepa's father's treatment options for three days and made her a document because she was too upset to think. When my marriage was bad and I couldn't tell anyone else, you listened for six months without telling me what to do, which is the hardest thing in the world for you because you have opinions about everything."

Kavitha was quiet for a moment. "I still have your recipe notebook."

"I know. Keep it. I got the recipes from Instagram now anyway."

She didn't mention that the recipe notebook had a custard recipe in it that had turned out to be the best thing at her dinner party, and that she had thought about Nandini every time she made it since, which was four times, which was more sentiment than she usually attached to borrowed objects.

That weekend she bought a bag of sugar. She bought a large bag — not a small one that would run out and require borrowing — and wrote Janaki's name on a small label she stuck to a portion she measured out in a steel container. She knocked on Janaki's door.

"This is for the fifteen Sundays," she said.

Janaki looked at the container and then at Kavitha with an expression that Kavitha had not anticipated — not surprise, but something like recognition. "You counted."

"Approximately."

"Come in. I made pakodas."

Chapter 3: The Direction of It

The umbrella she returned to Srinivas at work, which required a specific errand to his floor that she had been avoiding for eight months and took five minutes. He looked at it, then at her, and smiled with the relief of someone who had been not-mentioning-the-umbrella for so long that the not-mentioning had become a small private burden.

"Thank you," he said, which covered both the return and the fact that she was the one who brought it.

"I'm sorry it took so long," she said.

"It's raining season now anyway," he said. Which was generous but also true.

The mixer-grinder went back to her sister with a cheesecake — because Kavitha had taught herself to make cheesecake during the period she had the mixer-grinder, and the cheesecake was the best thing to come out of the whole borrowed-appliance arrangement, and her sister deserved the cheesecake on general principle.

The phone charger situation with Deepa she could not fully solve — she was a phone charger borrower in the way some people are umbrella borrowers, constitutionally, probably forever. But she bought a third one for her desk specifically to have one to lend, which was not perfect but was a direction.

She thought about what Nandini had said. About the intangible things being harder to inventory. She thought about this because she knew Nandini was right and she also knew that knowing it was right was not the same as accepting it, which required a different operation — the willingness to count what you gave and not only what you took.

She called Shruti on a Tuesday night, no particular reason, just to talk. Shruti, who worked a difficult shift job and was usually tired, picked up and said, "Kavitha, why are you calling on a Tuesday?"

"No reason. How are you?"

There was a pause. "Actually I'm having a bit of a bad week. My manager is impossible and I've been overthinking everything and—"

"Tell me," Kavitha said.

She listened for forty minutes. She did not offer advice. She asked one question, the right one, at the moment Shruti needed it. She stayed in it the way she had stayed with Janaki — not performing presence but actually being there, which was a different and more difficult thing.

When she hung up, she sat for a moment in her flat and thought about Nandini's inventory. The account was not balanced — she thought it probably never would be, not entirely, and that this was all right. The direction mattered more than the settlement. She had been flowing in one direction for a long time. It turned out you could simply begin flowing in both.
