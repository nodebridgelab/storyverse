Power Cut at Eleven

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

On the hottest night of May, the whole colony loses power and every single person ends up on the terrace.

Chapter 1: The Night the Fans Stopped

The power went at eleven-fifteen on the fourteenth of May, which the weather department had that morning declared the hottest day in the city in eleven years and which felt, to everyone lying in their respective flats under their respective ceiling fans, like a statement that could not possibly need statistical confirmation.

The fans slowed. Then they stopped. The silence was total for exactly three seconds, and then it was replaced by the sound of thirty-two flats simultaneously becoming aware of their situation. The colony — Kiran Vihar, forty-two years old, 200 metres from the main road, two gates and a watchman's cabin that had not had a watchman for six months — had twelve houses arranged around a central courtyard and a terrace above the common area where nobody went except the water tank man.

Within twelve minutes the terrace had eight people on it.

Within twenty minutes it had nineteen.

By eleven forty-five, which is when the last family came up — the Mehras from house seven, the mother carrying a sleeping child who remained asleep through the whole move with the extraordinary resilience of small children — there were thirty-one people on a terrace that was warm from the day's accumulated heat but had, crucially, a breeze. A real breeze. The kind that had apparently been up here all evening while everyone lay inside in the still, collected, personal heat of their rooms wondering why they were so hot.

Ramesh, who was sixty-four and had lived in house three for thirty years and remembered when the colony had a generator that worked, had brought up his cot. He had done this with the unhurried certainty of someone who knew exactly what kind of night this was going to be and had decided to be comfortable within it. He lay on his cot and looked at the sky, which was not particularly starry because of the orange glow of the city's lights, but was undeniably sky.

"Arrey," said Sunanda from house nine, who was twenty-eight and had moved in two years ago and was still at the age where a power cut at midnight was an incident rather than an event, "why is this place so nice? How did we not know this was here?"

"We knew," said Ramesh's wife Kamala, who had brought up the cot cushions and arranged them under her husband with the efficiency of long practice. "We just never came."

Chapter 2: What People Brought

People brought things when they came up, because this is what people do — they do not arrive empty-handed to a gathering, even an accidental one. Prashant from house two brought a watermelon because he had bought it that afternoon and not cut it yet and it seemed like exactly the right moment for a watermelon. His son, who was eleven, was dispatched back down for the knife and came back up with the knife and also a bag of chakli that he produced from some personal reserve.

Mrs. Nambiar from house eleven brought a thermos of coffee because she always had coffee ready at eleven regardless of circumstances. Mr. Nambiar, who was taller than every doorway in the colony at six foot two and had to bend whenever he moved between rooms, brought a hand fan that he operated constantly and aimed with democratic generosity at whoever was nearest.

The Dubey family from house one — parents, grandmother, two teenagers — came up together in a formation that suggested some brief internal negotiation had taken place before they arrived. The grandmother, who was eighty and moved with a stick and the absolute authority of someone who has earned it, was given Ramesh's cot immediately without any discussion. She accepted it without ceremony and lay down.

"Last time we had a cut this long," she said to the ceiling, which was the open sky, "was '98. I had seventeen people in my kitchen because the whole lane came."

Sunanda, who had settled herself on a chatai someone had produced from somewhere, said, "What did you do?"

"Talked," the grandmother said, as if this was obvious. "What else is there to do when there is nothing else to do?"

The watermelon was cut and distributed. The chakli circulated. Mrs. Nambiar poured coffee for anyone who wanted it, which was more people than she had thermos for, so a second thermos was requested and arrived via the Dubey teenagers who ran down to get it and came back winded and triumphant.

By midnight the terrace was doing something a terrace hadn't done in maybe twenty years. People were sitting in the dark and the heat and the intermittent good wind, and they were talking — not about anything important in the way that important is usually meant, but about the things that come out only in the dark, in accidental proximity, when there is nothing on a screen demanding attention and the night stretches long and humid around you.

Chapter 3: What the Night Made

Ramesh told the story of the colony's founding — he had been there for it, young then, new transfer job, new wife, this place still half-built, the trees barely taller than a man. He told it with the pleasure of someone who rarely gets to tell it but has been carrying it for years. The Dubey teenagers listened in the way young people listen to old stories when they don't have phones to look at — skeptically at first, then genuinely.

Sunanda told everyone about her grandmother's village in coastal Andhra, where the power went three times a week minimum and the whole neighbourhood moved outside as a matter of course. "She says people were happier then," Sunanda said. "I used to think that was just old person logic. But this" — she gestured at the terrace — "this is kind of what she meant."

Prashant's eleven-year-old fell asleep against his father's arm with the chakli bag still half in his hand. The Mehra child was still asleep on a folded shawl someone had donated. The grandmother in Ramesh's cot was asleep by one and snoring with the serene volume of someone who had decided the world's problems were someone else's tonight.

The power came back at two-seventeen. The fans started — you could hear them, distant and then present, as house after house blinked back to life across the colony. Someone cheered from a window below.

Nobody moved immediately.

This was the thing that Sunanda thought about later, and Ramesh's wife Kamala, and Prashant, and the Dubeys — that when the power came back and the option to go back inside appeared, they all stayed for another half hour without deciding to. The conversation ran down naturally, not cut off. People stood slowly, gathered their things — the thermos, the chatai, the cot cushions, the sleeping children carried carefully. Goodnight was said in a dozen directions.

In the morning the colony WhatsApp group, which was usually for water bill reminders and gate timing disputes, had a message from Sunanda: "That was genuinely the nicest night I've had since moving here. Same time next power cut?"

Thirteen laughing emojis. Then Ramesh, who typed very slowly and did not use punctuation: "no need to wait for a power cut. First sunday of month. Six pm. Terrace. I will bring the cot"

Eighteen people said yes before breakfast was over.
