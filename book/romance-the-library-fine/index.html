The Library Fine

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

A man returns the same library book twelve times, and the librarian has stopped pretending not to know why.

Chapter 1: Borrowed

The first time Sameer borrowed Selected Poems of Faiz Ahmed Faiz, it was a Tuesday in February. He had come in because of the rain — the old Baroda city library was the nearest building with an open door — and he had taken the book from the poetry shelf because he had needed something to do with his hands while he waited for the downpour to ease. He had sat in one of the reading chairs by the window and read two pages before he became aware that the librarian was watching him.

Not unpleasantly. She was behind the circulation desk, cataloguing, and she looked up from her work periodically with the easy unselfconsciousness of someone used to observing. She wore her hair pinned up with a pencil actually threaded through it, which he thought was either very absent-minded or a very deliberate statement, and she had small round glasses that she pushed up her nose when she read. She did not smile at him. She was simply — present, in the way certain rooms are present, making you feel contained and calm.

He checked out the book when the rain stopped. He was not a member. He became one in the three minutes it took her to process his form, and he left with the book under his arm and the receipt in his pocket and the feeling that he had done something quietly significant.

He read the book twice and returned it the following Tuesday, which was, he told himself, simply because Tuesday was a convenient day.

She processed the return without comment. He looked at the shelves for seven minutes. He checked out a collection of Gulzar translations. He went home.

He returned the Gulzar the following Tuesday. He borrowed Faiz again.

She looked at the book and then at him. One look. Then she stamped the slip without a word, and he took the book and left, and on the stairs outside he stood for a moment wondering what exactly he was doing.

He was doing it deliberately. This was the honest answer. He had thirty-seven books at home he had not read. He chose to come here and borrow this one. He chose to come on Tuesdays.

Chapter 2: The Fine

By the fifth visit, she knew his name. Meera — he had seen it on the badge she wore on alternate days, the one that said MEERA, LIBRARIAN, BARODA CITY LIBRARY — had a habit of sliding his membership card back across the desk rather than handing it, and he had a habit of not quite picking it up immediately, so for a moment his fingers and her fingers were in proximity to the same small plastic card. This was the kind of thing he noticed.

In April he kept the Faiz book one day too long, accidentally but also not accidentally. The fine was two rupees per day. She told him this with complete composure. He paid it with complete composure. She stamped his slip. She did not comment on the fact that this was the third time he had borrowed this particular volume.

She commented on it in May.

'You've borrowed this six times,' she said, looking at the card. Her voice was very even. 'Since February.'

'I reread things,' he said.

'Six times in three months is rereading?'

'I read slowly,' he said.

She looked at him over her glasses. She had remarkable eyes. 'It's 136 pages.'

He said, 'The poetry requires sitting with.'

A pause. She pulled up his borrower record on the old desktop system. She looked at the screen. She said, still very evenly, 'You have a fine of thirty-four rupees outstanding. Across several visits.' She looked at him. 'You always pay it. You never pay it before it's due.'

He said nothing to this.

She slid his membership card back across the desk. He picked it up without the usual pause. He said, 'I'll clear the fine today.'

'The fine is twelve rupees currently,' she said.

He paid it. He took the book. At the door he stopped and turned. She was already looking at him. Not in the evaluating way from the beginning. In a different way.

'I've been meaning to ask,' he said. 'The translation in this edition — I always feel like something is being lost in the third stanza of Mujh Se Pehli Si Mohabbat. Do you agree?'

She considered him. Then she said, 'Come back on Thursday. I'll have looked at three other editions by then.'

He came back on Thursday with a folder of comparative translations he had compiled from the university library and she was waiting at the desk with a cup of tea she had not offered him but placed on the desk in front of him without ceremony, and they argued about Faiz for two hours while the library emptied around them.

Chapter 3: Returned

In June, Sameer borrowed a different book. It was a deliberate departure and Meera noticed it immediately — he saw her notice, saw her glance at the title, saw her look up at him briefly before she processed the slip. The book was a translation of Premchand. He had chosen it because she had mentioned once, briefly, that she preferred prose.

She did not comment. But the following Tuesday the Faiz was on the desk before he reached it — she had left it at the circulation desk, he realized, kept aside. Without being asked.

'In case you wanted it,' she said.

'I was going to try something new,' he said.

'And?'

'I missed the Faiz,' he said.

They looked at each other across the desk. In the reading room behind her, two college students were arguing quietly about a footnote. The ceiling fan turned. Outside, the afternoon light was the colour that Baroda's light gets in June, low and golden and hazy.

'There's a reading,' she said, 'next Saturday. At the cultural centre. A poet from Delhi. I'm going.' She straightened a stack of return slips that didn't need straightening. 'You might find it interesting.'

He had already heard about the reading. He had seen the poster two weeks ago and spent ten days deciding whether to go. He looked at the book in his hands and then at her — at the pencil still threaded through her hair, at her glasses slightly crooked, at her expression of careful neutrality that he had spent four months learning to read.

'I might,' he said. 'What time does it start?'

'Seven,' she said. 'I'll be there at six-thirty.'

He returned the Faiz on Friday. All the way across the desk, no pausing. He had bought his own copy. He didn't say so.

On Saturday he was at the cultural centre at quarter past six. She arrived at six-twenty, and she saw him immediately, and she stopped, and then she came across the courtyard to where he was standing, and she said, 'You're early.'

'So are you,' he said.

'I didn't want to miss the beginning,' she said.

'Neither did I.'

They stood in the warm evening, surrounded by the smell of old stone and marigolds that someone had strung along the entrance, and he thought: he had returned that book twelve times. He didn't plan to return this.
