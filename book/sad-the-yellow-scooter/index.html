The Yellow Scooter

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

The last breakdown of a beloved yellow scooter asks a young woman to decide what she is willing to let go.

Chapter 1: Aktara

The scooter's name was Aktara, which Nandita had given it at twenty-two when she bought it second-hand from a mechanic in Pune who had repainted it yellow himself. He had charged her extra for the colour, which she had paid without negotiating because the yellow was important. She had been twenty-two and had just gotten her first real job and the yellow was the colour of the version of herself she was trying to become.

That had been nine years ago.

Aktara broke down on a Tuesday on the road between Nandita's office and her apartment. It was not the first breakdown — there had been the carburetor issue of 2019, the mystery electrical fault that took three mechanics to diagnose, the clutch cable that snapped on a hill which Nandita did not like to think about. But those had been fixable breakdowns, the kind that came with a diagnosis and a timeline.

This one was different. She knew it from the sound, which was not a dramatic sound but a final one, a kind of quiet giving up that she had not heard Aktara make before.

She pushed the scooter to the side of the road. She called her mechanic, Vinay, who had been Aktara's mechanic for the last six years. He came in twenty minutes on his own bike and crouched beside the scooter and started his examination. Nandita stood beside him and tried to read his face.

Aftter a while he stood up. He did not say anything immediately, which was its own kind of sentence.

Okay, Nandita said. Tell me.

Vinay told her. The engine was going. The cost to rebuild it would be more than the scooter was worth. He could do it if she wanted — he would do a good job — but she should know what she was paying for.

Nandita looked at Aktara. Yellow paint over a second-hand frame, scratched in two places on the left side from a fall she had taken in 2020, the handlebar grip on the right side slightly worn where her hand always rested. She had put thirty thousand kilometres on this scooter. She had learned, on this scooter, to exist in a city as herself.

Chapter 2: What She Was Keeping

She did not decide that day.

She had Aktara towed to Vinay's garage and she took a rickshaw home and she sat on her balcony with a cup of tea and tried to think about it practically.

The practical case was clear. The scooter was nine years old. The engine rebuild would cost more than buying a decent used scooter. She had a stable salary now. She could afford something newer, something that started reliably in the winter, something with a working odometer. She was thirty-one years old. She did not need to be sentimental about a machine.

But she knew that the argument was not really about practicality.

When she had bought Aktara she had been twenty-two and newly employed and somewhat scared of the city and somewhat scared of herself. She had ridden through Pune on that scooter through a period of her life that had been — she could say this now, with the distance of years — genuinely hard. Not catastrophically hard, not the kind of hard that gets named, just the ordinary accumulated hard of being young and uncertain and far from the people who knew her before she was anything in particular. She had ridden through it. The scooter had been there.

She had sat in traffic on Aktara and cried without anyone around her noticing. She had gone on a first date that she had ridden home from giddy and hopeful, and a last date that she had ridden home from more quietly. She had driven to the hospital when her father had his procedure, and afterwards she had sat in the parking lot on the scooter for a while before going inside. She had ridden home on monsoon evenings soaked through and not minding because there was something about rain on the streets at dusk that she would never get tired of.

The scooter was not the memories. She knew that. The memories were hers regardless. But the scooter was the physical fact of that time, the object that had been present, and she found that she had feelings about objects being present.

Chapter 3: Tuesday, Two Weeks Later

She went back to Vinay's garage on a Tuesday two weeks after the breakdown. She had been thinking. She had decided, and then undecided, and then decided again.

Vinay was working on another bike when she arrived. He wiped his hands and took her to where Aktara stood in the corner of the garage, patient and yellow and very slightly dusty.

I'm not going to fix it, she said.

Vinay nodded. He was not the kind of person who said things like: are you sure?

Can you find it a good home? she asked. Not scrapped. I want it to go to someone who needs a scooter. Someone who'll use it even if they have to fix the engine. If you give it to someone who's going to scrap it I'll be upset.

Vinay smiled. He had known her for six years. He knew she meant this. I know someone, he said. Young guy, works in the garage two streets over. Saves up. He'll do the engine himself.

Good, Nandita said.

She asked if she could have a few minutes, and Vinay went back to his other bike without comment.

She stood beside Aktara and put her hand on the handlebar the way you put your hand on the handlebar after nine years, without thinking, muscle memory finding the worn grip on the right side automatically. The yellow paint was still bright in places. The scratches on the left side were still exactly the same. The odometer, which had stopped working for unrelated reasons three years ago, was frozen at a number she no longer remembered meaning anything.

Thirty-one felt nothing like twenty-two. That was not a bad thing. That was just time doing what time does.

She said what she needed to say, quietly, to the scooter, which was either the action of a person who understood that some goodbyes require words or the action of a slightly unhinged person, and she was comfortable with the ambiguity. Then she walked out of the garage into the afternoon, and did not look back, and took the bus home.
