The Clock Winder of Fort Kochi

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

A priceless pocket watch vanishes from a locked glass case in a heritage hotel. Old Joseph Fernandez, who only came to wind the lobby clock, listens harder than anyone else and hears the answer.

Chapter 1: The Empty Case

Priya Menon found the case empty at half past six, and she stood looking at it for so long that the lobby clock struck the quarter before she remembered to breathe. The glass was unbroken. The little brass lock was shut. On the blue velvet lay a dent in the shape of a watch, and nothing else.

The Harbour Rest Hotel in Fort Kochi had been built by a Dutch trader's grandson, and the pocket watch was its pride, a silver hunter with a cracked enamel face that had not run since 1952. Guests photographed it every day. Now the photographs would show only velvet.

"Nobody has the key but me," Priya told the policeman on the phone. "And I never left it."

"Keep everyone calm," the voice said wearily. Four guests had been in the lobby since five o'clock, waiting out the evening rain. Priya gathered them near the cane chairs and apologised, and nobody said much until a thin, careful voice spoke from the corner by the grandfather clock.

"Forgive me. I only came to wind this." It was Joseph Fernandez, seventy-eight, who had wound the hotel clock every Thursday for thirty-one years. He held his winding key like a rosary. "Might I ask when the case was last seen whole?"

"Ten past five," said Priya. "I dusted it."

"Then the watch left between ten past five and half past six." Joseph nodded slowly, as if the number were a pleasant surprise. "Rain has kept everyone indoors. That is a help."

In Fort Kochi, people said old Joseph had once found a lost ring by listening to a drain.

He did not examine the glass or the lock. He simply sat down in a chair, set his hat on his knee, and closed his eyes. The grandfather clock ticked beside him, heavy and slow.

"Thursday is quiet," he murmured. "I can hear properly."

Chapter 2: Four Guests

There were four of them, and Priya introduced each as if reading a register.

Mr. Hendrik Van Dijk, a tall Dutch collector with silver hair, had arrived on Tuesday and asked three times to see the watch up close. Dr. Lakshmi Iyer, a historian from Thrissur, was writing a book about the spice trade and had spent every afternoon copying the hotel's old ledgers. Biju, the young waiter, had carried tea to the lobby at six and polished the tray rail afterward. And a quiet student named Farhan, who was staying in the cheapest room, had come down only for a book.

"I did not touch that case," Van Dijk said at once, patting his grey raincoat. "I admired it, that is all. A fine piece. Very sad that it does not run."

"I was in the ledger room until six," said Dr. Iyer.

"I only carried the tray, madam," said Biju. Farhan shook his head.

Joseph opened his eyes. "Dr. Iyer, which ledger did you read this afternoon?"

"Nineteen fifty-one. Why?"

"The watch stopped in fifty-two. Interesting." He turned to Van Dijk. "Sir, has your raincoat been wet through? It is a long way from the harbour."

"I walked quickly. It is dry now." The Dutchman smiled thinly, and Joseph smiled back as if they had agreed on something.

"We have a clue," said Joseph, "and it has been in the room all along. Come and stand beside me, everyone. Quietly, please."

They stood in a half circle on the cool tiles. The grandfather clock ticked, and the rain went on.

Then, very faintly, a second ticking joined the first. It was lighter and quicker, a tiny brisk heart.

"A watch that had not run for seventy years," said Joseph softly, "does not start by itself. Someone shook it carrying it. A cracked balance wheel only needs a jolt to wake up. And it will keep going now, for an hour or two, wherever it is hidden."

Chapter 3: What the Pocket Said

Nobody moved. The little ticking came from the direction of the coat stand, and then, undeniably, from the grey raincoat that Mr. Van Dijk had been patting so often.

He had stopped patting. His hand was frozen at his side.

"Sir," said Priya, very gently, "would you open your pocket?"

For a moment the Dutchman looked ready to argue. Then his shoulders dropped, and he drew out the silver watch. It lay on his palm, ticking, as shocked as the rest of them. "I did not think it would wake," he whispered. "In my whole life I have never stolen anything."

"Why now?" asked Dr. Iyer, not unkindly.

He told them slowly. His grandfather had been the Dutch trader's partner in Kochi, and in 1951, ruined by debts, the old man sold his own watch to the hotel's founder for rent money. Hendrik had come to see it just once, and then the lobby was empty, because Priya had stepped into the office. A little wire from his wallet had done the rest.

"The ledger," said Dr. Iyer quietly. "I read it this afternoon. Nineteen fifty-one, a sale of one silver watch, and a note in the founder's hand. 'To be returned to the family if ever they ask.'"

She turned the ledger page toward Priya, and there it was in faded blue ink.

"Mr. Van Dijk," she said, "you should have asked. I will call the owner tonight and ask him to honour the founder's note. But the watch stays in the hotel until the paperwork is done. Is that fair?"

"It is more than fair," said Van Dijk, and his eyes were wet.

She set the watch back on the velvet, still ticking, and closed the little brass lock. The grandfather clock struck seven, and Joseph Fernandez finally wound it, humming as he worked.
