The Pivot Call

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

At 1 a.m. on a Google Meet, three co-founders must decide whether to pivot or die; they cannot agree.

Chapter 1: Eleven Forty-Five

The call was supposed to start at eleven. It was eleven forty-five before all three of them were on it, which was already a signal about how the next two hours were going to go.

Sameer was in his Mumbai apartment, in the kitchen because his wife was asleep and he hadn't wanted to disturb her with what he knew was going to be a loud conversation. There was a half-empty glass of water in front of him and he kept moving it from one hand to the other. Nandita was in the Bengaluru office, which she still went to every day even though it was just her most of the time now—the team had been working remote since the second month of the runway crisis. She had the lights on only in her corner of the room, so most of the background on her screen was dark. Ashwin was in Delhi, at a co-working space that was technically closed but where he had an access card and the building wifi still worked.

"Okay," Sameer said. "We all read the memo."

The memo was eleven pages that Nandita had written over the previous week. It was a clear-eyed assessment of their current position—eighteen lakh in runway, two and a half months at current burn, a core product that was technically sound and commercially stalled, and three investor conversations in the past two months that had all ended at the same place: 'come back when you have more revenue.' Three investor conversations was twelve investor conversations, actually, because Sameer had been having the other nine and hadn't wanted to put them in the shared memo until he understood why they kept landing the same way.

"We have two options as I see it," Nandita said. She was sitting straight in her chair in the dark office, which Sameer found both admirable and slightly unsettling. "We can pivot to the SME market with a stripped-down version of the current product—I've done the unit economics and it's workable if we cut the team to five and remove the enterprise features. Or we can wind down with enough runway left to pay proper severance."

"There's a third option," Ashwin said.

Here it was. Sameer had been waiting for this.

"The D2C pivot," Nandita said, without inflection.

"It's not a random idea—I've done research." Ashwin was leaning forward slightly, the co-working space's neon lighting giving him an unfortunate greenish cast. "There are three comparable companies in the US market that made this exact transition from B2B SaaS to D2C subscription. All three grew faster post-pivot than they did on the original model."

"Those companies had different cost structures," Nandita said.

"Partially different."

"And different runway positions. They all had minimum six months when they pivoted."

"We could cut fast and get there—"

"Ashwin." Sameer put his glass of water down. "We've had this conversation four times. I need us to have the other conversation tonight."

Chapter 2: The Real Question

The other conversation was the one about what they actually believed.

Not the strategy—they had been talking about strategy for three months while the runway burned. The question of what each of them genuinely thought about the company's chances, which they had been carefully not asking each other because all three of them had learned, somewhere in the startup mythology they'd absorbed, that founders had to maintain belief. That the belief was partly the product. That if you admitted doubt in the wrong moment you created a self-fulfilling outcome.

Sameer had decided tonight was the wrong moment for that particular discipline.

"Do you actually believe the D2C pivot works?" he asked Ashwin. "Not 'do you have a thesis.' Do you believe it?"

Ashwin was quiet for five seconds. The co-working space had a slight hum from its HVAC system that was audible on the call.

"I think it has a better chance than the current model," Ashwin said. "Which is what I believe about the pivot."

"That's not the question I asked."

More silence.

"I don't know," Ashwin said finally. "I think there's a version that works. I'm not certain."

"Okay." Sameer turned to the camera, which meant turning to Nandita. "What do you believe?"

Nandita looked at the dark room around her for a moment. She had co-founded this company four years ago when they were three people sharing one table at a WeWork and it had felt—for two years, it had genuinely felt—like it was going to be something. She could still locate that feeling. It was just that the feeling and the evidence were no longer aligned.

"I believe we built a product that's technically impressive and commercially wrong," she said. "I believe we picked a market segment that was too slow for our runway. I believe an SME pivot could work if we had the energy for it, and I'm not sure any of us do."

"That's honest," Sameer said.

"Your turn," she said.

He picked up the water glass, put it down again. "I believe we're having this conversation two months too late. I think the right call was in September—either a hard pivot or a wind-down before we burned through the option value. I didn't push for that call because I didn't want to be the person who called time on something people had put four years into." He paused. "That was a mistake. My mistake, specifically, because I'm the one who saw the September investor signals and didn't share them immediately."

Nandita and Ashwin were both quiet.

"What are you recommending?" Nandita said.

"Wind-down," Sameer said. "Now, with enough runway to do it properly. Decent severance, good references, time for the team to find something before we're out of cash."

"I'm not ready to say that," Ashwin said.

Chapter 3: One Forty

By one-forty, they had been on the call for nearly two hours and were not in agreement.

Ashwin wanted the D2C pivot. Nandita wanted the SME option or a wind-down; she was agnostic between those two in a way that suggested she had already made her decision and was being diplomatic about it. Sameer had stated his position and was holding it—wind-down—with the composure of someone who knew that composure was the only useful thing he had left to offer.

"We need to vote," Nandita said. It was not a suggestion.

"Voting doesn't make one of us right," Ashwin said.

"No. But it ends the call."

Sameer said: "Ashwin. If Nandita and I both vote for wind-down—can you accept that decision?"

"I don't know," Ashwin said. And then, more quietly: "I just don't think we've actually tried everything."

Sameer understood this. He understood it the way you understand grief when someone is in the middle of it—with patience but without the ability to fix it. Ashwin had been the one who had believed the most consistently and for the longest. He had the founder conviction that was supposed to be the thing that powered through impossible odds, and maybe it had, for a while. The trouble with that kind of conviction was that it didn't come with an off switch.

"If we try the pivot and we have three bad months," Sameer said, "what does the team go through that they don't go through now?"

Ashwin looked at his hands.

"Three months of uncertainty on top of six months of uncertainty," Sameer said. "And then we wind down anyway, but with no severance runway because we'll have spent it."

"I know," Ashwin said.

"I know you know."

The call was quiet for a moment. In Mumbai, Sameer's upstairs neighbour was moving around—he could hear footsteps. In Bengaluru, somewhere outside the dark office, a car was going by on a night road.

Ashwin took a breath. "Okay. Wind-down."

It was not enthusiastic. It was not a change of mind—it was a change of vote, which was different and which all three of them understood. Ashwin still believed there was a version that could have worked. He was choosing to let go of it, which was its own kind of courage.

"Thank you," Nandita said.

They spent the next forty minutes on the immediate tasks. Who would call the team in the morning. Who would contact the investors. Who would handle the vendor contracts. They divided the list with the efficiency of people who had made hundreds of lists together and knew how the other two worked.

At two-twenty-three, Sameer said: "Same time tomorrow?" which was a joke they had made after every difficult call for four years, and it landed the way it always did.

Nandita smiled at her screen. Ashwin, for a moment, looked like himself again.

"Same time tomorrow," he said.

Sameer ended the call. In the kitchen, the water glass was still there. He picked it up and drank the rest of it standing at the counter, in the dark, with the specific tiredness of someone who has done a hard thing that needed doing.
