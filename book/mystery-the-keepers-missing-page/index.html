The Keeper's Missing Page

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

A page has been cut from a 1931 lighthouse logbook on the Cornish coast, and the family legend says the keeper let the light go dark the night a ship was lost. Wren Penhale, restoring the tower, has a pencil and a patient mind.

Chapter 1: Page Forty-One

Wren Penhale had been restoring the lighthouse at Pol Gwyn for four months before she opened the logbook, and when she did, her first feeling was a small cold dread.

The book was a heavy, leather-bound thing, one of fifty that had survived in a damp stone cupboard at the foot of the tower. It was the logbook for the winter of 1931, kept in her great-grandfather's tidy hand. Wind, tide, lamp trimmed, glass cleaned, supplies received. Hour by hour, night by night. And then, between the thirtieth of November and the second of December, a ragged edge where a page had been cut out with a blade.

Every family in Pol Gwyn knew the story. On the first of December 1931, in a gale, a small coaster called the Morwenna had struck the rocks below the lighthouse and been lost with four hands. The word in the village had always been that Tobias Penhale, the keeper, had let the lamp fail that night. He had resigned the next spring, and the family had never quite looked anyone in the eye since.

Wren held the cut edge to the window light. She had grown up believing her great-grandfather was careless. But a careless man, she thought, does not keep a logbook like this one.

"Somebody cut it out," she told her friend Jory, who ran the village chandlery and had come over with a flask of tea. "Why would a keeper cut out the proof of his own failure? To hide it, I suppose. But then why keep every other page?"

"Maybe he didn't," said Jory. "Maybe someone else did."

Chapter 2: The Pencil Ghost

It was Jory's remark that sent Wren to her father's old desk, looking for the soft pencils he used for drawing boats. She had remembered a trick from her school days, from a history teacher who had rubbed pencil over a blank sheet to bring up the writing of the page before.

"People press hard when they write," she said. "A good pen leaves a groove in the next page, even if the first is gone."

She laid the logbook flat on the table by the lamp. The page after the cut, the second of December, was blank at the top. She held a pencil nearly flat and rubbed gently across the paper, in long, even strokes, like a child shading a coin.

Slowly, out of the grey, white lines began to appear. First a date. Then a line, and another, a whole short paragraph in pale ghostly letters against the shadow, because the ink pressed from the missing page had dented the sheet below.

Jory leaned in. "Can you read it?"

"Some." Wren squinted. "Thirtieth November. Eleven at night. Inspector Mr. Alderidge from the district office arrived with orders. Lamp mechanism to be shut for inspection and repair. I objected in strong terms, given the gale warning. Ordered to obey." She swallowed. "He wrote that, Jory. He objected. And then, here, the next line: Light extinguished at one o'clock by order, not by my wish. I have remained on watch with the signal lantern in the gallery."

"The signal lantern," said Jory slowly. "He was trying to warn them anyway."

"Look at the last bit. Rockets fired at ten minutes past two. Morwenna did not answer. Vessel struck at twenty past." Wren's voice broke. "He fired a rocket, Jory. He did everything he could."

Chapter 3: Who Cut It

They sat in silence a while, with the sea breathing below them and the great lens hanging dark and dusty above.

"Then who cut it out?" asked Jory at last.

Wren turned back to the earlier pages, searching. And there on the entry of the twenty-eighth of November she found what she had overlooked: a pencilled note in the margin, in a different hand, sharp and angular. Visit to be recorded on the official log. A. A.

"Alderidge," she said. "The inspector. He ordered the light shut, and when the ship was lost, he knew the page would ruin him. So he took it."

"He took the proof, and your great-grandfather took the blame."

"He never defended himself." Wren ran her thumb along the cut edge. "Maybe he thought nobody would believe a keeper over an inspector. Maybe he was too sad."

By evening she had photographed the pencil rubbing, signed and dated it, and carried the logbook down to the village hall, where the old men of Pol Gwyn gathered on Thursdays to argue about the weather. She laid the pencilled page on the green baize and read it aloud, in her clear, shaking voice, and nobody interrupted her.

When she had finished, old Mr. Trevennen, ninety years old and half deaf, stood up from his chair. "My father fished with Tobias Penhale," he said. "He always said the man was straight as a plumb line. Well. Now we have it written."

The light at Pol Gwyn was lit again that winter for the first time in sixty years, as a memorial, and every night it was Wren who climbed the stairs to trim the lamp.
