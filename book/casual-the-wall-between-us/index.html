The Wall Between Us

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

Two neighbours in a Pune apartment block communicate for a year only through the shared wall, never meeting. When the wall goes silent one day, each realises how much the other mattered.

Chapter 1: Thump, Thump, Silence

Ishaan Kulkarni moved into flat 304 in Kothrud in March, and by April he knew that the woman in 303 woke at five-thirty, practised the tabla until six-fifteen, and sang off-key in the shower. He knew this because the wall between their bedrooms was thin enough to hear a spoon drop.

He was a night-shift software tester who slept from nine in the morning, and the tabla was killing him.

He did not complain. He was shy, and complaining meant knocking on a door, and knocking on a door meant a face. So he knocked on the wall instead, three soft thumps at six o'clock, hoping the message was clear.

The next morning, the tabla stopped at five-fifty-nine. Then, from the other side, came three soft thumps in reply.

Ishaan lay in bed grinning at the ceiling. It was the most polite argument he had ever won.

Over the summer the wall became their language. Two thumps meant good morning. Four meant the monsoon was coming, because she always heard the first thunder before he did. He once pushed a slip of paper under the door with "Thank you for the quiet," and found a card in his letterbox the next day showing a cartoon tabla wearing earmuffs.

He never saw her. He only knew the sound of her slippers in the corridor and the smell of cardamom tea.

Chapter 2: The Wall Goes Quiet

In November the thumps stopped.

Ishaan noticed on a Tuesday. He woke at nine, expecting nothing, but somehow expecting something, and the flat felt hollow. By Thursday there had been no tabla for three days, no off-key singing, no cardamom. The newspapers piled up outside 303 like a small paper hill.

He asked the watchman, Dattu kaka, who was cleaning the gate with a wet cloth.

"303? Madam Joshi? She went to hospital, beta. Fell on the stairs last Sunday. Hip is broken."

"Which hospital?"

"Ruby Hall. But what are you to her?"

Ishaan opened his mouth and found he had no word for it. Friend was too much. Neighbour was too little. "I live next door," he said at last, which was true and not nearly enough.

He stood in the compound for a long minute, then went upstairs, boiled some water, and made himself a cup of tea. It tasted of nothing. He sat on his bed and knocked on the wall, twice, the way he always did.

Nothing came back. He had not known that silence could have a shape.

The next morning, before his shift, he went to a sweet shop and bought a small box of soft coconut barfi, then took an auto to the hospital with the box on his knees like a nervous passenger.

Chapter 3: Face to Face at Last

Room 212 was a double room, and the woman in the nearer bed was reading a magazine upside down because she was clearly asleep. She was smaller than he had imagined, silver-haired, with a hand that still twitched in rhythm even while she dozed.

Ishaan stood in the doorway, holding his box. A nurse passed and gave him a suspicious look. He tiptoed in, put the box on the bedside table, and tapped, very gently, on the metal rail of the bed. Two taps. Good morning.

Her eyes opened. She looked at him, confused, and then at the box, and then at his hand still resting on the rail.

"Two?" she whispered.

"Good morning," he said, and felt foolish.

"You are the thumper," said Madam Joshi, and her whole face broke into a smile. "Thirty-four years I have lived in that building. No one has ever thumped back."

"I thought you would be angry about the thump."

"Angry? I stopped practising at six because of you. It was the best thing anyone has asked of me in years. I did not know you had a face." She looked at him carefully. "It is a kind one. Sit."

He sat. For an hour they talked more than they had in eight months of walls. She told him about the tabla, and he told her about his job, which was finding mistakes in other people's code, and she said that was exactly what a good neighbour does.

When she came home three weeks later, he carried her bag up. At five-thirty the next morning the tabla began again, and Ishaan woke happily to it. At six sharp he did not knock. Instead he went next door, in slippers, and knocked on the door.
