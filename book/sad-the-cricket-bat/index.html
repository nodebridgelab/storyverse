The Cricket Bat

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

A father packing up the family home finds his son's childhood cricket bat and cannot put it in any box.

Chapter 1: What Stays

The movers were coming on Saturday, so Suresh had given himself Thursday and Friday to sort the upstairs rooms. He had told his wife Kamala he could do it alone. She had given him a long look — the kind of look that says I know what you are really saying — and gone to stay with her sister in Nashik, which was its own kind of kindness.

The house in Nagpur had been theirs for twenty-six years. Their son Arjun had been five when they moved in, and now Arjun was thirty-one and lived in Melbourne and was not coming back, not to stay. He had offered to fly home and help with the packing. Suresh had said: no need, it's just boxes. He had meant it when he said it.

He started with the easy rooms — the dining room, the guest bedroom, things that had no particular history attached to them. He developed a system: keep, sell, give away. He was efficient. He finished the ground floor by Thursday evening and felt, briefly, that he had overestimated how hard this would be.

Friday morning he went upstairs to Arjun's old room.

The room had been a guest room for years — after Arjun left for college in Pune, then Mumbai, then his job in Singapore, then the move to Melbourne. Kamala had put a floral bedspread on the single bed and a vase of artificial flowers on the desk. But under the desk, pushed against the wall, was a cardboard box Suresh had sealed with tape years ago and labeled in his own handwriting: A's things — old.

He should have opened it years ago and sorted it properly. He had always meant to.

He cut the tape now and folded back the flaps.

On top: a geometry compass set with Arjun's name written in marker on the case. A certificate from a state-level quiz competition, 2002. A small clay Ganesha Arjun had made in class four that was slightly lopsided and had been displayed proudly on his windowsill for three years. And underneath all of it, wrapped in an old bedsheet: the cricket bat.

Suresh sat down on the edge of the bed and held it in both hands and said nothing.

Chapter 2: The Boy Who Played

Arjun had never been a great cricketer. He was enthusiastic, which at seven is more than enough and at twelve starts to show its limits. He would practice in the lane behind the house until dark, Suresh bowling to him on weekends, and he believed sincerely until the age of about fourteen that he would play for India one day. This was not unusual. Half the boys in Nagpur believed the same thing.

What was unusual was Arjun's relationship with the bat itself. He had bought it with his own saved pocket money — three months of Eid and birthday money and small amounts scrounged from cooperative relatives — and he had oiled it and knocked it in himself following instructions from a magazine, with a seriousness that Suresh had found both amusing and unexpectedly moving. He had written his name on the handle in permanent marker. He had kept it standing upright in the corner of his room.

The bat had a dent near the toe from a ball that had hit a crack in the lane and skipped low. Suresh remembered the exact afternoon that had happened because Arjun had come inside furious and then, upon reflection, decided it made the bat look experienced. He had shown Suresh the dent with something close to pride.

The dent was still there.

Suresh ran his thumb across it. The rubber grip had dried out and was slightly cracked at the top. The willow had gone darker with time and linseed oil.

He thought about calling Arjun. It was a Friday morning in Nagpur, which meant it was some ungodly hour in Melbourne, and Arjun would be asleep, and what would Suresh say anyway? I found your bat. I am sitting on your old bed holding your bat. Your mother is in Nashik. We are selling the house.

He knew what Arjun would say. He would say: Dad, don't keep it for my sake. He would say: I have no room for it here. He would say these things kindly, practically, because Arjun was a practical person who had built a practical life on the other side of the world, and Suresh was proud of him. He was genuinely, honestly proud.

He set the bat beside him on the bed and looked around the room: the floral bedspread, the artificial flowers, the clean emptiness of a space that had become something else. He sat there for a long time without sorting anything at all.

Chapter 3: Keep

By Friday afternoon Suresh had packed everything from Arjun's room into boxes except the cricket bat.

He tried it in the keep box. He tried it in the sell box. He stood it in the corner of the empty room the way Arjun used to keep it and looked at it from the doorway. He carried it downstairs and leaned it against the kitchen wall while he made himself a cup of tea.

He called Kamala.

She answered on the second ring. He could hear her sister's television in the background and then the sound of a door closing as Kamala went somewhere quieter.

How is it going? she asked.

Fine, he said. I found the bat.

A brief silence. Oh, she said. Then: How are you?

He thought about this. Fine, he said again, which was not exactly true but was not exactly a lie either. He was something more specific than fine and less specific than broken. He was simply standing in a half-empty kitchen on a Friday afternoon, holding a cricket bat that his son had bought with his own pocket money twenty-three years ago and would never use again.

Bring it, Kamala said. To the new flat. We'll find somewhere for it.

It won't fit anywhere, he said. It'll be in the way.

Then it'll be in the way, she said.

He looked at the bat against the kitchen wall. The dent near the toe. The dried-out grip. Arjun's name on the handle in permanent marker, slightly faded now but still legible, still exactly Arjun's twelve-year-old handwriting, all the letters slightly too large and earnest.

He thought about when they moved into this house twenty-six years ago, Arjun running ahead of the movers and claiming his room, standing the bat in the corner before his bed was even assembled. The pure uncomplicated certainty of a five-year-old who knows exactly where everything belongs.

Suresh wrapped the bat carefully in the bedsheet it had come from, and he placed it across the top of the keep box, and he sealed the box with tape, and he wrote on the outside in his own handwriting: A's bat.

The movers came on Saturday. The keep box went into the truck with everything else. In the new flat in Pune, smaller and quieter, Suresh stood the bat in the corner of the spare bedroom, where it would be in the way, and where it would stay.
