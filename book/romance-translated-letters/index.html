Translated Letters

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

A Punjabi man and a Tamil woman write to each other across languages, and what is lost in translation becomes everything.

Chapter 1: First Letter

It started as a pen-pal program run by a cultural exchange organization that Harpreet had signed up for because his colleague dared him to, which was an embarrassing origin story that he chose not to share. He was thirty-three, a school principal in Patiala, and he had been studying Tamil for six months — slowly, with a textbook and an app that gave him gold stars for small victories — because he had been to Chennai for a conference the previous year and found himself moved by a language he understood not at all but felt, in some way he couldn't explain, in his chest.

The program matched him with Latha, a librarian in Madurai. Her English was precise and formal in the first letter, the way Tamil often translates — structured, considered. She said she was thirty-one, that she had grown up in Tirunelveli and moved north for work, that she had tried to learn Hindi twice and found it elusive. She wrote that she liked correspondence because it gave her time to say what she meant.

He wrote back in English for the first letter. He also, at the bottom, included two lines in Tamil that he had composed with the help of his textbook and a great deal of revision. The lines said: The mango trees here are flowering. I think you would recognize this. He was not sure this made sense. He sent it anyway.

Her second letter arrived three weeks later. She wrote in English. At the bottom, in Punjabi, she had written something — he stared at it for a full minute before realizing she must have worked with a transliteration guide. Translated, it said: The mango trees here are also flowering. Everything recognizes something.

Harpreet sat with this for a long time.

He wrote back the same week. He included five sentences in Tamil. He had spent two evenings on those five sentences. He did not want her to see the effort — and then changed his mind, and in one of the English paragraphs wrote: I spent two evenings on those five sentences. I wanted you to know.

Her reply came with ten sentences in Punjabi. And a note that said: I spent three evenings. Yours are better.

Chapter 2: Six Months

By spring, the letters had a rhythm. She wrote every two weeks, sometimes three; he matched her pace without planning to. The English portions grew shorter. Not because either of them was becoming less articulate, but because the other language was becoming more possible.

His Tamil was improving in a way his textbook hadn't anticipated — it was her Tamil, specifically, the vocabulary she used, the rhythm of her sentences. She wrote the way she spoke, he suspected: complete sentences, nothing wasted, a quiet precision that he found he heard in his head in a voice he had invented for her, slightly low, slightly careful.

Her Punjabi was extraordinary. She had a gift for phonetic patterns, she wrote, which she attributed to growing up learning Sanskrit prayers by sound alone. She made mistakes that were interesting mistakes — she occasionally used a word with slightly too much formality, which made her sentences sound like a very gracious letter from a previous century. He told her this. She wrote back that in that case, he sounded like someone who had just learned to ride a bicycle and was enormously proud of it.

He kept this letter in his desk drawer.

In April he started including small things alongside the letters — a clipping from a newspaper in Punjabi, circled, about something he thought she would find funny. A recipe card for langar dal that his mother had written out, which he translated word by word at the bottom, including the instruction to cook slowly, without rushing, because the dal knows if you are in a hurry. She sent back a clipping from a Tamil magazine and a description of a temple in Madurai written entirely in Punjabi, which was technically imperfect and completely beautiful.

He had stopped telling people about the pen-pal program. Not because he was embarrassed. Because the thing had become, in some way, private. When his colleague asked about it, he said, 'I'm learning Tamil.' That was the true part. The other part — Latha, her letters, the voice he'd invented for her, the fact that he had read her last letter four times — he kept that. He wasn't sure why. He thought he knew.

Chapter 3: What Cannot Be Translated

In June, she wrote something that took him three readings to fully understand. In Tamil — his Tamil was good enough now, mostly — she wrote: There are words in Tamil with no Punjabi equivalent. I have been thinking about this. The word anbu means something different from love. It is love and it is tenderness and it is the feeling of wanting to protect a thing from being diminished. I don't know what Punjabi word comes closest. Maybe you know.

He sat with this for a long time. He looked up various Punjabi words. He wrote several drafts.

In the end he wrote back in Punjabi — all in Punjabi, which he had never done — and he wrote: I think the closest word is pyaar but that isn't quite right either. Pyaar is the word everyone uses and it is true but it is also general. There is a phrase my grandmother uses — dil da tukda — which means a piece of my heart. People use it for children, for beloved things, for anything they cannot imagine losing. I don't know if that is equivalent. I think anbu might be wider.

Then, in Tamil — his most careful Tamil, revised twice — he wrote: I have been saving your letters. I don't usually keep things. I am telling you this because I think you would understand what I mean by it.

Her reply came after two weeks, which was slightly longer than usual. It came in Punjabi, all of it, no English at all, which had not happened before. She wrote: I have been reading your letters to the librarian in the next room, who does not read Punjabi. I read them aloud and translate as I go. She says the translation is very good. She says the feeling needs no translation.

At the very end, in Tamil, two words: Naan purindhen. I understand.

Harpreet sat in his office in Patiala with the letter in his hands. Outside, the summer was enormous and bright. He thought about the distance between their cities — Patiala and Madurai, which is not a short distance. He thought about how long it took a letter to travel it, and how long it took a person to decide something.

He opened his laptop. He looked up trains to Madurai.

Then he wrote one more letter. In Tamil, mostly, with one line in Punjabi at the end. The Punjabi line said: I think I should come to see what the mango trees look like in Madurai. If that would be all right.

He mailed it. He waited. Her reply came in ten days — faster than usual — and it was one line, in Punjabi, that she had clearly worked on very carefully because it was quite good: The trees are very good here. You should come.
