Retirement Speech

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

A schoolteacher gives her last assembly address to children too young to understand what they are witnessing.

Chapter 1: 35 Years

Lata Deshpande had been teaching at Vidyasagar Primary School for thirty-five years.

She had come at twenty-three, fresh from her B.Ed., intending to stay for two or three years while she figured out what she actually wanted to do. What she actually wanted to do turned out to be this. She had never quite got around to leaving.

She had taught four thousand three hundred and twelve children, by the rough count she had made in a notebook over twenty years of keeping attendance registers, and she had stopped counting because the number had become too large to feel real. She knew some of those children now as adults — she sometimes saw them in the market or at the doctor's clinic, grown people with their own children sometimes, who said: Madam! And her heart did something complicated.

The retirement party was scheduled for Friday evening, in the school hall, with the staff and the older students who had been with her longest. But the Principal — Ashok Sharma, who had himself been her student in Class Three before his career had brought him back to the school in an unexpected reversal that she still found mildly amusing — had asked if she would say something at Friday morning assembly. All the children present. The full school.

She had said yes.

She had regretted this on Thursday evening, sitting at her desk at home trying to write something. What do you say to five hundred children between the ages of five and eleven, who largely do not know who you are, who are present because they are always present at Friday assembly, who will be thinking about their breakfasts and their football and whatever argument they were having with their friends on the way to school?

She wrote: Children, I have been at this school for thirty-five years.

She crossed it out.

She tried again, and crossed that out too.

In the end she put the notebook away and decided to simply say what was true.

Chapter 2: The Assembly

The children stood in rows on the assembly ground in the morning light, youngest in front, oldest at the back, in the familiar grid that had been assembled every morning for thirty-five years under various combinations of weather and mood and term-end nervousness.

Lata stood at the microphone after the prayer and the pledge and looked at them.

She saw, in the front row, a small girl in a yellow hair clip who was examining the hair clip of the girl beside her with intense concentration. She saw, in the middle section, two boys of about nine who were having a whispered argument and thought they were being subtle. She saw, in the back row, a cluster of Class Five students who were old enough to look slightly bored, which was correct and appropriate, they were ten and eleven and looking slightly bored at things was basically their job.

She saw that none of them knew what this was.

And she saw, standing at the back near the staff, her colleagues — Rekha who had been here twenty-two years, young Deepak who had arrived four years ago and was still surprised by things that had long since stopped surprising her, the peon Ramesh who had worked at the school for longer than she had and who she suspected felt things more deeply about today than he would show. She saw Ashok, who was trying to look professionally composed and was not entirely succeeding.

Her throat tightened, briefly. She let it.

Children, she said. I have been at this school for thirty-five years. Most of you were not born when I started teaching here. Some of your parents were students here while I was teaching.

A ripple of mild interest in the middle rows. This was the kind of fact that penetrated.

Today is my last day, she said. After today I am going home to do many of the things I have put off for thirty-five years. I am going to read more books and grow tomatoes and visit my sister in Mysore properly instead of just for four days.

A small laugh from various places.

She paused.

What I want to tell you, she said, is this. You are very lucky to be in school. I know that is not what it feels like when you have a maths test. But you are very lucky. Every day someone here is trying to help you become someone larger than you already are. Please let them.

Chapter 3: After

The assembly dispersed in the usual way — the teachers calling their classes into lines, the sound of two hundred pairs of feet on concrete, the ambient noise of children returning to the business of being children.

The small girl in the yellow hair clip went back to Class One. The arguing boys were separated by their class teacher, who had been watching them through the whole assembly. The slightly bored Class Five students walked in a loose cluster back toward their room.

Lata stood at the microphone for a moment after they had gone.

Rekha came and stood beside her. Neither of them said anything.

The assembly ground was empty now except for Ramesh, who had begun the routine sweeping with the wide broom he used every morning. He did not look up. He was not a man who made a production of things, and this was, she thought, one of the things she would miss.

She had thirty-five years in this ground. In the particular patch of it where Class Two always stood she had a memory of a girl named Amrita who had cried every morning for the first two weeks of the school year and then, one day in September, simply stopped. In the back row space she had a memory of a boy who had asked her once, with complete sincerity, why the sky was blue, and when she had explained — light and scattering, she had done her best — he had considered this and said: so blue is the colour of nothing getting through? She had never forgotten that. She had never been able to think about the colour blue in quite the same way since.

Four thousand three hundred and twelve children. And all of them, even the ones she barely remembered, even the ones she had only taught for one term, had given her something she could not name precisely. Something to do with being trusted with someone else's unfinished self.

She picked up her folder from the podium. She walked back across the assembly ground toward her classroom, where her students were waiting, where she had one last lesson to teach, where she had always known, she realised now, that this was the thing she had wanted to do.
