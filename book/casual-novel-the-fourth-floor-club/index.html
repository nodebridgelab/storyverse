The Fourth Floor Club

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 11 chapters · Completed

Five Delhi neighbours who've only ever nodded end up sharing a kitchen at midnight during a water crisis — and keep meeting long after it's fixed.

Chapter 1: The Night There Was No Water

The building was called Green View Apartments, which was aspirational in the way of all building names in South Delhi — there was no particular green view, only the grey-brown texture of the city at various distances. The building had six floors, thirty flats, and a water crisis that had been building for two weeks before it became undeniable on a Thursday night in March.

The overhead tank ran dry at nine PM. The motor that was supposed to refill it had, it emerged, not been working properly for two weeks. The residents' association had a message in the complex WhatsApp group from maintenance saying repairs were scheduled for Friday morning, which was eight hours away, and to please collect water from the ground floor taps which were on a separate line.

Floor six came down with pots and bottles and the resigned energy of people who have been through building crises before. Floor five sent their children down, which was efficient. Floor four had a committee meeting.

It was not a formal committee. It was four people who had all arrived in the stairwell at the same time, each carrying vessels of various sizes, and had looked at each other with the recognition of people who shared a floor and a problem. The fifth member of what would later become the Fourth Floor Club arrived thirty seconds after: Nandini from 4E, who had been on a call with a client in San Francisco and had only just noticed the situation.

"Is it the whole building?" she said, slightly out of breath, carrying a large steel pot.

"Yes," said the tall man in the kurta — his name, they would learn, was Ishan, 4A — because he was the one who had checked the group first.

"The ground floor line still works?" This was Sulekha, 4B, who was sixty-something with the decisive manner of a woman who had managed large things and did not stop managing them when she retired.

"Yes."

"Then we go down and fill and come back up and that's four floors of stairs twice. Fine. Let's not all go separately like some kind of circus." Sulekha had already assessed the situation and determined a more efficient approach. "Who has the biggest vessel?"

The man in the kitchen across the landing — nobody knew him yet, quiet type from 4C — appeared in his doorway holding a very large aluminium bucket. "This?"

"Yes. That. What's your name?"

"Vinod."

"Good. Vinod takes the bucket. You two help him carry it up. You" — she pointed at Nandini — "come with me, we'll take the smaller ones. We do one trip, smart, together. Yes?"

They had not agreed to this. They agreed to it anyway. Sulekha was the kind of person whose plans you step into like stepping into a current — easier to let it take you where it's going.

On the way back up the stairs, water sloshing, slightly breathless by the second floor, something happened that the five of them would later all mention as the actual beginning of everything: someone started laughing, and they all joined in, which was the laughter of people who are tired and slightly absurd and in the same situation, which is one of the better kinds of laughter.

Chapter 2: What They Found Out About Each Other

Sulekha had lived in 4B for eleven years. She had been a principal of a government school in Lajpat Nagar, had retired four years ago, and had since been extremely productive in ways that did not fit any obvious category. She grew herbs on her balcony. She read two newspapers and annotated them in the margins. She had taken up baking at sixty-four because, as she explained without a trace of whimsy, she had thirty years of school canteen grievances and wanted to know what a good biscuit actually required.

Ishan, 4A, was thirty-three, worked in architecture, and had lived in the flat for two years — new enough that he still thought of the apartment as temporary, old enough that he had settled into it in the way you settle into temporary things and they become your life. He was from Pune originally and had moved to Delhi for work and found Delhi loud and interesting and somewhat harder to make friends in than he had expected.

Nandini, 4E, was the newest arrival — eight months. She was a UX researcher and kept the hours of her international clients, which meant she was frequently awake at strange times and frequently asleep in the afternoon. She had been the most invisible of them, a fact she was aware of and not entirely comfortable with. The flat felt like a hotel room some days. She had been telling herself this would change once she settled in. Eight months in, she was still telling herself this.

Vinod, 4C, had lived there the longest — fourteen years. He worked in accounts at a government ministry and lived alone since his son had left for the UK two years ago. He was fifty-eight and quiet in the way of people who have become quiet not by nature but by circumstance — years of cooking for one, watching one's preferences shrink to fit a single life. He had once been a very social person. He did not think about this often.

The fifth person on the fourth floor was Preethi, 4D, who arrived at the stairwell late because her eight-year-old daughter Meghna had been asleep and she'd had to wait for her to resettle after waking her briefly. Preethi was thirty-eight, had moved back to Delhi from Hyderabad after a divorce two years ago, was raising Meghna alone, and was in the particular state of someone who has reorganized their whole life and is now systematically filling in the gaps. She was exhausted in a daily way she had made her peace with. She was also, underneath the exhaustion, funny — the kind of funny that only comes out with people you trust, which meant most of the building had not seen it.

They met properly for the first time on the fourth floor stairwell, standing with their water vessels, at nine-forty-five on a Thursday night. Sulekha said her flat had the biggest kitchen and invited them for tea if anyone wanted, since they were all already awake and the maintenance would sort itself in the morning. This was the kind of invitation that people who live in close proximity spend their whole lives almost making and not making. Sulekha was not the kind of person who almost made things.

Chapter 3: Sulekha's Kitchen

The kitchen was larger than any of them had imagined from the outside of the building, and orderly in the way of a person who has organized space to serve a purpose rather than to impress. There was a small whiteboard on the refrigerator with a weekly meal plan written in neat capital letters and a handwritten list of every film Sulekha had watched in the past six months, rated on a simple scale of one to five.

"You rate films on a whiteboard?" Nandini said.

"I kept forgetting which ones I'd seen. And then I'd read a review and think, was that one good? So now I write them down." Sulekha was already filling the kettle. "Tea. Yes or no. No discussion about what kind, I'm making adrak wali."

Everyone said yes. Preethi asked for half sugar. Vinod asked for half and then said no, full, with the small rebellion of someone changing a habit for the first time in a while.

They sat around the kitchen table — four chairs and one dining chair pulled from the living room for Preethi — in the particular configuration of people who have not chosen each other but are not sorry to be there. Meghna was asleep in Preethi's flat across the hall, door unlocked, baby monitor on Preethi's phone.

Ishan asked Sulekha how the residents' association worked, because he had been meaning to understand it and never had. This started a conversation about the building's history — Sulekha had seen four property management companies, two pump replacements, one major structural repair involving the third-floor terrace, and a seven-month dispute about the parking allocation that she narrated with the compressed drama of someone who had been in the middle of it and has processed enough of it to find it entertaining.

Vinod laughed at the parking story in a way that meant he had his own version from the inside. Sulekha turned to him. "You were on the committee that year?"

"I wrote the letter," Vinod said.

"The third letter?"

"The second."

Sulekha looked at him. "You're the one who wrote that. I've been wondering who wrote that for five years."

Nandini asked what was special about the letter. "It was very polite," Sulekha said, "and also somehow made the management feel that not fixing the parking situation would be a decision they'd have to defend to history." She looked at Vinod with something like professional respect. "Very good bureaucratic writing. Where did you learn?"

"Ministry," Vinod said, which explained it entirely.

Preethi, who had been quiet in the way of someone who is listening from a slight distance, said: "Meghna is obsessed with the idea of a building that's alive. She thinks all the buildings have feelings and they remember everyone who's lived in them." She said this with the self-deprecating half-smile of a parent relaying a child's strangeness, but they could all tell she found it beautiful. "She'd love this. A kitchen story."

"Bring her next time," Sulekha said.

The next time was assumed by that sentence. Nobody corrected it. The tea was finished. It was past eleven. They washed their cups in the sink — Ishan and Vinod did it without being asked, which Sulekha noticed and approved of — and went to their separate flats.

Chapter 4: The Text Group

Nandini started the group the next morning. She called it "4th Floor" because she couldn't think of anything better and didn't want to overthink it. She added the four numbers she'd gotten the night before and typed: "Good morning. Did everyone sleep okay after all that?"

This was a small act — starting a WhatsApp group — that feels small until you consider that it requires a decision to treat something accidental as something you'd like to continue. Nandini had been in enough cities to know that you have to do something actively, at the right moment, or the moment dissolves. She had let other moments dissolve. She didn't let this one.

Sulekha replied at seven with: "Slept fine. The motor is working, by the way. They came at 6. Tank is refilling now."

Ishan sent a thumbs up.

Vinod sent nothing, which they would learn was normal — he read everything and responded in person.

Preethi sent: "Meghna says hello to the building's new friends."

This made Nandini smile at her phone at eight in the morning in a way she hadn't smiled at a phone in a while. She saved the group with a better name: "Fourth Floor."

The crisis was over. The motor was fixed. There was no practical reason to keep meeting. This was exactly the kind of juncture where accidental proximity groups dissolve — the crisis passes, the chai was nice, bye. The building's WhatsApp group went back to maintenance updates and noise complaints. Life resumed.

But Sulekha sent a message on Sunday morning: "Batch of biscuits today. The ragi ones that either worked or I've made a mistake. Anyone want to find out?"

Four people appeared at 4B within twenty minutes, at different intervals, with the slightly self-conscious air of people who have decided to show up and are glad they did. Meghna came with her mother and immediately began telling Sulekha about the building's feelings, specifically that Green View Apartments was a building that liked being full and went a little sad on Sunday afternoons when people were out.

"You're right," Sulekha said, with complete seriousness. "I've thought that too."

Meghna looked at her with the assessment of a child deciding whether an adult is trustworthy. "You feel things properly," she said finally.

"I try," Sulekha said. "Now taste this and tell me if the ragi works."

The biscuits were excellent. Vinod said so in the flat way of a man who means it. Ishan ate three before saying anything. Nandini had two and then said, "Can I have the recipe?" and then, because Sulekha looked at her, added, "I'll actually make them. I'm not just saying it."

"I know," Sulekha said, which was generous and also possibly true.

Chapter 5: Ishan's Wall

Ishan's flat was the most sparsely furnished of the four — the furniture of someone who had moved in with a specific minimum and not added to it, partly from busyness and partly from a vague feeling that the place wasn't quite his yet, that he was still deciding. There was a drawing desk near the window where he actually worked, surrounded by the controlled chaos of architectural drawings and small physical models of things he was currently designing. The rest of the flat had space in it that had never been filled.

He mentioned this once to the group — not as a problem but as a fact — and Sulekha appeared the next afternoon with an opinion about it. She walked the flat in a way that reminded him of how the older architects at his firm walked a site — observant, calibrating.

"The problem," she said, "is not that it's sparse. The problem is it's sparse without intention. Spare and sparse are different."

"I'm not a décor person."

"You design buildings for a living."

"Other people's buildings."

She looked at the drawing desk, which was the liveliest thing in the flat — covered in evidence of his thinking, the models and drawings and small material samples. "Everything you need is right there," she said. "You just haven't given it permission to be part of the flat."

He didn't know what to do with this at first. Then, on a weekend when he had no deadlines, he moved the drawing desk from its corner to the centre of the main room, where it occupied the space directly when you walked in. He put a light over it. He hung two of his own drawings on the wall — detailed elevation studies, the kind of work he'd never thought to put on a wall because it was work, but which were, he realized once they were up, genuinely beautiful.

The flat changed. Not dramatically — same furniture, same spare floors — but it changed the way a room changes when the thing that matters most in it is acknowledged.

The group came by the following Sunday and Nandini said, immediately, "What did you do in here?" and he said he'd moved the desk, and she said, "It's completely different," and Preethi said, "Now it looks like you live here," which was, he realized, exactly right. He had lived here for two years. He had just started doing it properly.

Vinod looked at the drawings on the wall for a long time. "What building is this?"

"Nothing yet. A competition entry. We didn't place."

"It's beautiful," Vinod said. "You should have placed."

This was the most certain thing Vinod had said in any conversation so far, delivered with a bureaucrat's confidence in his own assessment. Ishan looked at the drawing. He had liked it too. He had let the competition result settle over it like dust. Having someone brush the dust off was a small and disproportionately meaningful thing.

Chapter 6: Preethi and Meghna

Meghna took to the Fourth Floor the way children take to things — fully, without the qualifications adults put on belonging. She adopted Sulekha as a secondary grandmother, which Sulekha received with the matter-of-fact warmth of a woman who had spent thirty years being a figure of authority to children and knew what it meant to be chosen by one.

She adopted Vinod more cautiously. Vinod was initially uncertain about children — his own son was twenty-six and had always been a quiet, self-contained child; he did not have the toolkit for eight-year-olds with theories about buildings. Meghna helped him by requiring very little toolkit. She would sit in his flat and colour while he read, occasionally asking him questions about accounting that were not economically sophisticated but were philosophically interesting. "If you have a hundred rupees and you give fifty away, do you have more or less?" "Less," Vinod said. "No," Meghna said, "you have more of the important things." Vinod thought about this for a while and said, "You're right," and meant it.

Preethi watched all of this from the particular vantage of a single parent who is always watching — alert to how her daughter is received, to the generosity of people who make room for a child without being asked. In her experience in a new city, this generosity was not guaranteed. The Fourth Floor, with its accidental formation and its absence of an agenda, had produced it in every direction, and she was grateful in a quiet way that she didn't know how to say.

She said it, eventually, to Nandini on a Tuesday evening when they had ended up in Nandini's flat after the others had gone home. Nandini had been walking Preethi through some basic research methods for a project Preethi was doing — she was doing a part-time certification in UX because she had decided to change fields — and they had gone past the point where one person is teaching and into the territory where two people are just talking.

"I was worried about coming back," Preethi said. "Delhi feels like someone else's city when you leave it for long enough. And then you come back and you have to construct it for yourself again."

"I've had five cities in eight years," Nandini said. "Delhi is the one I've stayed longest in and I still sometimes feel like a tourist."

"How do you stop feeling like a tourist?"

Nandini thought about this. "You need one specific place where people know you. Not the whole city. Just one place."

Preethi looked around the flat. Nandini had put up things on her walls in the months since they'd started meeting — photographs, a print, a calendar from a museum she'd visited. The flat looked less like a hotel room.

"You've settled in," Preethi said.

Nandini looked surprised, then looked at her walls. "I have," she said. "Hadn't noticed."

Chapter 7: Vinod Calls His Son

Vinod called his son Rohan on the second Sunday in April, which was not an unusual event — he called every Sunday — but which felt different this week because he had things to say that weren't the usual things.

Rohan was in Manchester, doing a Master's in engineering, and their calls followed a pattern that had calcified over two years: Rohan would ask how Papa was, Vinod would say fine and ask about the weather in Manchester, Rohan would say cold, Vinod would say eat properly, Rohan would say I do, and then they would talk around the fact that they had run out of the easy things and neither of them had found the harder ones.

This Sunday, Vinod said: "I have neighbours now."

Rohan said, "You've always had neighbours."

"Not this kind. We eat together on Sundays. The woman in 4B makes biscuits. The man in 4A is an architect, he designed a building that's very good, I saw the drawing. There's a woman from Hyderabad with a daughter who asks me questions about money that I can't answer."

There was a pause on the Rohan end. "This is all happening in the building?"

"Fourth floor."

"How did it start?"

"Water crisis. Three weeks ago."

Another pause. Vinod could hear his son recalibrating something — the image he held of his father's life in Delhi, alone in the flat, watching news, eating alone, being fine in the way you say you're fine when you've accepted something. Vinod had known this image existed. He had not known how much he wanted to change it.

"You sound different, Papa."

"Do I?"

"Lighter."

Vinod thought about this. He thought about the kitchen table in Sulekha's flat, the Sunday morning light, Meghna's question about money and the important things. He thought about Ishan's drawings on the wall and how he had said they were beautiful with a certainty he hadn't felt about an opinion in a long time.

"Yes," he said. "I think I am."

"Tell me about them properly. The neighbours."

So Vinod did. He told Rohan about Sulekha's management efficiency and her whiteboard film ratings. About Ishan's desk in the middle of the room. About Nandini's international client calls at two in the morning and the way she had started the group as if it was a casual thing but had done it precisely because she knew it wouldn't happen otherwise. About Preethi's quiet gratitude and Meghna's theory about buildings having feelings.

Rohan listened without interrupting, which was unusual for him — he was a person who completed sentences, kindly but presumptuously. Letting his father talk uninterrupted was a different kind of listening, and Vinod noticed it.

"I want to come in June," Rohan said when Vinod finished. "For proper time, not just the long weekend. Can you ask if they mind — the neighbours — if I join the Sunday thing?"

"I'll ask," Vinod said. He already knew the answer.

Chapter 8: The Sunday System

By May the Sunday mornings had developed the shape of something intentional — though none of them had intended it, which was the thing they found most pleasing about it when they thought about it.

The structure was loose: Sulekha's kitchen, ten-thirty or eleven, tea to begin with and then whatever food the rotation produced. It had started with Sulekha's biscuits and generalized from there. Ishan brought dosas twice using a batter he'd asked his mother for and then worried about until the first batch came out right. Nandini brought fruit salad from the fruit market on Saturdays and said she had no cooking skills but she had good produce instincts. Preethi made one dish and made it the same way every time — her mother's poha recipe, which nobody could fault and which Meghna treated as a permanent fixture.

Vinod cooked lunch, one Sunday, and said nothing about it in advance. He made rajma chawal in the quantities of someone who has not cooked for a group in a long time and was not sure how much to make and erred significantly on the side of generosity. There was enough rajma chawal for eight people. They were five.

"We'll eat it tomorrow too," Sulekha said cheerfully, portioning out steel boxes for each of them to take home.

Vinod had not cooked rajma since his wife had last been in the kitchen, which was twelve years ago. He had made it from memory, which turned out to be precise — she had made it the same way every time, and the muscle memory of watching her had deposited itself somewhere in him. Eating it in Sulekha's kitchen with people who said it was the best rajma they'd had in Delhi, he felt something specific: the sense of giving something he hadn't known he still had.

Ishan brought his laptop once and showed them a building he was designing — early stages, still fluid, more questions than answers. He explained it in the way he explained things to clients, which was clear but slightly formal, and then Sulekha said "No, show us what you actually think about it," and he laughed and started again and showed them what he actually thought, which was richer and less resolved and more interesting.

Meghna, who had been quiet during the first explanation, said during the second one: "It looks like it wants to be by water."

Ishan looked at her. "It's going to be by a river."

"I know," Meghna said, as if she had simply read it from the drawing.

Nandini watched all of this with the researcher's habit of paying attention to dynamics. She was professionally interested in how people used spaces and made meaning in them. She had data from her actual research projects and she had data from these Sunday mornings, and she found the Sunday morning data richer — not more rigorous, but more real in the way that real things are harder to systematize.

She had started writing some of it down, not as research, just as notes. She didn't know what they were for. She kept writing them.

Chapter 9: The Argument

The argument, when it came, was about the residents' association and specifically about the new parking policy that the management committee had proposed, which would reallocate spots in a way that Sulekha considered unfair and that Ishan thought was actually reasonable given the building's current configuration.

It was not a large argument. It lasted twenty minutes and was conducted with the politeness of people who have learned to disagree with someone they like, which is a different and more demanding skill than disagreeing with someone you don't. Sulekha had strong opinions about the history of the building's parking disputes — she had lived through all of them — and Ishan had a professional interest in spatial allocation that made him read the proposal differently.

Nandini, Preethi, and Vinod stayed out of it. Meghna ate her poha with the focused calm of a child who has correctly assessed that this was a grown-up thing and was not her concern.

The argument ended the way most good arguments end: not with one person winning but with both people having said something the other hadn't considered. Sulekha acknowledged that the proposed layout was technically more efficient. Ishan acknowledged that efficiency divorced from history was its own kind of unfairness.

"You'd make a good advocate," Ishan told her.

"I was one, informally, for thirty years," Sulekha said. "Every principal is."

This was a Thursday evening, not a Sunday, and the fact of them being there on a Thursday was itself evidence of something — they had begun to move in and out of each other's spaces without the Sunday framework. An evening on Nandini's balcony. Ishan's flat for a match. Sulekha's kitchen for a tea that extended into dinner.

Preethi said to Nandini, the week after the argument, "Do you think it'll mess anything up? The argument?"

"No," Nandini said. "I think it'll make things better. Groups that can't argue stay at a surface level."

"Is that the researcher talking?"

"Partly. Partly just — I've had enough groups that stayed at the surface level. They dissolve. This one won't."

Preethi thought about the city she had returned to, the project of constructing a life in it. She thought about Meghna sitting in Sulekha's kitchen with total ownership of her place there. She thought about Vinod calling his son with something new to say. She thought about Nandini's walls covered in photographs now, the hotel-room feeling gone.

"How do you know?" she asked.

"Because we all showed up for things that weren't crises," Nandini said. "The water crisis started it but we stayed for the biscuits. That's different."

Chapter 10: What June Brought

Rohan arrived in June with two suitcases — one for clothes and one, it emerged, almost entirely containing things his mother had sent from the kitchen in Patna. Dried spices in small containers, hand-written recipes on paper she had laminated herself, a bottle of her homemade pickle that had been packed inside three layers of plastic bags as a precaution.

"She sent you with a kitchen," Vinod said at the door.

"She said if I'm going to meet your new friends I should take them proper food," Rohan said. He was taller than Vinod had remembered — or possibly Vinod had stopped updating the mental image and Rohan had simply kept growing into himself. He had his mother's directness and his father's careful attention. He stood in the fourth-floor corridor and looked at the building with the curiosity of someone trying to understand why a place has become important.

He met them all at Sunday lunch. Sulekha interrogated him about his research in the thorough but genuinely interested way she interrogated everyone, which Rohan handled well — he was used to being asked questions, it turned out, he liked it. Ishan talked to him about engineering and infrastructure, finding the overlap between what they both understood about how things were built. Nandini asked him questions about his experience of designing for a new city, which connected to her research in a way that turned into a forty-minute conversation that the others followed from a slight distance.

Meghna, upon meeting him, said: "You're the son who went away. Your papa talks about you on Tuesdays."

Rohan looked at Vinod. "Tuesdays?"

"They play chess on Tuesdays," Preethi said. "Meghna watches."

Rohan had not known about the chess. There was, it was clear, a whole week that existed that he had not known about — not because Vinod was hiding it but because they had not had the language for it before. Tuesday chess with Meghna supervising. Thursday evening teas on Nandini's balcony. Saturday market runs where Ishan and Sulekha had a running disagreement about whether organic produce was worth the price that never resolved and both of them found entertaining.

At the end of the Sunday lunch, when the food from his mother's kitchen had been eaten and praised and the spice containers had been opened and smelled with the specific appreciation of people who know what real spice smells like, Rohan helped Vinod wash the dishes. They stood side by side at Sulekha's sink, which was where they ended up most Sundays.

"You're happy, Papa," Rohan said. Not a question.

"Yes," Vinod said.

"Good." Rohan handed him a bowl to dry. "I was worried about you."

"I know. Don't worry."

"Can I visit more?"

"There's a key under the mat," Vinod said. "I'll cut another one."

Chapter 11: The Club

There was no moment when they named it. Meghna named it — she started referring to them as "the club" sometime in July and the name stuck with the adhesion of things named by children, which is to say completely and permanently.

"The Fourth Floor Club is having lunch," she would announce to her friends at school, with the authority of membership. "I can't come right now."

By August, the water crisis that had started it was a story they told rather than something any of them would have necessarily recovered as a specific memory — one of those small crises of building life that accumulate in the background and are only memorable if something comes of them. This one had come of something.

Sulekha finished her film ratings whiteboard and started a new one. The rating scale had been modified by committee, specifically by Meghna, who objected to stars on philosophical grounds and suggested buildings instead: one building for fine, two for good, three for very good, four for excellent, five being reserved for films that made the building feel things. Sulekha had adopted this without argument and was using it rigorously.

Ishan submitted the river building to a second competition and placed. He put the notice on the group with one line — "we placed" — and came home to find a small celebration in Sulekha's kitchen that he had not been told about and had been, as far as he could tell, assembled in under an hour. Preethi had made her poha. Vinod had brought sweets. Nandini had found a bottle of something celebratory from her fridge.

"How did you know I'd be home at this time?" he asked.

"I didn't," Sulekha said. "We were already here."

Nandini had been writing her notes about the Fourth Floor Club for months. She came to the decision about them on a Thursday evening in September, sitting on her balcony with the last of the monsoon air and a cup of Sulekha's ragi tea. The notes were a paper. She would write it properly and submit it to a journal she respected. Not an exposé — not with names or flat numbers. A paper about a type of community formation that she had read about theoretically and was now watching happen in real time from the inside.

The question at the centre of the paper was this: what makes accidental communities persist when they could easily dissolve? She had several answers now — the right combination of people, the specific generosity of Sulekha's first invitation, Meghna's complete inclusion that required reciprocity, Nandini's own decision to start the group before the moment passed. But the answer she returned to most was simpler: they had all been separately lonely in a way that did not require crisis to reveal, only the right small opening. The water crisis was not the cause. It was the occasion.

The Fourth Floor of Green View Apartments was not a building with a green view. It had four flats, five adults, one child, one visiting son from Manchester, and a Sunday morning that had become, without anyone planning it, the fixed point of the week. The building remembered everyone in it, as Meghna said it did. And they had become, in the only way that matters, its people.

On the last Sunday of September Meghna brought a drawing she had made of the building — crayon, bright colours, figures in each window of the fourth floor. She gave it to Sulekha, who pinned it above the whiteboard film ratings in her kitchen, where it remained for a very long time.

Under the drawing she wrote, in her school-principal capital letters: Fourth Floor Club. Est. March.
