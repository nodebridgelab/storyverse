The Carpool

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

Two colleagues share a carpool for eleven months and share everything — except the one thing that matters.

Chapter 1: Monday to Friday

The arrangement had started in January, when Priya's scooter broke down irreparably and Aditya mentioned, entirely too casually, that he drove past Andheri East on his way to the office anyway. It was a practical solution. That was all it was.

By March, they had a system. Aditya would text her when he turned onto SV Road — never too early, never too late. Priya would be waiting at the gate of her building, coffee in hand, and she would bring one for him too because she had figured out, sometime around the third week, that he took it with no sugar and just a splash of milk. He hadn't mentioned this. She had simply noticed.

The commute was forty-five minutes on a good day, over an hour when the Jogeshwari flyover became the parking lot it secretly wanted to be. They spent this time in a way Priya found difficult to explain when her friend Deepa asked about it. They talked, yes — about the projects at work, about the news, about a podcast one of them had listened to. But it was the silences she couldn't explain, the way the quiet between them was never uncomfortable, how she would look out at the city waking up and feel, in some way she refused to examine, that this was the best part of her day.

Aditya was the kind of person who said little but meant everything he said. He had a habit of tapping his fingers on the steering wheel when he was thinking, a soft arhythmic pattern, and she had come to read it the way some people read weather. Three taps meant he was going to say something he'd been sitting with for a while. She never let on that she knew.

In April, they drove through rain so heavy the world outside the windshield turned to watercolour, and they sat parked outside the office for seven minutes after arriving because neither of them moved to open the door. She didn't know what stopped her. She suspected, quietly, that she did know.

Work kept them apart during the day. They were in adjacent departments — she in UX, he in product — which meant they overlapped at all-hands meetings and at the coffee machine and occasionally in the corridor. At work she was very careful to treat him the way she treated any colleague. She thought she was convincing. Her colleague Meera's constant, knowing smile suggested otherwise.

Chapter 2: What We Talk About

By June, Priya knew that Aditya's mother lived in Nagpur and called every Sunday evening. She knew he had wanted to study architecture but had drifted into product management and found, to his own surprise, that he loved it. She knew he kept a spare umbrella in the back seat always, that he disliked cilantro on principle, and that he had a tendency to reread certain books every year — he was on his third reading of The God of Small Things, which he refused to discuss because he said it made him feel too much.

He knew she grew up in Vadodara in a house with a mango tree in the backyard. He knew she made the best chai of anyone he had ever met, though he'd only tasted it the one time, when her building's lift broke and he'd helped carry groceries up four floors and she had offered him tea because it was the decent thing to do and they had sat on her balcony for an hour talking about nothing until he suddenly said he had somewhere to be, which she was fairly sure was not true.

In July she got a promotion. He texted at seven in the morning, before she had even told anyone else, because somehow he had heard. She stared at the message for a long time. It said: Well deserved. I always knew. Nothing else. She read it six times.

The days were getting to a particular configuration now. She would think of something during the day and save it to tell him on the way home. He started sending her articles — sent to her work email, carefully, because it was safer than her personal number, she supposed — about things they had discussed. Design philosophy. A particularly unusual architectural project in Jaipur. Once, a piece about the etymology of the word longing. She didn't know what to do with that one. She kept it open in a tab for two weeks.

In August they were stuck in traffic for an hour and forty minutes. They had exhausted the podcast they were listening to, and in the quiet that followed, Aditya said, 'What would you have done if you hadn't become a designer?' She said she didn't know — maybe a librarian, maybe nothing, she had always liked the idea of being surrounded by things that mattered. He didn't say anything. He just nodded, that slow nod he had, and tapped his fingers once on the wheel.

She started dreading weekends.

Chapter 3: November

November arrived cold and hazy, the city wrapped in that particular Mumbai chill that people from elsewhere laughed at but Priya had always found melancholy and beautiful. The jacaranda outside the office was shedding purple flowers onto the cars in the lot, and she thought she should mention it to him, that he would appreciate it, and then felt absurd for knowing that about him.

The office announced a year-end reshuffle. New project allocations, new team structures. Her name appeared on a project that would run through April. His appeared on a different one. The teams would be on separate floors. It was logistics. It didn't mean anything.

But that Friday, when he dropped her off, she reached for the door handle and something stopped her — the same thing that had stopped her in April in the rain. She turned. He was looking straight ahead, his hands still on the wheel, and then he wasn't — he was looking at her, and the expression on his face was one she had never let herself name but recognized immediately.

'It's the last weekday of November,' she said, which wasn't what she had meant to say at all.

'I know,' he said.

A pause. Outside, someone's dog barked. A tempo trundled past.

'I was thinking,' he said, and stopped. Tapped his fingers. One, two, three. 'I was thinking we could — there's a place in Bandra, the rooftop one, they do good filter coffee. I thought maybe — not the carpool, just us. Saturday.' He said it all very fast and then went quiet, and she could see from the way he held his jaw that he was bracing.

Priya had a policy about not being the kind of woman who let things happen to her instead of choosing them. She had maintained this policy imperfectly over the years. She looked at him — at the familiar line of his profile, the spare umbrella in the back, the two travel mugs in the cupholder that had somehow become a habit — and felt eleven months of careful, pleasant, daily restraint arrive at its obvious conclusion.

'I've been waiting for you to say that since April,' she said.

He turned to look at her fully then, and his face did something she had not seen before — something uncomplicated and bright. He laughed once, quiet. 'April,' he said. 'I thought June was more like it.'

'April,' she said firmly. 'The rain.'

He nodded. 'Fair enough.' He picked up his coffee cup, realized it was empty, set it down. 'Saturday, then.'

'Saturday,' she agreed. She opened the door and stepped out into the November evening and walked to her building without looking back, though she could feel, with complete certainty, that he watched until she was inside.
