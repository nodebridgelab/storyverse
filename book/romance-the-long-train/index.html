The Long Train

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

Delhi to Kochi, forty-eight hours, two reserved berths — and a conversation that refuses to be ordinary.

Chapter 1: Departure

The Kerala Express left Hazrat Nizamuddin at 11:35 PM, which meant that by the time Ananya found Berth 34 in S3 and stowed her bag and taken off her sandals and climbed to the upper berth with her book, the platform lights were already sliding away into darkness and the train had found its rhythm. She had done this route before. She liked it — the long haul, the dissolution of ordinary time, the particular pleasure of reading while the country moved past the window.

Berth 33 was occupied. She'd noticed this while arranging her bag — a man about her age, also settling in, the deliberate organization of someone who traveled this route enough to have a system. He had a small backpack that went in the space under the seat rather than the overhead rack, a steel bottle that he placed where he could reach it, a book of his own that he stacked with two others. She noticed the books without meaning to. Three novels. Long ones.

'Forty-eight hours,' he said, without looking up. 'Need enough to read.'

She had been looking at his books. She looked away. 'I know the route.'

He glanced up then. He had the look of someone who had been traveling a long time — not tired, exactly, but distilled. 'Kochi?' he said.

'Thrissur,' she said. 'I get off an hour earlier.'

'Kochi,' he said. 'Vishnu.' He offered his name the way you do on trains — briefly, correctly, then back to what you were doing.

'Ananya,' she said, already opening her book.

The lights went out at midnight. She read by the small overhead reading light, aware of it as a particular rudeness to other passengers but equally aware of herself as someone who could not simply lie in the dark. He read too — she could see the edge of his light — and they read across from each other in the moving train until somewhere past Mathura when she fell asleep with the book on her chest and woke up at four AM in Agra's stillness, the train paused, the light off, the book put aside with a bookmark that was not hers.

Chapter 2: Day One

In the morning the train was doing what long trains do — establishing its own culture, its own small society. The vendor came with chai. The family in the next compartment shared breakfast with a generosity that long-distance trains invite. Outside, Madhya Pradesh moved past in its flat morning manner — occasional towns, the occasional gleam of water, the sky going through its colours.

Ananya came down from the upper berth at seven and found Vishnu already awake and reading, chai in hand. He had bought two cups from the vendor, she realized. One was on the small table folded between the berths.

'I guessed,' he said. 'Tea or coffee?'

'Tea,' she said. She sat down and picked up the cup. It was exactly right — strong, a little sweet. 'You guessed right.'

'The book,' he said. He looked at her novel — Toni Morrison, which she'd had for months. 'People who read Morrison at midnight on trains drink tea.'

'That is a completely invented rule,' she said.

'It worked.'

They talked through Madhya Pradesh. Not about themselves, initially — about the book she was reading and the ones he had. He had two Kerala novels and a Manto collection, which told her something she found interesting. He was a documentary filmmaker, she learned — he was going to Kochi for a project, something about the old trading routes of the coast. She was a climate scientist at a Delhi university, going home to visit her parents.

The landscape changed gradually. The afternoon brought the Vindhyas, their particular drama, and they fell quiet in the way that people do when a landscape demands it. She was reading; he was looking out the window. She looked over and his expression was the expression of someone who found train travel genuinely pleasurable, not endured. She thought: this matters, somehow.

At dinner they shared the platform food from Nagpur — she bought vada, he bought some poha — without discussing it, just distributing things across the small folded table. The family next to them approved vocally. Ananya caught herself explaining something about monsoon patterns that he had asked about and realizing she had been talking for twelve minutes while the food went cold and he was listening — not politely, actually listening, asking follow-up questions that were the right follow-up questions.

'You find this genuinely interesting,' she said, pausing.

'I find you genuinely interesting,' he said, and then looked very slightly surprised by himself.

Chapter 3: Day Two

She thought about that sentence for a long time. Not anxiously. Just turned it over in the night while the train crossed into the south and the landscape shifted slowly, imperceptibly, the air through the window becoming something different — softer, damp with a different kind of green.

In the morning — their second morning, which felt both fast and long in the way train time creates — she came down from the berth and found him standing at the open door between coaches, looking out at the Western Ghats which had appeared in the night, ridge after ridge in the morning mist.

She stood beside him. The wind was cool and clean and smelled of something she recognized as home.

'I've done this route six times,' he said, 'and this part never loses it.'

'I know,' she said.

They stood there for a while in the door, not talking. The train curved around a hill and for a moment the whole length of it was visible — the long silver thread of it against the green — and she felt the particular pleasure of being inside something enormous and moving.

Thrissur was at three in the afternoon. She knew this, had known it all along. The knowledge had been there, running parallel to the conversation, to the two days of chai and shared food and long sentences, and now it was arriving.

She started to gather her things at two-thirty. He helped her bring down her bag from the overhead rack without being asked, which was the kind of thing she noticed. They stood in the corridor.

'There's a film festival in Thrissur next month,' he said. He said it carefully. 'Documentary section. I have a film in it.'

She looked at him. 'You do.'

'I wasn't sure if I would say that.'

'But you did.'

'You make it easy to say things,' he said. Simple, direct, the way he had talked for two days.

The train was slowing. Outside, the platform was coming into focus — familiar architecture, familiar sky. She had her bag strap over her shoulder. She had her book in her hand. She thought about forty-eight hours and everything that had accumulated in them.

'Send me the details of the festival,' she said. 'I'd like to see the film.' She handed him her card — her university card, with her email. He took it, looked at it, put it in his pocket with the deliberateness of someone who intended to keep it.

The train stopped. She stepped off onto the platform and turned. He was in the doorway, and the train was the train — the long train, Kerala Express, continuing to Kochi — and he raised a hand, and she raised one back, and then the platform moved and he was gone. She stood in the Thrissur afternoon and thought: next month, the festival. She was already looking forward to it.
