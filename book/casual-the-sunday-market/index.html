The Sunday Market

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

Three old friends find a hidden flea market, and what each of them buys reveals exactly who they've become.

Chapter 1: The Rumour

Priya had heard about the market from her auto driver, which was exactly the kind of thing that happened to Priya and to no one else in their friend group. She had the auto driver's number saved under "Shyambabu reliable," and Shyambabu had mentioned, somewhere between the signal and the flyover, that there was a Sunday market behind the old textile mill on the Banjara Hills road that had antique furniture and old books and handmade things and you should go early because by ten everything good was gone.

She told Divya and Sunita over their regular Friday chat, and Divya said immediately yes and Sunita said let me check and then said yes. This was their dynamic so reliably that no one noticed it anymore. Priya found things, Divya committed instantly, Sunita considered and then joined.

They had been friends since first-year engineering at a college in Hyderabad, which was twenty years ago, a fact that all three of them occasionally stopped to verify against some internal sense of time and found mildly impossible. They now lived within four kilometres of each other, which was either a coincidence or, as Divya maintained with complete seriousness, the result of a group energy that kept them in proximity whether they planned it or not. Sunita said this was nonsense. Priya said it was both.

They met at eight-thirty on Sunday at the chai shop nearest to where Shyambabu had said the road turned. The chai shop had no name, just a blue tarp and a stove and a man who made the chai with the relaxed authority of someone who had been making chai before any of them were old enough to want it. They drank standing up, watching a Sunday morning come into itself — vegetable vendors, a dog with a considered walk, a Swiggy delivery boy eating a paratha with the focused peace of a man who had a moment.

"What kind of things does it have?" Sunita asked. She was the one who researched things before going to them. Not having researched this was mildly uncomfortable for her.

"Antique furniture and old books and handmade things," Priya repeated faithfully. "That's all he said."

"He said old books?" Divya looked up from her chai.

"He did."

This changed the tenor of the walk slightly. Divya walked a little faster.

They found it down a narrow lane that smelled of wet stone and something sweet — sugarcane being pressed somewhere nearby, as it turned out, by a man with an ancient press that looked like it had been operating since the mill was running. The market was not marked by any sign. There were perhaps thirty stalls set up in a rough courtyard behind a crumbling compound wall, tarps stretched between poles, the kind of informal order that looks like chaos from a distance and reveals its own logic when you're in it.

It was half-past eight and already warm. They went in.

Chapter 2: What Each of Them Wanted

Divya went directly to the book stalls the way water finds a drain. There were four of them at the back wall, paperbacks and hardcovers piled on trestle tables and plastic sheets on the ground, organized by some system that only the sellers understood. She crouched down and started going through a pile on the floor with the particular reverence she reserved for used books, reading first lines, turning pages to check for underlining and margin notes because margin notes were her favourite thing about old books — the trace of someone else's reading life.

She found, within twelve minutes, three things: a water-stained collection of short stories by a Kannada writer translated into English that she had been looking for in a diluted way for two years, a Penguin Classic edition of a novel she owned but whose cover she liked better than her own copy, and a children's book she had read at her nani's house as a child and had never seen since. The children's book had someone's name written in it in the large uncertain handwriting of a young child. Padmini. She stood with it for a moment.

"How much for this?" she asked the seller.

He looked at it. "Twenty rupees."

She paid thirty. She always did this and it embarrassed her when people pointed it out, so she had stopped mentioning it.

Sunita, meanwhile, had found a stall selling old brass things — small statues, boxes, measuring weights, kitchen implements — and was examining each one with the methodical attention she gave to most things. She was not a collector. She did not have a weakness for objects the way Divya did for books. But she had recently moved into a new flat and was in the slow process of making it feel like a home, which she was approaching with the same careful research she gave to everything else.

She picked up a small brass diya, plain, old, its surface worn to a soft glow. It was not decorated or special in any obvious way. It was just very clearly a thing that had been used, for a long time, for what it was made for.

"This one," she told the seller.

Priya was everywhere and nowhere in particular. She had looked at a hand-embroidered tablecloth and put it down. She had looked at an old radio that didn't work and appreciated it without wanting it. She had eaten a plate of mirchi bajji from a snack stall and talked for several minutes with the woman frying them about where she sourced her green chillies. She had taken a photograph of a painting leaning against a wall that was very bad by any conventional measure but had tremendous commitment to its subject, which was a tiger standing in front of what appeared to be the Charminar.

She bought the painting for a hundred and fifty rupees. It was the tiger's expression that got her. The tiger looked completely unbothered by its improbable location.

Chapter 3: What It Said

They met at the sugarcane juice stall at ten, which was when Shyambabu had been right about the good things being gone. The light had shifted from the soft early-morning gold to the flatter brightness of proper Sunday morning. The crowd had thickened.

Divya laid her three books on the plastic table between their glasses of juice. She arranged them in a row and looked at them with the quiet satisfaction of someone who has found things she didn't know she was looking for. This was, Sunita thought, exactly how Divya was — she committed to things before she knew what they were, and it usually turned out that she had known all along.

Sunita took the brass diya out of the newspaper the seller had wrapped it in and held it in the light. It was warm from being in her bag. She rubbed a smudge off the side with the edge of her dupatta.

"It's very plain," Priya observed.

"That's what I wanted."

"I know. That's what I said."

Priya propped the tiger painting against the table leg and they all looked at it. There was a silence of the particular kind that exists between friends who have known each other long enough that silence requires no explanation.

"Why," Divya said finally.

"Look at his face," Priya said.

They looked at the tiger's face. The tiger was gazing slightly to the left of the viewer with an expression that could only be described as serene indifference to everything that was wrong with the painting, the composition, the setting, and indeed the general situation of being a tiger painted by someone who had possibly never seen a tiger.

"He simply does not care," Priya said. "I find that very aspirational."

Sunita laughed first. Then Divya, and then they were all laughing in the way they sometimes still laughed, which was the same way they had laughed twenty years ago in the hostel corridor over things that had seemed very important at the time and were now impossible to remember.

On the auto back, with the tiger painting balanced carefully across all three of their laps, Priya thought about Shyambabu and his tip. She thought about how you spent twenty years building a life and sometimes a single Sunday morning rearranged something in you, not dramatically but the way a room is rearranged when you move one piece of furniture — suddenly there is light where there wasn't.

Divya was already reading the translated short stories. Sunita was looking out the auto at the city going past. The tiger gazed past all of them at something in the middle distance that seemed to satisfy it entirely.

"Same time next Sunday?" Priya asked.

"Yes," Divya said without looking up.

"Let me check," Sunita said, and then: "Yes."
