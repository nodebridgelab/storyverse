The Forwarded Joke

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

Six months after his father's death, a man still receives the terrible WhatsApp jokes his father used to send.

Chapter 1: Good Morning

Kiran's father had been a devoted practitioner of what Kiran privately called the Bad WhatsApp Forward.

Every morning, reliably, between seven and seven-thirty, a notification: Papa. And then an image, usually with elaborate decorative borders, containing a joke or a motivational saying or — this was the worst category — a joke presented as a motivational saying. The jokes were bad in a specific and consistent way, the way only forwarded jokes from a certain generation of WhatsApp uncles could be: slightly mistranslated from Hindi, featuring wordplay that only worked in one language and usually not even in that one, accompanied by emojis that bore no relationship to the content.

Kiran had complained about them to his wife Deepa many times. Deepa had pointed out, with the patience of someone who had been listening to this complaint for three years, that his father was thinking of him every morning and that this was the way his father expressed it, and perhaps Kiran might consider this.

Kiran had considered it inadequately.

When his father had a heart attack in April — sudden, nobody's fault, the kind of thing the doctor says you cannot predict — Kiran had been at his desk at the office. He had called his mother from the parking lot and then driven to the hospital and then done all the things you do when something like this happens, which take up every hour and most of your capacity for weeks.

He had not thought about the morning jokes until the morning they were not there.

It was a Tuesday, two days after the funeral. Seven o'clock, seven-thirty, eight. No notification. He had picked up his phone and looked at it with a feeling he could not name precisely — not exactly surprise, because of course, of course — and then set it down and made his tea and stood in his kitchen and was very quiet for a long time.

He did not tell Deepa. He did not tell anyone. It felt too small to explain, which meant it was too large to explain.

Chapter 2: May

The first forwarded joke came in May.

It was a Tuesday morning, just after seven. The notification said the name of a contact Kiran recognized — his father's friend Govind Uncle, whom he knew vaguely, a retired railway man from Pune who had been in his father's WhatsApp group of college friends. The joke was about a man who went to a doctor and a cow and a parrot were somehow involved; the punchline did not quite work because the translation had lost something in the middle. There were three laughing emojis at the end and the words: Good morning! Stay blessed.

Kiran sat at the kitchen table with his phone and read it twice.

Then he understood: Govind Uncle did not know. He was in the college-friends group that his father had been in, and he was forwarding the same jokes to the same list, and Kiran's father's name was still on that list because Kiran's father's number still existed in people's contacts and no one had told Govind Uncle, or perhaps Govind Uncle had been told and had forgotten, or perhaps his father had added Kiran directly to some separate thread that Govind Uncle did not know about.

He called his mother. She confirmed: yes, some of your father's friends, they still forward things. To the old family group. Some of them don't know yet. Some of them know but do it anyway, I think, because they don't know what else to do.

Kiran sat with this.

Deepa came into the kitchen and found him at the table. She read the joke over his shoulder. She said nothing for a moment, and then she said: from Govind Uncle?

Yes, Kiran said.

Is he going to keep sending them?

I don't know, Kiran said.

Deepa sat down across from him. They looked at each other.

I'm not going to tell him to stop, Kiran said.

Deepa nodded. Okay, she said, and did not ask him to explain, because she understood without needing it explained.

Chapter 3: The List

By June, three different people from his father's various circles were forwarding jokes to Kiran on Tuesday and Thursday mornings, between seven and eight, as if they had coordinated it.

There was Govind Uncle with his doctor jokes. There was Prakash — Kiran's father's cousin in Hyderabad — who preferred jokes about husbands and wives that were mildly mortifying. There was someone named Bharat Bhai, whom Kiran did not know at all and could not identify, who sent elaborate good morning images featuring sunrises and eagles and text that said things like: A strong mind can move mountains. The eagle does not care about the opinions of chickens.

Kiran had begun to look forward to these messages.

He understood that this was not rational. He understood that Govind Uncle and Prakash and the mysterious Bharat Bhai were simply people who had not gotten the memo, or had and still did not know what to do with their Tuesday and Thursday mornings. He understood that none of them were his father.

But they had been his father's friends. His father had laughed at these same jokes — or tried to, or forwarded them because they arrived in his own inbox and he was part of this unbroken chain of bad humour that connected a generation of retired men who had mostly nothing in common except WhatsApp and a particular era of their lives. His father had been the link between Kiran and this chain, and now the chain continued, not knowing the link was gone.

Kiran started sending replies. Just a thumbs up emoji, or sometimes the laughing face. Nothing more. He did not know if they noticed. But it felt like something — like sitting at a table where his father's friends still told bad jokes, like being in a room that still smelled faintly familiar even though the person who had made it familiar was no longer there.

Govind Uncle sent a particularly terrible one in July — a bilingual pun that required knowledge of both Marathi and English and still didn't quite work — and Kiran laughed. A real laugh, sudden and short, in his kitchen at seven-fifteen on a Tuesday morning.

He typed three laughing emojis. He sent it.

He made his tea. He got ready for work. He carried his father with him through another ordinary day, which was all he could do, which was, he was learning, enough.
