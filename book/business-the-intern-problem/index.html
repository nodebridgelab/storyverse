The Intern Problem

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

A summer intern sends the wrong report to a client, and her founder must decide who absorbs the damage.

Chapter 1: The Email

It was Shweta who called Nitesh first, which told him something. Shweta was the client relationship manager for Fenix Retail, their second-largest account, and she did not call without reason.

"They got the wrong report," she said. Her voice was the particular flat of someone who has already decided that being upset is a luxury for later.

Nitesh was at his desk at three-fifteen. "How wrong?"

"Wrong client's numbers. The Fenix summary has Brightline's Q2 data in the appendix."

Brightline was another client. A competitor to Fenix in the apparel distribution space. The appendix in question contained Brightline's regional inventory breakdowns for the second quarter, which was information Fenix would find very useful and Brightline would find devastating.

"Who sent it?"

He already knew. There was only one person who had touched that report in the past twenty-four hours—Ishaan, their summer intern from a Hyderabad MBA programme, who had been doing good work for eight weeks and had been trusted with report compilation for the first time last week.

"Ishaan sent the compiled file at seven this morning. I didn't catch it before it went to the client."

This was a second problem layered inside the first: Shweta should have reviewed the file before it went out, and hadn't. Nitesh filed this away.

"What does Fenix know?"

"They noticed the client name in the header of the appendix and called me directly. They've read enough to see it's competitor data. They want to understand how it happened."

"Okay. Don't tell them anything more until I call you back. Thirty minutes."

He put down the phone and sat very still for a moment, which was what he did when he needed to think without acting.

The sequence of problems was: first, Brightline's data was now with Fenix, which was a confidentiality breach regardless of what happened next. Second, both client relationships were now at risk—Fenix would lose trust if they felt data handling was sloppy, Brightline would lose trust entirely if they found out. Third, there was a legal dimension to a confidentiality breach that he needed to understand before he did anything public. Fourth, Ishaan was twenty-three and had been given a task he wasn't adequately supervised on, and what happened to Ishaan in the next forty-eight hours was also a decision Nitesh was making.

Chapter 2: Who Is Responsible

He called the company's legal advisor, who told him what he expected: notify Brightline immediately, document everything, do not wait for the situation to escalate before disclosure. The advisor said Nitesh's exposure was manageable if he moved quickly and transparently. The kind of exposure that became serious was the kind where a company tried to quietly manage a breach that then became known.

Nitesh called Brightline next. Their account manager, Harish, answered.

"I need to tell you about something that happened this morning," Nitesh said. He said it in one breath, cleanly. A compilation error had attached the wrong appendix to a report. The appendix containing their Q2 regional inventory data had gone to another client. The other client had not been briefed to use it. Nitesh's team had identified the error within six hours. He was calling to tell Harish before they discussed next steps.

Harish was quiet.

"Which client?" he asked.

"I can't tell you that. I can tell you it's not a direct strategic competitor in the markets where your data would matter most, but I understand that doesn't fully address your concern."

"No, it doesn't." Harish's voice was careful. "I need to speak to my CEO before I say anything further. I'll call you back."

"Of course. I'll be available."

After that call, Nitesh walked to the main floor. Ishaan was at his desk, clearly already aware something was wrong—the silence in the office had the texture of bad news in transit. He was staring at his screen without really looking at it.

"Can I talk to you?" Nitesh said.

They went to the small meeting room. Nitesh explained what had happened. Ishaan went from aware to fully aware over the course of a sentence, and then sat very still with the expression of someone for whom the ground has moved unexpectedly.

"I'm sorry," Ishaan said. "I should have—I double-checked the main data but I didn't open the appendix file. It was labeled right. I assumed—"

"I know," Nitesh said. "Walk me through your process."

Ishaaan walked through it. It was clear within two minutes: the labeling system for compiled reports was confusing, Ishaan had not been given a checklist for the compilation process, and Shweta had not reviewed the final file before sending. Ishaan had made a mistake. The mistake had multiple contributing conditions.

Nitesh said: "This is going to be hard for a few days. I need you to stay, answer questions honestly, and help us document the process failure so we can prevent it from happening again. Can you do that?"

Ishaan nodded. He looked like he wanted to say something else and then didn't.

"Go on," Nitesh said.

"Should I offer to leave? I feel like I should offer to leave."

Nitesh thought about this for a moment. "No," he said. "I'm not asking you to leave. We gave you a task without adequate process around it. That's on us as much as it's on you."

Chapter 3: Public and Private

Harish called back at five. His CEO had reviewed. Brightline wanted three things: a written incident report, a review of Nitesh's data handling procedures by an external auditor, and a conversation about the next six months of their engagement.

Nitesh said yes to all three.

Fenix was simpler—they had not been threatened by the breach, they had benefited from it in a sense, and their concern was procedural rather than strategic. Shweta handled that conversation. Fenix asked for the same incident report and said they'd wait to see the outcomes before deciding anything about the ongoing relationship.

The incident report took Nitesh three hours to write. He was careful to be accurate: the specific sequence, the system failure in the labeling process, the supervision gap, the response timeline. He did not write Ishaan's name in the section about the sequence. He wrote 'a team member compiled the report' and 'the compilation step was undertaken without final review by the account manager.'

He thought about whether this was protecting Ishaan or being honest, and decided it was both. The intern's name was not relevant to Brightline's understanding of what had happened and what was being fixed. What was relevant was the process failure, which was the company's responsibility and not one person's.

He sent the report to both clients and posted a condensed version on the company's client portal with a note that said the full details were available on request.

Then he called a team meeting. Not a large one—just the people involved and his two senior managers. He laid out what had happened, what had gone wrong in the process, and what they were changing: a mandatory checklist for all compiled deliverables, a second-review step for any report containing client data before it went to the client, and a relabeling of the file storage system that had been confusing Ishaan and, as it turned out, one of the senior managers had also found confusing but hadn't said anything.

He did not make anyone apologise in the room. He did not assign blame publicly. After the meeting Shweta came to him privately and said she was sorry she hadn't reviewed the file, which was the conversation that needed to happen and did.

Ishaan's internship ended three weeks later, on its original schedule. On his last day, Nitesh sat with him for thirty minutes and gave him feedback—real feedback, not the kind that was wrapped in so much positive framing that nothing landed. He said Ishaan had good instincts and was careful in most of his work, and that the lesson from the incident was not about this specific mistake but about the habit of verification in high-stakes situations.

Ishaan said: "Are you going to be able to give me a reference?"

"Yes," Nitesh said. "An honest one."

Ishaaan thought about that for a second. "Okay," he said. "That's fair."

Brightline's engagement renewed at the end of the year, two months after the incident. The email from Harish said: 'How you handled this gave us more confidence in you, not less.' Nitesh kept that email in a folder. He looked at it sometimes when he was dealing with something hard, not as comfort exactly, but as evidence that the straightforward path occasionally led somewhere decent.
