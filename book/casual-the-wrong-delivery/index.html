The Wrong Delivery

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

A Swiggy mix-up lands a stranger's biryani at the wrong door — and sparks the friendship three years of proximity never could.

Chapter 1: Flat 12B Gets Someone Else's Biryani

Arnav had ordered dal tadka and two rotis. He was very sure about this because he had ordered the same thing every rainy Wednesday for the past four months, partly because it was the only item on the menu of a place called Home Flavours that actually tasted like what it claimed to be, and partly because Wednesdays were the kind of days that asked for something reliable.

So when the delivery notification said arrived and he opened the door to find a bag that was dramatically larger and more fragrant than dal tadka and two rotis had any right to be, he knew something had gone wrong. He checked the label. The name on the bag said Meera. The flat number said 12A. He lived in 12B.

He stood in the doorway with this information for a moment. It had been a long Wednesday. He had been in back-to-back meetings since nine and had eaten nothing except a banana and three biscuits. The biryani smell, which was unmistakably biryani, was not helping his objectivity.

He knocked on 12A.

In three years of living next to 12A, Arnav had knocked on this door exactly once, to ask if they could hear a water leak sound because he could and his plumber couldn't figure out where it was coming from. A woman had opened the door, said she'd check, come back thirty seconds later, said she couldn't hear anything, and closed the door. They had nodded at each other in the corridor perhaps two hundred times since. This was the full extent of their relationship.

The door opened. The same woman. Late twenties or early thirties, he guessed, with paint on her right forearm — not the artistic kind, just the accidental kind you get when you're painting a wall and lean into it without thinking. She looked at him, then at the bag, and something in her expression went from polite-stranger to recognising-the-smell in about half a second.

"I think this is yours," he said.

She checked her phone. "The app says it was delivered." She looked at the bag, then at him. "He must have left it at the wrong door."

"He did."

She reached for the bag. Then stopped. "It'll be cold by the time I reheat it. My microwave is broken."

He had a microwave. He almost said this, and then he did say this, because it was true and the biryani smell was extremely present in the situation. "I have a microwave. If you want to— I mean, it's your food."

She looked at him for a moment with the particular assessment that people reserve for strangers they are considering trusting with something small.

"Ten minutes?" she said.

"Sure."

Chapter 2: The Microwave and After

Her name was Meera. She worked from home as a UX designer and was painting her bedroom a colour she described as "somewhere between green and not green" that she had spent three weeks choosing and was now not entirely sure about, which was why her arm had paint on it at seven-thirty on a Wednesday evening.

Arnav found this out in the five minutes it took to heat the biryani, during which they stood on opposite sides of his small kitchen island and talked with the slightly heightened friendliness of two people who have just discovered that proximity does not require awkwardness, and who are both a little embarrassed that it took a food delivery error to establish this.

The microwave beeped. She picked up her bag.

"Thank you," she said. She hesitated. "Do you want some? There's way too much for one person. I wasn't really thinking when I ordered it, I was just craving biryani and clicked quantity two instead of one."

His dal tadka was still pending. The Swiggy app was showing a new estimated delivery of forty minutes, which felt unreasonably long for dal tadka.

"Sure," he said.

They ate at his dining table, which he usually ate at alone while watching something on his laptop, so it felt strange to have the laptop closed and someone sitting across from it. Strange but not bad. The biryani was from a place called Siddique's and was the kind of biryani that requires concentration — long-grained rice, the egg tucked in like it belonged there, the smell of whole spices that had been taken seriously. Meera ate with the focus of someone who had genuinely been craving this specific thing.

"Good call on the biryani," he said.

"I have good biryani instincts," she said. "Bad colour instincts apparently."

"Show me the colour."

She looked up. "Seriously?"

"I painted my whole flat last year. I have opinions about it that I have not been able to share with anyone."

So after dinner they went to look at her bedroom wall. It was, he thought, a very nice colour — a muted sage that was doing something warm in the evening light that he suspected it was not doing in the morning when she had looked at it under daylight and had doubts.

"It works," he said. "Come look at it when the morning light hits from the left. It'll be different."

She leaned in the doorframe and looked at it. "I trust you because you have strong microwave energy," she said, which made him laugh.

His dal tadka arrived while he was in her flat. He ate it cold at ten PM and it was still fine.

Chapter 3: The Habit of It

The rule they didn't make but somehow both followed was this: door-knocking was fine for practical things. Meera knocked when she needed the communal broom that lived outside his door for some reason neither of them questioned. Arnav knocked when a package arrived for her while she was out and he had signed for it. This was already more door-knocking than had happened in three years and it established something — a sense that the door was a door you could knock on, not just a wall with a handle.

The biryani thing happened again three weeks later, except this time Arnav's pizza arrived at her door and she knocked to return it, and he invited her in because the TV was on and the match was at a critical point, and she turned out to have strong opinions about cricket for someone who had previously not struck him as a cricket person. She stayed for forty minutes and left during the ad break after the wicket fell.

They had chai together twice on the corridor — not planned, just both happening to open their doors at the same moment on evenings when the air outside was nice and neither of them felt like being inside. These were half-hour conversations about nothing in particular: her deadline, his bad video call, a chai stall near the office that did a cutting that she had described so well he'd gone and tried it.

He thought about this — about how you could live next to someone for three years and build a life in their immediate proximity and not know a single thing about them except that they could hear a water leak if it was loud enough. And then a delivery person left a bag at the wrong door and suddenly there was a person there.

He mentioned this to Meera one evening on the corridor and she said, "I think we were both just waiting for a reason. We didn't not want to know each other. We just didn't have a moment."

"A biryani moment."

"Exactly."

He thought about all the things that served as biryani moments — the shared smoke outside, the meeting that runs long and you end up talking to someone you'd never have talked to otherwise, the flight delay, the building evacuation, the stuck lift. The ordinary disruptions that turn out to be the actual structure of how people enter each other's lives.

Meera's bedroom wall, when the morning light hit it from the left, turned out to be exactly right. She sent him a photo of it through the new contact she had saved as "12B Arnav" and then changed, after a moment's thought, to just "Arnav."

He changed hers from "12A neighbour" to "Meera" at the same time, in the same moment, without knowing she was doing it too.
