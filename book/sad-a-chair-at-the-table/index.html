A Chair at the Table

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

After their grandmother dies, a family keeps setting her place at dinner for six months without ever saying why.

Chapter 1: Six

The first time it happened, Meena assumed her daughter had made a mistake.

The family sat down to dinner three nights after the funeral — Meena, her husband Rajan, their daughter Saanvi who was seventeen, their son Dev who was thirteen, and Rajan's younger brother Vikram, who had stayed on from Delhi and was sleeping on the pull-out sofa. Five people. But when Meena carried the rice pot to the table she counted six plates, six glasses of water, six sets of cutlery laid out neatly on the tablecloth that had been her mother-in-law's.

Saanvi was the one who had set the table. She was standing at the counter now with her back turned, cutting limes.

Meena set the rice down. She looked at the sixth plate, which sat at the head of the table where Aaji had always sat. She looked at the empty chair, the wooden one with the slightly wobbly left leg that Rajan kept meaning to fix.

She did not say anything about it. She sat down. Rajan came in from washing his hands and looked at the sixth plate and also said nothing. Dev came in arguing about something with Vikram and neither of them seemed to notice. Saanvi brought the limes and sat down.

They ate dinner. The sixth plate stayed empty. The glass of water was never drunk. At the end of the meal, when Saanvi cleared the table, she took all six plates to the sink and washed them together without comment.

In the weeks that followed, the pattern continued. Sometimes it was Saanvi who set the extra place. Once it was Dev, coming home late after cricket practice and laying the table hurriedly while dinner was being served. Once — and this was the one that stopped Meena's breath — it was Rajan, who set the table while Meena was on the phone, and who sat down with the rest of them as if nothing particular had happened.

No one talked about it. No one acknowledged it. The chair stayed at the head of the table with its wobbly left leg, and every evening a plate was laid in front of it, and every evening the plate was cleared away clean.

Chapter 2: What Each of Them Knew

Saanvi had started it without deciding to.

She had been setting the table on auto-pilot, three nights after the funeral, and she had laid six places before she knew what she was doing, and then she had looked at the sixth place and something in her had simply refused to take the plate away. It felt — she would not have been able to explain this to anyone — like an act of basic courtesy. Aaji had sat there for as long as Saanvi could remember. It seemed rude to stop.

She knew it was not rational. She was seventeen and not an irrational person. She just could not make herself pick up the plate.

Dev noticed within the first week but said nothing because he assumed Saanvi had a reason and he trusted her reasons the way younger siblings trust older ones, not always wisely but persistently. Also, if he was being honest with himself — and Dev was still young enough that this kind of honesty came relatively easily — he found he did not want the chair to be empty either. He had started sitting slightly differently at dinner so that Aaji's chair was in his eyeline.

Rajan knew exactly what he was doing when he set the sixth place the one time. He had stood in his own kitchen counting the plates in his hand and thought: not tonight. He thought about his mother bringing those plates from her own home when he got married, wrapping them herself in newspaper in the way people used to wrap things. He thought about her eating at this table for twenty years. He thought: not tonight.

Meena said nothing because she understood that some things hold a family together not by being discussed but by being shared. She had been a daughter-in-law for twenty-two years. She had learned, slowly and not always gracefully, that some of what made this family itself was carried not in words but in habits — the way they took tea, the particular pickle Aaji made at Diwali, the prayers on certain mornings that felt less like religion and more like muscle memory.

The sixth place at the table was the same kind of thing. It was them saying, without saying: we know she is gone. We know. But we are not ready to make the table smaller yet.

Chapter 3: March

It ended in March, six months after the funeral, on the night Vikram called from Delhi to say he was getting married.

It was a Sunday. Rajan put the phone on speaker and they gathered around the table to hear Vikram's news, and there was the noise and warmth of an unexpected announcement — when, where, is she nice, of course she's nice he wouldn't ask otherwise — and then Saanvi went to set the table for dinner and came back with five plates.

No one saw her make the decision. She did not announce it. She laid five places, and they all sat down, and they ate dinner while Rajan told stories about Vikram as a child that Vikram, still on speaker in the middle of the table, protested loudly.

At one point in the middle of the meal Dev laughed so hard at one of the stories that he knocked his glass of water over, and in the chaos of mopping it up and the complaints and the laughter, Meena looked at the head of the table.

The chair was empty, as it had always been empty. But tonight no plate had been laid in front of it.

She looked at Saanvi, who was blotting the tablecloth with a dish towel. Saanvi met her eyes for a moment and Meena gave her a small nod, the kind of nod that says: yes. I see. It's okay.

Rajan fixed the wobbly leg of the chair the following weekend, not because anyone asked him to but because he had been meaning to for years and now, somehow, the moment felt right. He got down on his knees with a screwdriver and tightened the joint, and when he was done he pressed on the leg to test it, and it held.

They moved the chair slightly when the new arrangement felt better. They ate their dinners. The table was five now, and sometimes four when Dev was at practice, and the family continued in the way families continue — forward, carrying what they carry, putting down what they finally can.
