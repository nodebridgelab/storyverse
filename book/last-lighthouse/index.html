The Last Lighthouse of Kanyakumari

by Arjun Mehta · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

A marine archaeologist returns to a decommissioned lighthouse to learn why three ships vanished in its final year of operation.

Read nowOpen in the app

About this story

The Last Lighthouse of Kanyakumari is an atmospheric mystery set at the southern tip of India, where three seas meet and a decommissioned lighthouse holds a secret that has stayed hidden for thirty years. It is part family story, part historical puzzle — Meera is not just an archaeologist investigating three ship disappearances; she is a granddaughter trying to understand a man she thought she knew.

The story moves the way a lighthouse should: in slow, deliberate rotations, each chapter revealing something that changes the angle on what came before. The setting — the wet granite, the oil room, the staircase that leads down into rock — is used precisely, not as backdrop but as the architecture of the mystery itself.

Readers who enjoy compact, atmospheric mysteries with a strong sense of place, and stories where the puzzle is inseparable from the person solving it, will find exactly that here.

Meet the characters

  • Meera — a marine archaeologist who arrives at Kanyakumari with the lighthouse keys her mother posted her and instructions to "go properly." She is methodical and observant in the way people are when they are holding grief at arm's length.
  • Meera's grandfather — the lighthouse keeper in 1994, present only through his logbook, his crossed-out entry for March 15th, and the wooden crate in the lamp room. The mystery is inseparable from who he was.

Reading time & format

3 chapters · approx. 12–15 minutes total · best read in one sitting, ideally at night. The story is designed to be read as a single experience — the three chapters build toward a final image that only lands fully if you have felt the weight of the first two. It is a story that benefits from quiet and from your full attention.

Why we published it

Indian mystery fiction rarely uses the landscape as precisely as this story does. Kanyakumari — the tip of the subcontinent where the Bay of Bengal, the Arabian Sea, and the Indian Ocean meet — is a setting charged with endings and edges, and the story knows this. We published it because the final paragraph achieves something genuinely difficult: it resolves the mystery, deepens the character, and contains a line about secrets and light that we have not been able to get out of our heads. That is worth sharing.

Chapter 1: The Iron Door

The monsoon had spent itself by the time Meera reached the tip of the subcontinent. Kanyakumari wore the aftermath well: wet granite shining like old coins, fishermen mending nets the colour of rust, and the sea on three sides arguing with itself about which direction to leave in.

The lighthouse stood where it had always stood, white as a fresh page. Its iron door was the same iron door from her grandfather's stories, with a wheel handle frozen mid-turn and a smell, even from ten paces away, of salt and cold lamp oil.

She had the keys. That was the strange part. Her mother had posted them with a note that said only: If you must go, go properly.

Think about it

  1. The lighthouse is "white as a fresh page." Given that Meera is about to discover something old and hidden, what does this image set up?
  2. Her mother says "if you must go, go properly" but doesn't say why Meera must go. What does the mother's note imply about how much she knows — and how much she is not saying?

Chapter 2: Logbook, 1994

The logbook was on the third shelf of the oil room, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin as if it could still get wet.

Meera sat on the bottom stair with her phone torch between her teeth and read her grandfather's handwriting for the first time in her life. It was small, slanted and infuriatingly calm. March 14th: Lamp trimmed. Visibility poor. The entry for March 15th was crossed out so hard the pen had torn the page.

It was only when she lowered the phone that she noticed the second staircase, the one that went down into the rock, where no lighthouse plan she had ever seen said a staircase should be.

Think about it

  1. The handwriting is "infuriatingly calm." What does Meera want to find in the logbook, and why is calm the wrong register for it?
  2. The torn page is the mystery's pivot — something happened on March 15th that her grandfather tried to erase. What does the act of crossing out rather than tearing out the page tell us about him?

Chapter 3: The Lamp Room

Two hundred and twelve steps. She counted them the way her grandfather must have counted them, four thousand times a year, one honest step at a time.

The lamp room was smaller than she had imagined and brighter, the great lens throwing the morning sun back in fractured petals across the walls. In the centre, where the lamp should have been, stood a wooden crate stamped with the mark of a company that had dissolved the year she was born.

Whatever her grandfather had kept in the light, he had kept it in plain sight. The best place to hide a secret, Meera thought, is where everyone expects the light to be.

Think about it

  1. "Two hundred and twelve steps. She counted them the way her grandfather must have." What is the story doing with this act of parallel counting?
  2. The final line: "The best place to hide a secret is where everyone expects the light to be." Does this make Meera's grandfather a villain, a protector, or something harder to categorise?
  3. The story ends as Meera discovers the crate — before she opens it. What do you think is inside, and does the story actually need you to know?
