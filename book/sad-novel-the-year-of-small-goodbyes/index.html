The Year of Small Goodbyes

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 11 chapters · Completed

When her father's retirement forces a family to leave their government quarter, Nandini records every goodbye in a notebook no one is meant to read.

Chapter 1: The Announcement

The first thing Nandini did when her father told them was go to her room and open a new notebook.

This was not a dramatic gesture. She opened notebooks the way other people made tea, automatically, when something needed holding. She had been doing it since she was eleven, which was now fourteen years ago, and she had a shelf of twenty-six filled ones — not journals exactly, more like containers. Things went into them when there was no other place for things to go.

The announcement had been made at dinner, which in itself was unusual. Her father, Ashok Mehta, was a man who delivered important news indirectly — you learned things via implication and the arrangement of certain conversations. For him to sit down and say: I am retiring in March, the quarter must be vacated by June, was almost theatrical in its directness.

Nandini's mother had said nothing for a moment. Then: so soon.

That was October. There were eight months.

The government quarter — officially called D-7 in the residential complex maintained by the Ministry of Works — had been their home for twenty years. Nandini had been eleven when they moved in, had gone to college from here and come back, had done her masters from here, had taken her first job and then her second, had been home during a pandemic, had returned after a heartbreak she was not going to document in a notebook ever, and had found, at twenty-five and then twenty-seven and then thirty, that she was still here, still in the room with the almond tree outside the window, and she had not minded.

She opened the notebook and wrote at the top: October 18. Papa is retiring.

Then she wrote: Eight months.

Then she drew a line down the centre of the page and wrote on the left side: Things I will miss. On the right side she wrote: Things I will not miss.

The left side filled up quickly. The right side had: the drain smell in July. She crossed the right-side column out. This was not the kind of honest she was going to be about this.

Chapter 2: Ramsewak

The notebook's first real entry was about Ramsewak, which surprised her.

Ramsewak was the dhobi who had been coming to D-7 since before they moved in. He was part of the complex's institutional history in the way certain people become institutional — he had been coming so long that the residents no longer registered him as a choice, he was simply a fact, like the electricity schedule or the tree maintenance. He collected laundry on Mondays and returned it on Wednesdays, pressed and folded and wrapped in brown paper with a system that Nandini had never been able to entirely decode but which never produced errors.

He came on a Monday in November, which was how Nandini found herself writing about him.

She had gone out to give him the bundle — her mother was at her sister's — and she had stood at the door watching him note the items in his small register, the same register he seemed to have been using for years, soft with handling at the edges. He had a particular way of entering things, a rapid cursive she couldn't read, and a particular way of folding the register shut when he was done.

She said: Ramsewak bhaiya, we are leaving in June.

He looked up. Then he looked at her with the expression of someone calculating. How many years had he been coming? He would know to the year.

Where will you go? he asked.

She explained — they were looking at flats in Dwarka, her father had family in Delhi proper, there were possibilities not yet decided.

He nodded slowly. He picked up the bundle. Then he said, more to himself than to her: the Sharmas went in '09. The Kapoors in '14. He did not say this as a complaint. He said it the way you say things that are simply true, that are part of the record.

She went upstairs and wrote: November 4. Ramsewak bhaiya. He has been coming here since 1998. He remembers when we moved in. He will find other customers. He always does. But something will be over that was never really named. I never thanked him. I don't know how to start now.

Chapter 3: The Peepal Tree

The peepal tree at the gate had been old when they moved in.

Nandini had gone through a phase as a child of asking her father how old things were — the building, the road, the Delhi Metro, the school — and he had told her the peepal was at least sixty years, maybe older. Old enough that the base of its trunk had cracked and heaved the paving stones on one side, and the complex administration sent someone every few years to do something about the paving stones, and the paving stones were always heaved again within a season. The peepal always won.

In March, when the tree came into its full spring unfurling — the pale copper-red of new peepal leaves before they darkened — Nandini went and sat under it on a Sunday morning with the notebook.

She had been recording things all winter. The entry about Ramsewak. An entry about the market at the colony gate where she had bought bread and eggs for ten years, where the egg vendor knew she wanted the medium ones and set them aside. An entry about the evening light in their flat at four-thirty from October to February, which came through the west window at an angle that made the walls go gold and that she had never mentioned to anyone as significant but that she had been watching her entire adult life.

She wrote about the peepal now. It was harder because the tree had no transaction history with her, no Monday-morning schedule, no economy. The tree was just there. It had been there before her. It would be there after. It did not know about June.

She wrote: there is no sentimentality in a peepal tree. They outlive everything. They crack foundations. They drop leaves all year. If I grieve the peepal it is only because it will not notice.

Then she wrote: but someone else will sit here in October and look up through these leaves and not know that I sat here first and that this was mine, the way all trees are temporarily and secretly yours.

Chapter 4: Her Mother's Version

Her mother did not keep a notebook. Her mother's equivalent was cleaning.

From November onward, her mother Nirmala cleaned with increasing intensity — not the ordinary cleaning but the deep archaeology of a house occupied for twenty years. She went through cupboards and found: a pressure cooker her own mother had given her that she had replaced fifteen years ago but not been able to discard. Letters from Nandini's childhood school sent home and never dealt with. A small idol wrapped in red cloth that she placed in the prayer corner without explanation. A bag of mustard seeds so old they had solidified.

Nandini watched her mother do this and took notes, though not in a way her mother would see. She wrote: Mama is remembering things forward. She is sorting out what to keep before she knows where she's going.

She did not ask her mother how she felt about leaving. This was not because she did not care but because she knew, from twenty years of reading her mother carefully, that direct questions about feelings produced deflection while proximity and shared tasks produced honesty. So she sat with her mother during the cleaning. She carried boxes. She held things up for decisions. She let her mother talk about the things the objects reminded her of, which was its own kind of telling.

Her mother said, sorting through a bag of miscellaneous cables: when you were small and cried at night I would bring you to the kitchen and make warm milk. This kitchen.

Nandini did not say anything. She accepted a cable whose purpose was unclear and put it in the pile to be discarded.

Her mother said: you know what I will miss most? The kitchen window. Because when I cook I look out the window and the garden is there. In Dwarka — she had been to see the flat twice — the window faces the next building.

Nandini wrote this down that evening: the kitchen window. The garden. Facing the next building. These are things no property listing has words for.

Chapter 5: Salim

The watchman's name was Salim.

He was one of three watchmen who rotated through D-7 — morning, evening, night — but Salim had been the morning watchman for as long as Nandini could remember, which meant that every morning when she left for work or went for a walk or ran down for something from the market, Salim was there in his plastic chair by the gate with his register, which was a larger and more official register than Ramsewak's but used in the same spirit.

He was from somewhere in Uttar Pradesh — a district she had never been to, could not locate precisely on a map, that she knew only as the place Salim came from. He had been in Delhi for thirty years. He had a son in Class Ten. He sometimes had a radio on very quietly. He called her didi.

In April she stopped at the gate on her evening walk and told him.

He said: haan, I heard. Madam told security office.

She felt, stupidly, that she should have told him herself. That the information coming through official channels was — what? Less personal? But it was not personal. He was the watchman. She was a resident. That was the relationship, and in twenty years it had not been any more or less than that, and she was the one making it more in her own head.

She said: we have been here a long time. Twenty years.

Han didi, he said. Twenty years is long.

They looked at each other. In the street behind her an auto passed, and the evening call to prayer started somewhere a few streets over, carried on the air the way it always was in this part of Delhi in this hour.

She said: you will be here after we go.

God willing, he said. Then he smiled, slightly: someone else will come. It is always like this.

She went home and wrote for a long time. At the end of the entry she wrote: he is right. It is always like this. I don't know why I think our leaving is different. But I think our leaving is different.

Chapter 6: Vidya Aunty

Vidya Aunty lived in D-12, which was a ten-second walk from D-7, and in twenty years she had been a constant in the way some neighbours become constants without ever becoming intimate — she was there in the periphery of every significant occasion, at every colony event, at every informal gathering, always with an opinion about something and always with a steel box of biscuits or namkeen to share without announcement.

She had known Nandini since Nandini was eleven.

She came for tea in May, which was something she had done perhaps twice in twenty years but which she did now as if she had always done it, sitting in the drawing room with her steel box (chakli this time) and her opinions and her complete unembarrassed directness.

She said: so you are going.

Nandini's mother said: June.

Vidya Aunty said: where are you looking? Has Ashok decided?

Dwarka, Nandini's mother said. A flat near his cousin.

Vidya Aunty made a sound that communicated complex thoughts about Dwarka without requiring articulation. Then she said: when did we all get so old that we are leaving? First the Mehrotras, then the Sharmas, now you.

Nandini's mother said: it is the way of things.

Vidya Aunty said: yes, yes. But I don't like it.

Nandini looked at Vidya Aunty — who was perhaps sixty-eight, whose husband had died six years ago and whose children lived in Bangalore and Hyderabad and who would still be in D-12 in June and in October and in the next decade — and thought about what it meant to be the one who stays while others leave. The reverse of what she had been thinking.

She wrote that night: I have been thinking of leaving as a loss that belongs only to us. But Vidya Aunty will lose us too. All the leavings belong to everyone left behind. Why didn't I understand that before?

She wrote: I should have asked her what she would miss.

Chapter 7: The Notebook

In May, her younger brother Rohit came from Pune for a weekend.

Rohit had left the quarter for college twelve years ago and had not lived here since, though he came for every Diwali and occasionally for a month in summer. His relationship with D-7 was therefore different from Nandini's — he had fewer continuous years in it, but perhaps more concentrated memory, the way places you visit become more vivid than places you live in.

He found the notebook.

This was her fault. She had left it on the dining table, which she never did — she was careful with the notebooks, not out of secrecy exactly but out of a sense that they were for no one else. But she had been called to help her mother with something and had left it face up on the table and Rohit had come in from his walk and seen it.

When she came back he was sitting at the table with the notebook open.

She stood in the doorway.

He looked up. He didn't look guilty exactly. He looked the way people look when they have read something they weren't meant to and found it more real than they expected.

He said: why haven't you shown this to anyone?

Because it's not for showing, she said.

Nandini, he said. This is — he held it up. This is everything. Salim, Ramsewak bhaiya, the kitchen window, the peepal tree. It's all here.

I know, she said. That's why I wrote it.

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said: do you know how Papa feels? About leaving?

She sat down at the table across from him. She did not know, actually. She had written about her mother and the neighbours and the market and the tree. She had not written one word about her father.

She took the notebook. She looked at it. She said: I don't know how Papa feels.

Neither do I, Rohit said. He never says.

She put the notebook down between them on the table. She said: I'll ask him.

Chapter 8: Her Father

She asked him on a Sunday morning in the last week of May.

They were both up early — she never slept late, and her father, in retirement, had discovered that he also did not sleep late, which surprised him. He had made himself chai. She made herself chai. They sat at the kitchen table in the early light.

Papa, she said, how do you feel about leaving?

Her father looked at her over his cup. He was a man of sixty-three who had worked for the government for thirty-eight years and had the particular quality of long-service bureaucrats, which is a kind of composed expressiveness — he showed you what he chose to show, which was not nothing, but was edited.

He said: it was time.

I know, she said. But how do you feel?

He set down his cup. He looked at the kitchen window, which faced the garden, the way her mother always looked at it.

He said: when I came here in 2004, D-7 had the family of a forest officer before us. I met him at the handover. He had been here eighteen years. He told me: you will not want to leave when your time comes. I thought this was excessive.

He paused.

He was right, he said. Not excessively — he was right by exactly the right amount. I do not want to leave. But I am not unhappy that I was here.

Nandini thought about this.

She said: what will you miss most?

Her father said: the walk. The morning walk through the quarter. It is nothing special — just the roads, the same trees, the guard posts, the other walkers. But I have walked it every morning for twenty years and I know every inch of it. In Dwarka I will have to learn a new walk.

He said this plainly, as a logistical fact. But it was not a logistical fact. She knew it was not a logistical fact.

She wrote it that afternoon: he will have to learn a new walk. This is what it costs, at sixty-three. Learning the same morning over again in a new place.

Chapter 9: The Last Market

She went to the market at the colony gate on the Tuesday before they left, which was the last Tuesday she would be resident here, and she went intentionally, slowly, with the notebook.

The market was not a real market — it was a cluster of small shops at the entrance road, the sort that springs up wherever there are enough residents to support the basics: a general store, a vegetable vendor, a tailor, the man who sold eggs. A chai stall that had been there since before Nandini's arrival and that served tea in glasses so small they required two.

She bought eggs from the egg vendor. He had the medium ones out already.

She said: bhaiya, we are leaving on Friday.

He said: haan, Madam ji told me. He did not pause in his counting. He wrapped the eggs in newspaper. Then he said: where to?

Dwarka.

A pause. Then: far.

Far enough, she said.

He handed her the package. He said: the new people — whoever comes to D-7 — I'll remember what they need. It takes time but I remember.

She knew this was kindness dressed as practicality. She accepted it as both.

She walked the market lane slowly after that, looking at things. The chai stall. The tailor's shop where her mother's blouses had been altered twice. The general store where she had bought salt and oil and band-aids and mosquito repellent and birthday candles and every small necessity of twenty years. The vendor who sold flowers in the mornings, who was not there today but whose spot she knew by its absence.

She did not buy anything else. She did not need anything else. She walked the lane and looked and wrote afterward, sitting on the steps of D-7: I have never seen this market except as a convenience. Today I see it as itself. This is the problem with leaving — it teaches you to see what you were looking at. It always teaches you too late.

Chapter 10: The Last Night

The flat was empty by Thursday evening.

The movers had come on Wednesday and Thursday, and by evening on Thursday the rooms were what rooms become when they are emptied — slightly larger, slightly more echoing, showing their own shapes without furniture to soften them. The walls had marks where things had hung. The floors had the slight indentations of twenty years of furniture in specific places.

Nandini's father walked through the rooms once. He was very quiet.

Her mother had gone to stay with her sister to give herself some distance from the day — she had said her goodbyes in the morning before the movers finished, she could not watch them take the last things. Rohit had gone back to Pune the previous weekend and would meet them in Dwarka.

It was just Nandini and her father, sitting on the floor of the drawing room with the torchlight on their phones because the overhead bulb had been packed.

She had the notebook.

She had not decided to show it to him. But she had brought it, and he had looked at it, and she had handed it to him without fully meaning to.

He read it for a long time. The whole market walk. Ramsewak. Salim. Vidya Aunty. The peepal tree. Her mother and the kitchen window. The conversation with him about the walk.

When he finished he closed the notebook and held it for a moment and then held it out to her. His face was the edited face. But the edit was less complete than usual.

He said: twenty years is what I gave you. As a home. This is what you made of it.

She did not know what to say. She took the notebook.

He said: it's good. That you wrote it.

She said: I wasn't going to show anyone.

He said: show them or don't. But don't lose it.

They sat in the empty room for another hour, not saying much. When it was time they locked the door, and her father held the key for a moment — the original key, on the keyring he had been given at handover in 2004 — and then put it in his pocket to return to the office in the morning. And they walked out past Salim, who was on his chair in the night, and through the peepal gate, and out.

Chapter 11: Dwarka

The new flat was smaller.

This was not a surprise. They had known it would be smaller. The kitchen faced the next building, as her mother had noted. The morning walk, as her father had said, was unknown territory, to be relearned. There was no peepal tree. There was a neem, young, planted by the builder along the boundary, that would take decades to become itself.

Nandini's room had a different window — west-facing, which she had not had before. In the evenings the light came in at an angle she did not yet know.

She opened a new notebook. She wrote the date and the address. She looked at the room around her.

She had twenty-six filled notebooks on a shelf that would be reassembled in this room. She had one half-filled notebook about leaving, which was not about leaving at all but about paying attention, which was the same thing. She had a window that would make its own particular light in its own particular seasons, which she did not yet know but would.

She wrote: October 15. New address. We have arrived.

She had thought she would feel only loss. She did feel loss — clean and specific, the way loss is when you have documented it properly and it does not ambush you. But she also felt something else she had not expected. Not hope exactly, which seemed too large. More like the feeling of standing at the beginning of a list with fresh pages.

She had said goodbye to Ramsewak and Salim and the egg vendor and the peepal tree and the kitchen window and Vidya Aunty and twenty years of mornings in a particular place. She had written them all down. They were in the notebooks, in language, real and held.

She did not know the new dhobi yet. She did not know what the morning walk looked like. She did not know the egg vendor, or if there was one nearby, or the chai stall, or the watchman's name.

She wrote: I will learn it. I will learn it the way I learned the other one, which is by living inside it until it becomes itself. And someday I will leave this too and I will write that down also, because that is the only way I know to be in a place: to see it clearly, every ordinary thing, every unremarkable morning, every person who does a small service with care. To hold it while I have it. To let it go when I must. To carry what can be carried. To know the difference.

She closed the notebook. The evening light came through the west window, unfamiliar, golden, new.
