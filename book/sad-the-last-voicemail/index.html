The Last Voicemail

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

A woman replays her late mother's voicemail every morning over chai, unable to press delete.

Chapter 1: Forty-Three Seconds

The phone is old, a Samsung with a cracked screen protector that Reena has never replaced because replacing it would feel like erasing something. She keeps it in the kitchen drawer beside the spare key and a roll of masking tape, and every morning, before the chai has finished brewing, she takes it out.

The voicemail is forty-three seconds long. It begins with her mother saying her name the way only her mother ever said it — Reenaaaaa, with that particular trailing note, as if the name itself were a question and an answer at once. Then there is the sound of her mother adjusting the phone against her ear, the small domestic rustle of a woman who never quite figured out speakerphone. Then: Beta, I made that dal you like. The one with the tamarind. I put some in a box. You can pick it up whenever. No rush. And then, before hanging up, a pause so ordinary that Reena used to rush past it in the old life, when her mother was still alive and pauses between them were not precious. Just: Okay. Bye-bye.

That is all. Okay. Bye-bye.

Reena had received the voicemail on a Tuesday in March, eight months before her mother's stroke. She had not listened to it then. She had been at a meeting. She had meant to call back. She had called back that evening and they had talked for twenty minutes about nothing — a neighbour's new car, a television serial, whether Reena's building society was reliable — and she had completely forgotten there was a voicemail waiting.

She found it four days after the funeral, scrolling through her phone in a kind of numb archaeology, and she sat down on the kitchen floor and did not get up for a long time.

Now she pours the chai into the blue mug her mother brought from a ceramics fair in Pune, and she presses play. Every morning. She tells herself she will stop. She tells herself it is not healthy. She tells herself her mother would laugh at her and say, Reena, you always made everything so dramatic. But every morning the chai is ready and the drawer is right there, and the forty-three seconds are the closest she can get to standing in her mother's kitchen again with the tamarind dal on the stove and nowhere she has to be.

Chapter 2: What Her Sister Said

Priya came in October, the first Diwali after, and she found the old Samsung on the counter instead of in the drawer, which meant Reena had been listening to it in the afternoon as well.

Priya did not say anything at first. She made tea — she always made tea when she did not know how to begin — and they sat at the kitchen table and talked about Priya's son's school and a water leak in the building and the price of flights. Then Priya picked up the Samsung and turned it in her hands the way you turn something you already know.

You still have it, she said.

Reena said nothing. Priya set the phone down between them on the table.

I deleted mine, Priya said. There was a voicemail from Ma on my phone too. From around the same time. I deleted it in the first week. I don't know why. I just — I couldn't.

Reena looked at her sister. She had not known this. In the months since the funeral they had talked every few days, they had sorted the paperwork together and given away the saris together and decided together what to do with the flat, and this was the first Reena was hearing of another voicemail, a second forty-three seconds she would never hear.

What did she say? Reena asked.

Priya shook her head slowly. Something about calling the plumber. It was nothing. Just Ma being Ma.

They were quiet for a while. Outside, someone's children were setting off small crackers on the street, the pop and fizz of them carrying up through the window Reena had left open an inch for the smell of the city's smoke and marigolds.

I'm not ready, Reena said, though Priya had not asked.

I know, Priya said.

She picked up the Samsung again and pressed play without asking, and they sat there together in the kitchen while their mother said Reenaaaaa, and adjusted the phone against her ear, and told them both about the dal with the tamarind. When the forty-three seconds ended and the silence came, Priya did not say anything wise or useful. She only put the phone back down between them and wrapped both hands around her mug of tea, and that was enough. That was more than enough.

Chapter 3: The Morning She Almost Did

It was a Wednesday in February, almost fourteen months after the funeral, when Reena came closest.

She had not slept well. The building had been noisy until late — a wedding in the flat below, the bass of the sound system carrying up through the walls, which made her think of her mother dancing at cousins' weddings, the particular unselfconscious way she moved, as if music were something that simply happened to your body and you had no choice but to go along with it.

She made her chai. She took out the Samsung. She pressed play.

Reeenaaaaa.

The sound hit her differently that morning. Not softer, not louder. Differently. As though she had been hearing it in one language for fourteen months and this morning she suddenly understood what language it actually was.

Her mother had not been a demonstrative woman. She did not say I love you the way people say it in films. What she said was: come eat. What she said was: wear a jacket. What she said was: I made that dal you like. She said love through the specific and the ordinary, through tamarind and phone calls about plumbers and forty-three seconds on a Tuesday when she had simply rung her daughter to say: I was thinking of you and I made you something and there is no rush.

There is no rush.

Reena sat with the phone in her hand after the recording ended and she opened the settings menu and found the delete option and put her thumb above it.

She stayed that way for a while. Long enough for the chai to go cool.

Then she closed the menu and put the Samsung back in the drawer.

She was not ready. And also — she understood this now, clearly, in the grey February morning light — she might never be ready, and that was all right. The voicemail was not holding her back from grief. It was not a trap or a wound she was keeping open. It was simply the dal in the box. It was simply her mother saying: I was thinking of you.

She could pick it up whenever.

No rush.
