Notice Period

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

Priya hands in her notice after four years and her founder must decide whether keeping her is the right thing.

Chapter 1: The Letter

The resignation letter was a Google Doc, shared with view access, which was somehow both very modern and very sad. Vikram read it on his laptop in the small glass-walled room they used for calls, while Priya sat across from him with the particular stillness of someone who has already rehearsed this moment several times.

It was a good letter. It said the right things without being dishonest. Four years of growth, immense learning, time for a new challenge. She thanked him by name. She thanked the team by name. There was no mention of what had actually happened, which was the considerate version and also the kind that makes the receiver feel worse.

Vikram had known this was coming for about six weeks. You didn't run a twelve-person company without learning to read the signs—the slight withdrawal from optional meetings, the careful boundaries around new projects, the way someone starts completing tasks with the thoroughness of someone tying up loose ends. He had told himself it might be something else. People had off months. Life happened outside offices.

But Priya was their head of operations. She had built the vendor relationships, the internal processes, the onboarding system that had taken two years to get right. She had been employee number three, before they even had a proper office, back when the team met at a café in Koramangala because it was the only place that had reliable Wi-Fi. She knew where every body was buried, which was a metaphor but also nearly literal—she had been present for every significant mistake the company had ever made.

"Thank you for sending this over," Vikram said.

"I thought it was better than just saying it in a meeting," she said.

"It is. It's a good letter." He closed the laptop. "Where are you going?"

A pause. "A Series B in Hyderabad. Operations lead. It's a step up."

"Of course it is." He meant this. He said it in a way that he hoped communicated that he meant it. "Are you happy with the package they've offered?"

She looked at him carefully. "Yes."

"Can I ask what it is? Not to counter. I just want to understand where we are as a company."

She told him. It was forty percent more than she was making now. The equity was decent. There was a relocation allowance.

Vikram nodded. He didn't say anything for a moment. He was thinking about the appraisal cycle from eight months ago, when he had approved a twelve percent raise for Priya and told himself it was competitive. He had known then, at some corner of himself, that twelve percent was not competitive. He had approved it anyway because the company was between funding rounds and he had been afraid of what the numbers would look like if he paid her what she was worth.

She hadn't complained. She had said thank you and gone back to her desk.

Chapter 2: The Counter

There was a counter-offer available. Vikram knew what it looked like before he'd even finished thinking about it. He could match the Hyderabad number, or get close. He could offer a title change—VP of Operations had been a conversation they'd skirted around for a year. He could talk about the equity refresher they'd been discussing informally, make it concrete.

He could keep her.

He thought about what keeping her would actually mean. She would stay—for a while, maybe for longer, people were loyal and Priya had always been loyal. But she would stay knowing that he had let eight months pass paying her below what she deserved, and that it had taken her handing in a resignation letter for him to find the money. She would know that the company valued her at roughly the minimum required to retain her, not at what she was actually worth.

And eventually—maybe in a year, maybe in two—she would leave again. Because the next appraisal cycle would come and he would feel the same cash pressure and make the same calculation. Or she would feel it happening and leave before it did. Either way, they would have this same conversation in a different year, and it would be worse the second time.

"Can I be honest with you?" Vikram said.

"Please," Priya said, and he caught a small flicker of surprise in her face—she had been expecting the counter-offer conversation.

"I should have paid you more eighteen months ago. Probably two years ago. I kept telling myself that the market rate was what I was paying you, and I knew it wasn't. I was managing cash and I was also taking advantage of the fact that you're not the kind of person who makes demands."

She looked at him steadily.

"I could make you a counter-offer now," he continued. "I've been sitting here building it in my head. Title, money, equity. It would be real—I can actually do it. But I think if I offer it now, you should take the other job anyway."

She blinked. "Why?"

"Because you've been ready for this for at least a year. I've been—I don't know. Comfortable. Having you here and not having to think about it. A counter-offer now would be me asking you to stay for my convenience, not for yours."

The room was quiet. Through the glass wall, Aryan from product was explaining something to a screen with excessive hand gestures. The air conditioning made its low background hiss.

"That's a very honest thing to say," Priya said finally.

"I figured I owed you at least that much."

"It doesn't make me feel better about leaving."

"I don't think it was supposed to." He paused. "Will you tell me what broke for you? Not for a counter-offer. Just so I understand what I actually need to fix for the next person."

She looked at the table for a moment. Then she started talking.

Chapter 3: What She Said

She said three things, and all of them were correct.

The first was the money, which they had already covered. The second was that for the past year she had been doing a VP's job with a senior manager's title, and the distance between the two wasn't just about compensation—it was about being in the room for decisions that she was then expected to execute, but having no actual say in whether those decisions were the right ones. She had flagged the new vendor contract as under-negotiated. The contract had gone through anyway. She had raised concerns about the team structure during the last hiring sprint. The sprint had happened anyway. It wasn't that she needed to win every argument. It was that she needed to have the argument.

The third was the hardest to say and the hardest to hear. She said that after four years, she had started to feel like a feature of the company rather than a person in it. That Vikram trusted her completely as a functional operator and almost not at all as a strategic thinker. That when she walked into a room with a vendor, she was effective; when she walked into a room with Vikram and a potential investor, she was invisible.

She said this carefully, without anger, which made it more painful to receive.

Vikram took notes. He didn't defend himself. When she finished, he read back what he'd written to confirm he'd understood, which was a thing he never did in any other meeting because he prided himself on his memory, but felt necessary now.

He put in the call with HR that afternoon to start the handover planning. Priya said she'd give sixty days instead of the standard thirty, which was generous and which he accepted because the work required it, though he recognised the generosity as a small indictment of how complicated the transition would be.

Her last day fell on a Friday in November. The team ordered food from the place they'd all been going to for three years—biryani, the usual—and someone made a WhatsApp collage of photos from the Koramangala café days. Priya laughed at the photos. She looked happy and sad in the way people do when they are leaving something real.

Vikram gave a short speech. He said she had built half the company and that the other half wouldn't have happened without her. He said it to the room but he was saying it to her, and she knew it.

He did not say that he should have paid her more, or given her the title, or put her in those investor rooms. The moment for saying those things had been a year ago, and then four months ago, and then during the conversation in the glass room. He had said it then, which was something. Whether it was enough he genuinely didn't know.

She sent him a message from Hyderabad six weeks later. She had started the new role. It was harder than expected and she was learning a lot. The team was good.

He wrote back: glad to hear it. He meant it. He also called HR the next morning to run a full compensation audit against market benchmarks. It was late, as most necessary things were. He did it anyway.
