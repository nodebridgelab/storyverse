Grandpa Learns to Text

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

In Kochi, a retired schoolteacher decides to join his family's group chat and discovers that sending a message is harder than teaching algebra. His grandchildren become his reluctant teachers.

Chapter 1: A Phone Too Smart

Retired headmaster Varghese Thomas had survived forty years of schoolchildren, three monsoon floods and one very strict wife, but nothing had prepared him for the smartphone his daughter gave him on his seventieth birthday.

"It is like a small television that argues with you," he told his neighbour over the fence in Kochi.

His family lived in four different cities and had a group chat, which he had learned about only by accident when his daughter said, "Appa, we posted it in the group." He had gone to the garden and sat among the curry leaf plants, offended. He had a family that had a group and he was not in it.

So, the next Sunday, his grandson Jerin, aged twelve, came to teach him. They sat on the veranda with coconut water and a plate of banana chips.

"Thatha, you press this green thing."

"Which green thing?"

"The green thing. The one that looks like a telephone."

"Everything looks like a telephone on a telephone," said Varghese.

Forty minutes later he had learned to open the chat, to type with one finger, and that the little face with tears of joy did not actually mean crying. He had also learned that the phone corrected his words into other words, which was the beginning of all his troubles.

Chapter 2: Messages Gone Wrong

His first message to the family was meant to read: "Good morning all. God bless. Rain expected today."

What arrived, thanks to the phone's generous imagination, was: "Good mourning all. God bless. Rain exploded today."

Within minutes, his daughter in Bengaluru had replied with seven question marks. His son in Dubai wrote, "Appa, is everything all right?" His niece in Kottayam sent a prayer. And a cousin he had not spoken to since 2009 wrote, "Heard about the explosion, are you safe?"

Varghese read all of this on the veranda with growing alarm. He typed, slowly, one finger at a time: "No explosion. Only rain. Phone is foolish."

The phone, helpfully, sent: "No explosion. Only brain. Phone is foolish."

For the rest of the week, Varghese's messages became a kind of poetry. He wished his sister a happy "birthing." He asked his son whether he had eaten "dinosaur." He told the group that the vegetable seller had raised the price of "tomatoes" to a hundred and forty rupees, but the phone made it "tornadoes," and the family spent a whole evening worried about the weather in Kerala.

Each time, Jerin would call, laughing so hard he could barely speak. "Thatha, please stop, Mummy is crying."

"Is she sad?"

"No! She says she has never loved a group chat so much."

Chapter 3: The Best Message of All

That Friday, Varghese made a decision. He sat Jerin down again and said, "I do not want the phone to be clever for me. I want to write my own words, right or wrong."

"Thatha, you can turn that off."

"Then show me."

They turned it off. Now Varghese's messages arrived exactly as he made them, with every spelling wobble and every missing space, and strangely, they read better. They sounded like him. Slow, careful, and slightly formal, like a man writing a letter on a good day.

One evening, as rain drummed on the roof, he typed a message he had been thinking about for a long time. It took him twenty minutes.

"My dear children and grandchildren. I am an old man and I am new to this. But I want to say that I am glad to be in your group. When you were all small, I read to you at night. Now you read to me, in small words on a small screen, and I like it even better. Please keep writing to me. I read everything twice."

He pressed send. Then he sat on the veranda, listening to the rain, afraid he had done something silly.

The phone buzzed. And buzzed again. In Bengaluru, Dubai, Kottayam and a dozen other places, people were typing at once. His daughter wrote a long paragraph with a heart. His son sent a photograph of himself wiping his eyes at his desk. Jerin sent a single line: "Thatha, you are the best texter in the family."

Varghese smiled, found the little face with tears of joy, and sent it back, this time knowing exactly what it meant.
