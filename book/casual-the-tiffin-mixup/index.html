The Tiffin Mix-Up

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

In Mumbai, a dabbawala delivers the wrong lunch to the wrong office, and an accountant and a young chef accidentally begin a month-long lunchtime friendship through notes inside empty boxes.

Chapter 1: Wrong Box, Right Day

Sandeep Gaikwad had carried tiffin boxes across Mumbai for nineteen years and had never once made a mistake. The famous system of colour codes and marks that moved two hundred thousand lunches a day was, in his hands, as natural as breathing.

So on a rainy Thursday, when he delivered a steel tiffin to the sixth floor of an accounting firm at Fort and an hour later discovered that it belonged to a hotel kitchen in Colaba, he stood in the rain for a full minute, feeling like a bird that had flown the wrong way.

The mistake had a name, a small one, because a smudge of rain had turned a chalk mark from a seven into a one. Somewhere in Colaba, a young chef named Nilofer, who ate her own cooking only on holidays, had received instead the lunch of a fussy accountant: dal, two rotis, and a pickle so sour it brought a tear.

Sandeep could not fix it in time. He apologised to both ends, sheepishly, and hoped that was the last of it.

It was not.

The next morning, a small piece of paper was tucked inside the returned empty box of the accountant. It read, in neat handwriting: "Thank you for yesterday's lunch. The dal was honest. The pickle was an assault. Next time, less lemon."

Chapter 2: Notes in the Empty Box

The accountant, Pradeep Joglekar, was fifty-three, careful, and not accustomed to being teased by strangers. He looked at the note for a long time. Then he took a pen and wrote on the back: "Madam, my wife makes that pickle. She does not allow criticism. But the lemon will be reduced."

The note went into the empty box, which went back down with Sandeep.

It became a habit. Sandeep would carry the note from one end to the other, an unwilling postman inside his own system. On Monday, Nilofer wrote that she had left a little chocolate cake in her own box for the accountant, as an apology for her insults. Pradeep wrote back that cake was against his doctor's advice, but he had eaten it in the stairwell. On Wednesday, she wrote about a customer who had asked for a "less spicy biryani, but with more flavour," and he replied that he had a client exactly like that.

Neither of them had met. Neither knew the other's face. By the second week, Sandeep, who could read small handwriting perfectly well and was not supposed to, was waiting for the answers as eagerly as they were.

"You two should meet," he finally said to Pradeep one afternoon.

"Never," said Pradeep, blushing. "It would ruin everything."

Chapter 3: The Last Note

On the twenty-eighth day, the note in the box said only: "I am leaving the hotel. Chef in a new restaurant, Pune. The first lunch there is on the house, if you ever come."

Pradeep read it in the pantry. He read it again. Then, for the first time in nine years, he left his desk at half past twelve and walked out into the rain without an umbrella.

He found Sandeep on a bicycle under the Fort arches, tying down a stack of boxes with rope. "Which way to Colaba?" said Pradeep.

"Sir, what are you doing?"

"I am going to taste something with a little less lemon."

Sandeep smiled, wiped the rain off his face, and put a hand on the accountant's shoulder. "Sir, I am a dabbawala. I do not tell people where to go. I only deliver them."

He pointed the way. By two o'clock Pradeep was standing in a kitchen doorway, soaked, holding a steel box, facing a young woman in a white coat who looked at him and said, "You are shorter than your handwriting."

"You are louder than yours."

They had lunch together, sitting on flour sacks. The dal was honest, and the pickle, this time, was perfect.
