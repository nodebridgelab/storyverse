The Empty Swing

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

In a Guwahati courtyard, Bhaskar keeps a rope swing that his estranged brother built them as boys. When word of his brother's death arrives, he must decide what to do with twenty years of stubborn silence.

Chapter 1: Brothers

The swing hung from the old jackfruit tree in the courtyard of the family house in Uzanbazar, and for twenty years Bhaskar had refused to look at it.

His younger brother, Dhiraj, had built it when they were boys, nine and six, out of a plank and a coil of jute rope stolen from the neighbour's cowshed. They had fought over turns on it every summer afternoon. Later, they had fought over the land, over their father's will, over the money Dhiraj had borrowed and never repaid. The last thing Bhaskar had said to him, at a funeral for their father, was: You are no brother of mine.

He had meant it only as a weapon. But weapons, once thrown, do not return to the hand.

In the years after, he heard fragments through relatives. Dhiraj had left for Cachar. Dhiraj had married, then lost his wife. Each time Bhaskar told himself it was none of his concern, and each time he lay awake afterward counting the rafters.

Now a phone call came on a Tuesday evening in October, from a woman in Silchar he had never met. Dhiraj had died in his sleep, she said. A heart attack. He had been working as a bus conductor, and he had a small room, a daughter of seventeen, and a photograph of two boys on a swing. The woman did not accuse him of anything. She only said, "He kept your number. He never called it. I think he was afraid."

Chapter 2: The Road to Silchar

Bhaskar took the night bus the next morning, though his knees complained. Seven hours of hills and checkpoints and chai stops, the Barak valley sliding by green and indifferent.

At a roadside stall near Lumding he bought tea he did not drink, and held the warm glass just to have something to hold. He rehearsed what he would say and found he had no sentences, only the image of a small boy with scabbed knees shouting, Push higher, Dada, higher!

The room was in a lane behind the market. It was clean and bare. On the wall hung the photograph, creased from handling. Beside it, a girl stood with her arms crossed, defending her father's memory against a stranger. Her name was Mitali. She had Dhiraj's eyebrows, the same stubborn slant.

"You are the uncle," she said. It was not a greeting.

"I am." His voice cracked. "I am sorry. I am so sorry I was not here."

She studied him for a long moment, then uncrossed her arms. "He talked about you every Bihu. He said you were the only one who could make the sweet pitha properly. He said you would call when you were ready, and then he said maybe he was not worth being ready for."

"Did he suffer?" Bhaskar asked.

"No. He had been laughing at a joke on the bus, the driver said. Then he sat down and was gone." She softened a little. "He would have liked that you asked."

Bhaskar sat down on the narrow cot because he could not stand. He wept as a man of sixty-three weeps, without sound, shoulders shaking.

Chapter 3: Higher

They brought Dhiraj's ashes to Guwahati in a small brass pot, Bhaskar carrying it on his lap the whole way as though it might spill.

The jackfruit tree was heavy with fruit. The swing hung still in the evening light, the rope grey and the plank worn smooth. Mitali stood looking at it. "He told me about this," she said. "He said it was the best thing he ever made."

"It was," said Bhaskar. "It was better than anything I made."

He sat on the plank, which creaked, and he did not mind. He felt Mitali hesitate, then step behind him. Her hands settled on the rope.

"Higher?" she asked, and her voice shook between laughter and tears.

"Higher," Bhaskar said.

She pushed. The swing rose and fell through the gold air, and the old man closed his eyes and for a few seconds was nine years old, with a brother's laughter at his back. Nothing was mended. Twenty years could not be handed back. But there was a girl in the courtyard with his brother's eyebrows, and a house with room for her, and he would not let the swing hang empty again.
