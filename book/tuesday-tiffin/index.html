The Tuesday Tiffin Club

by Zoya Ansari · Casual · 3 chapters · approx. 10–12 minutes

Five strangers on the fourth floor of a Pune software office swap lunch boxes every Tuesday. By the third week they are swapping secrets too.

Read nowOpen in the app🇮🇳 हिंदी में पढ़ें

About this story

The Tuesday Tiffin Club is a warm, funny story about how small rituals create unexpected community. It is set in the kind of open-plan office most of us know — fluorescent lights, too-loud keyboards, colleagues who have worked three desks apart for two years without learning each other's names.

The story moves quickly, with a lightness of touch that feels closer to a good lunch conversation than a formal narrative. Its central theme is generosity — not the grand kind, but the quiet, food-shaped kind that shows up when someone cooks a little extra and sets it in the middle of a table.

Readers who enjoy stories about workplace friendships, found community, and the small ceremonies of Indian office life will feel right at home here.

Meet the characters

  • Rishi — the inventor of the tiffin-swap rule, whose real motivation is escaping his mother's lauki sabzi. Good-hearted and slightly chaotic.
  • Priya — from accounts, cautious at first, the one who notices things others miss. Her reaction to the coconut curry tiffin is the story's emotional turning point.
  • Kabir — from security, unexpectedly dignified about a tuna sandwich, and the first to say out loud what everyone is thinking about Sulochana-ji.
  • Sulochana-ji — the cleaner from Floor 3, whose mystery tiffin is the heart of the story. She cooks for a husband no longer there and feeds strangers instead.

Reading time & format

3 chapters · approx. 10–12 minutes total · best read in one sitting. Each chapter is short enough to finish in a tea break. The story has a natural arc from setup to surprise to warmth, so reading all three together gives the full effect.

Why we published it

We published this story because it does something rare: it finds real feeling inside a setting — the Indian corporate office — that fiction usually treats as either a backdrop for ambition or a target for satire. The Tuesday Tiffin Club treats it as a neighbourhood. The people in it are ordinary, the stakes are small, and somehow it matters enormously that there is a chair pulled up at the end of the table.

Chapter 1: The Rule of Swapping

The rule was Rishi's, and he had invented it purely to escape his mother's lauki.

Every Tuesday, anyone with a tiffin could drop it into the middle of the pantry table, and anyone else could take a different one. No complaints, no returns, no asking who cooked what. By 1 p.m. the table looked like a small, delicious casino.

Priya from accounts drew a tiffin that smelled of coconut and curry leaves and nearly cried at her desk. Kabir from security drew her tuna sandwich and said, with great dignity, that he had never been so confused or so grateful.

Think about it

  1. Why do you think Rishi frames his rule as being about variety rather than about escaping his mother's cooking? What does this say about how he handles awkward truths?
  2. The pantry table is described as "a small, delicious casino." What is the story saying about risk and reward with this image?

Chapter 2: The Mystery Dabba

In week three, a tiffin appeared that nobody owned. Steel, three layers, tied with a faded green ribbon.

Top layer: jeera rice. Middle: dal so good it made two interns stop arguing about Kubernetes. Bottom layer: a note in careful handwriting that read, You all look like you need feeding. Sincerely, the cleaner on Floor 3.

The pantry went quiet. Then Kabir said what everyone was thinking: nobody in the building had ever learned her name. By Tuesday next, there were six new tiffins on the table and one chair pulled up at the end, with a place set for Sulochana-ji.

Think about it

  1. The dal is so good it stops an argument about Kubernetes. What is the story doing by juxtaposing the food with the tech debate?
  2. "Nobody had ever learned her name." Why is this detail placed here, after three weeks of the tiffin club? What has the club been missing without knowing it?

Chapter 3: A Seat at the End

Sulochana-ji sat down at 1:03 p.m. with her hands folded as if the chair might bite.

'I only cook extra,' she said. 'My husband used to eat four rotis. Now I still make four.'

Nobody laughed, and nobody pitied her either, which Rishi later said was the real miracle. Priya passed her the pickle. Kabir asked for the dal recipe and wrote it on his palm because he had no paper.

The club never got a name. But from then on, the pantry table had seven chairs, and the cleaner of Floor 3 was never again the last to be thanked.

Think about it

  1. Rishi calls it "the real miracle" that nobody laughed and nobody pitied Sulochana-ji. Why is that specific response — neither laughter nor pity — so important?
  2. The club never gets an official name. Why do you think the story ends on that detail? What does it say about the kind of community this is?
  3. Kabir writes the recipe on his palm. What does this small act tell us about him, and what has changed between Chapter 1 and Chapter 3?
