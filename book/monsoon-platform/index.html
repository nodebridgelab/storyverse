Monsoon at Platform Nine

by Ananya Rao · Romance · 3 chapters · approx. 10–12 minutes

Two commuters, one delayed local train, and a shared umbrella that neither of them can afford to return.

Read nowOpen in the app

About this story

Monsoon at Platform Nine is a quiet love story set in Mumbai during the rains, in the particular suspended world of a delayed local train. It moves in the rhythm that Mumbai commuters know: the platform, the waiting, the accidental togetherness, and what happens when two people keep finding excuses to be in the same place at the same time.

The tone is light and observant, with a dry wit that never tips into silliness. What gives it warmth is its honesty — both Naina and Dev are adults who know what they are doing and find ways to be roundabout about it anyway, until they stop.

This story is for readers who enjoy slow-burn romance that is actually slow, where the tension comes not from obstacles but from the small, deliberate acts of two people choosing to move toward each other.

Meet the characters

  • Naina — a commuter who tells herself it is coincidence that she keeps leaving home five minutes earlier. Perceptive, self-aware, and unwilling to admit things before she is ready.
  • Dev — a maths teacher who carries a pocket notebook for unsolved problems and writes the most direct sentence in the story on a torn-out page. His laugh is described as loud, clumsy and impossible to dislike.

Reading time & format

3 chapters · approx. 10–12 minutes total · best read in one sitting or over two evenings. The story is paced to match the rhythm of a commute — Chapter 1 is a first meeting, Chapter 2 is the week that follows, Chapter 3 is the monsoon's last act. Reading it in one go lets the arc land cleanly.

Why we published it

We published Monsoon at Platform Nine because it uses the Mumbai local train — one of the most written-about settings in Indian fiction — and finds something new in it: not the crowd, not the city, but two people in the margins of the platform, shoulder to shoulder under a blue umbrella. The story's object — the umbrella — does the work of a whole relationship arc without ever becoming symbolic in a heavy-handed way. It stays an umbrella. That restraint is rare.

Chapter 1: The Shared Umbrella

The 6:42 to Dadar was cancelled, which was how Naina learned that the man with the blue umbrella had a laugh like a dropped steel plate: loud, clumsy and impossible to dislike.

'You can stand under it,' he said. 'It is a big umbrella and I am a small person.'

She was not small and he was not either, and the umbrella was not big. They stood with their shoulders almost touching while the rain made Mumbai look like a watercolour somebody had left on a windowsill.

Think about it

  1. Dev says "it is a big umbrella and I am a small person." Neither of these things is true. What is he actually doing with this sentence?
  2. The rain makes Mumbai look like "a watercolour left on a windowsill." Whose perspective is this — Naina's, Dev's, or the narrator's? Does it matter?

Chapter 2: Same Time, Platform Nine

She told herself it was coincidence that she left home five minutes earlier on Wednesday. She told herself the same thing on Thursday.

His name was Dev. He taught mathematics to children who did not want it, and he carried a pocket notebook where he wrote problems he could not solve. On Friday he handed her a page. It said: If two people keep missing the same train on purpose, how many Fridays until one of them says so?

Naina took the pen from his hand and wrote underneath: Today.

Think about it

  1. Dev frames the question as a maths problem. Why does the story give him this particular profession and this particular way of communicating?
  2. Naina's answer is a single word. After two chapters of her telling herself things are coincidence, what makes "Today" land so powerfully?

Chapter 3: The Return of the Umbrella

The umbrella was never returned. It sat in her hallway, dripping onto the floor, a blue accusation.

In August, the monsoon broke early and the trains stopped for six hours. They waited at a tea stall, with strangers, with bad chai, with the whole city holding its breath.

'Keep it,' Dev said, folding the umbrella into her hands. 'I think I'd rather keep walking you to the station.'

She laughed, a little too loudly, and realised with some alarm that she had started to sound like him.

Think about it

  1. The umbrella is called "a blue accusation" sitting in Naina's hallway. What is it accusing her of?
  2. Dev gives up the umbrella to keep the walks. What does this trade say about what he values?
  3. The story ends with Naina noticing she has started to sound like Dev. What kind of ending is this — hopeful, unsettling, or both?
