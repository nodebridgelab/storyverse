Chai for the Whole Lane

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

A Lucknow chai stall owner decides to raise his prices by one rupee and discovers that his customers have been running a quiet system of kindness that he never knew existed.

Chapter 1: One Rupee More

Hari Prasad had sold tea at the corner of Aminabad lane in Lucknow for twenty-two years, and for twenty-two years the price had crept up like a shy cat. Five rupees, then eight, then ten. Now milk was dearer, gas was dearer, and even the little clay cups cost more than before.

On a hot Monday morning he took a piece of chalk and wrote on the slate behind him: Chai, Rs. 11.

He stood there expecting rebellion. A man who raises prices by one rupee is like a man who tells the world he no longer loves it.

The first customer was Sharma ji, a retired railway clerk who came at seven every day with a folded newspaper under his arm. He read the slate, adjusted his glasses, read it again, and said nothing. He paid twelve rupees, took his cup, and walked off.

Hari stared at the extra rupee. Surely it was a mistake. Then the second customer, a tailor named Imran, did the same. And the third, a schoolteacher. Each paid one rupee more than the board said. Hari was puzzled, and a little frightened. People did not do this without a reason.

By noon he had a small steel bowl full of extra coins and no explanation at all.

Chapter 2: The Slate Behind the Slate

It was his nephew Golu, who helped on Sundays, who finally solved it.

"Chacha, you really don't know? Turn the board around."

Hari turned the slate. On the back, in faded chalk handwriting he had never noticed because he never turned the board, were names. Dozens of them. Beside each name were small tally marks.

"What is this?"

"It is the udhaar list," Golu said, rubbing his nose. "People who couldn't pay one day, and then paid later. Or never paid. Sharma ji started it, years ago, when you were sick with fever and Bhola's wife drank your tea for a month without money. He said the lane would cover it. Every day, someone pays a rupee extra, and that rupee goes to whoever can't."

Hari sat down on his upturned crate. For years he had given tea on credit to the rickshaw pullers when their pockets were empty, never writing it down, sometimes grumbling to himself, and he had always been strangely short in his accounts by nothing at all. The tally marks were not debts. They were all the cups the lane had quietly paid for.

"But who told them to do this?"

"Nobody. They just saw you were kind and decided to keep up."

The steel bowl suddenly seemed to weigh more.

He looked up the lane, at the tailor, the teacher, the clerk, all of them going about their business as if nothing at all was wrong with the world.

Chapter 3: Chai Is on the Lane

The next morning Hari rubbed out the number eleven and wrote ten again.

Sharma ji arrived at seven, newspaper in place, and stopped. "You changed your mind?"

"I did not change my mind," said Hari. "I changed the slate." He pulled out a clay cup, filled it with tea, and set it on the counter. "This is free. Today and every day for whoever's name is on the back of that board. And your rupee goes in the bowl, as before, but I will run the bowl properly now."

"Why?" asked Sharma ji, with a small smile, because he knew perfectly well.

"Because I am a tea seller and not a king," said Hari. "A king would not have known his own lane was feeding him."

News travelled, as news does in Lucknow. By the end of the week a baker named Zaheer began leaving a bag of rusks at the stall every morning. A girl from the tuition class two lanes over put a small drawing of a teacup on the slate with chalk of her own. Rickshaw pullers stopped looking at their feet when they came for tea.

Hari raised the price to eleven after all, three months later, on his own birthday, and nobody said a word. They paid twelve.

On the slate, near the bottom of the back, in a corner where only a person who truly looked would find it, he wrote one more name. It was his own. Beside it he drew a single tally mark, for the day a customer once paid for his fever.
