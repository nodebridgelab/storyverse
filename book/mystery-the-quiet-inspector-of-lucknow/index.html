The Quiet Inspector of Lucknow

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

At a grand wedding in an old Lucknow haveli, the bride's silver paandan turns out to be a fake. Inspector Rizvi, a man who says little and notices everything, has only the length of the baraat to find the swap.

Chapter 1: The Box That Did Not Sing

Inspector Aslam Rizvi had come to the wedding as a guest, which is to say he had come to eat, and he was halfway through his second kebab when the old lady's cry rose above the music.

"This is not my paandan!"

The music stopped. In the courtyard of the Naqvi haveli, under strings of marigolds, Begum Salma Naqvi sat on her carved chair holding a silver betel box on her lap. She was eighty-four, small as a sparrow, and at that moment she looked like a very angry one.

The box was to be given to the bride that evening, as it had been given to every Naqvi bride for five generations. Begum Salma had opened the lid to show her granddaughter Zoya the carved leaves inside, and the lid had opened without a sound.

"It sang," she said. "For seventy years that hinge has sung one note, like a bird. Somebody has taken my paandan and given me a stranger."

Everyone began speaking at once. Rizvi put down his plate. He was a lean man of fifty with a quiet voice, and in Lucknow he was known for being the only policeman who could make a room go silent by saying nothing. He walked across the courtyard, bowed to the Begum, and asked, "May I see the box, Begum sahiba?"

He weighed it in his hands. He sniffed the silver, ran a thumbnail over the hinge, and held the lid to the light. "A good copy," he said. "Heavy, and carefully carved. But new."

"Who touched it?" he asked the room.

Chapter 2: Four Pairs of Hands

The box had spent the last three days in the zenana sitting room, locked in a wooden almirah. There were four keys. Begum Salma kept one. Her son Shaukat, the groom's uncle by marriage, kept another. Zoya, the bride, had the third. The fourth was with the old housekeeper, Imtiaz Bi, who dusted the room each morning.

Rizvi spoke to each of them in turn, in a corner of the courtyard, over cups of kahwa he did not drink.

Imtiaz Bi wept at once and swore on her late husband that she had not so much as breathed on the almirah. Zoya laughed, a bit shakily, and said she would sooner give away her own wedding sari. Shaukat Naqvi, a stout man in a cream sherwani with a worried forehead, shook Rizvi's hand warmly and said he had not been near the room in a week.

Rizvi smiled. He did not press the point. But as he let go of Shaukat's hand, he looked at his own fingers and noticed a faint grey on the pads, and a sharp smell, like vinegar and metal.

"Silver polish," he murmured, almost to himself.

"What was that?" said Shaukat.

"Nothing. This courtyard is very clean. Even the pillars gleam." Rizvi studied the nearest one. "Tell me, Mr. Naqvi, when did the jeweller from Chowk last visit?"

Shaukat's smile slipped a quarter of an inch. "No jeweller has been here. Why do you ask?"

"Because a fake box takes weeks to make, and someone had to measure the real one first." Rizvi turned to the Begum. "Begum sahiba, when was the paandan last out of the almirah?"

"Two Fridays ago. For Eid. I polished it with my own hands, Inspector, with soft cloth only."

"Soft cloth," Rizvi repeated gently, and looked at Shaukat's right hand, which had suddenly vanished into his pocket.

Chapter 3: The Note of the Hinge

"There was a time," said Rizvi, sitting down beside the Begum, "when I was a young constable and learned that people who lie about small things usually do so to hide a larger fear. Mr. Naqvi, you have been polishing something this morning. Silver, I think. Polish stays under the nails for days, and it does not wash off like ordinary dirt."

Shaukat's face worked. "I polish my own cufflinks," he said unconvincingly.

"You wear none." Rizvi's voice stayed mild. "You also told me you had not touched anything in the zenana for a week. But the real paandan was taken between Eid and the wedding, and it was carried out of this house by someone with a key, who knew the box would not be opened until tonight. That is a long list. But only one person on it had a debt at the Chowk money lender, which the whole bazaar has been discussing since Monday."

The courtyard was perfectly still. Zoya had gone pale. The Begum watched her son with eyes that held no anger yet, only a terrible patience.

"I only meant to borrow it," Shaukat said at last, and sat heavily on the bench. "The dealer in Chowk gave me a loan against the silver and a month to repay. I had the copy made so nobody would know, and then I would take the box back after the wedding. Amma, I would never have sold it. I swear it."

"You have not told me the dealer's name," said Rizvi.

"Hakim Rafiq's shop. Behind the cloth market."

Rizvi nodded to the constable at the gate, and within the hour the real paandan was brought back, wrapped in a faded cloth. Begum Salma opened it herself. The lid lifted, and a single clear note rose from the hinge, like a bird greeting the morning.

She did not speak to her son that evening. But at the end of the ceremony, when the new bride sat before her, she placed the paandan in Zoya's hands, and asked Rizvi, quietly, to stay for dinner.
