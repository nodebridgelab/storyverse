The Retainer

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

A freelance consultant calculates exactly how much her biggest client costs her beyond what they pay.

Chapter 1: The Math

On the first of every month, Kavitha received a payment notification from Horizon Analytics that read 'Retainer – INR 1,85,000' and felt a complicated mix of relief and something she had not named for a long time.

She was an independent strategy and research consultant, five years out of a corporate job, working from the second bedroom of her Pune apartment with a client base of seven companies of which Horizon was by far the largest. The retainer represented forty percent of her monthly revenue. It had allowed her to take on smaller, more interesting clients. It had, in the first two years of the relationship, been straightforward: a monthly deliverable, occasional ad-hoc projects, a point of contact who was professional and responded to emails within twenty-four hours.

Then the point of contact had changed.

Karan Mehta had joined Horizon as VP Strategy fourteen months ago, and the relationship had transformed in ways that were difficult to articulate in any single conversation but were unmistakable in aggregate. Karan communicated primarily by phone—which was fine—but called at hours that were increasingly not nine-to-six. He sent 'quick questions' on Sundays that were not quick. He reviewed deliverables with a level of detail that would have been appropriate for someone paying twice the retainer and then revised them again after she had incorporated his feedback, a cycle she had been through four times in the past two months.

He was not unreasonable in any individual interaction. He was genuinely smart and the work he was asking her to do was interesting. He was just always there, at the edge of her working life, and the weight of that presence had expanded to fill spaces she hadn't offered him.

Kavitha had spent the October long weekend—which she had planned as her first fully-disconnected weekend in three months—doing a Horizon deliverable because Karan had marked it urgent on Thursday at six in the evening, which meant she had given up her own time for a project that the invoice did not account for.

After that weekend she sat down and did the math.

In October she had worked sixty-two billable hours for clients other than Horizon. She had worked forty-one hours on Horizon deliverables. She had spent, by her honest estimate, another eighteen hours on Horizon-related communication—the Sunday calls, the revision cycles, the email threads that went six rounds when two would have been sufficient. That was fifty-nine hours for one-eighty-five thousand, which worked out to approximately thirty-one hundred rupees per hour. Her standard rate for other clients was four thousand five hundred.

She stared at the spreadsheet for a while. Then she made a list of what else the numbers were telling her.

Chapter 2: The List

The list had four items.

One: She had declined two project enquiries in the past quarter because she had not been confident of her bandwidth. One of them had been from a company she genuinely wanted to work with—an early-stage climate-tech start-up that had come via a referral and had offered a project scope that would have been genuinely interesting. She had said no because she was worried about Horizon's demands during that period.

Two: She had not taken a proper holiday in seven months. She had taken three long weekends, all of which had been partially colonised by Horizon work. Her parents had asked twice about a visit to Chennai and she had said 'next month' both times.

Three: Her work for other clients had deteriorated in the past two months, not catastrophically but perceptibly—a deliverable for a mid-sized pharma company that had come back with more revision notes than usual, a presentation for a fintech client that she had felt good about at submission and bad about a week later when she had the time to review it. She was doing less careful work for clients who were less demanding because the demanding client was consuming her best attention.

Four: She was not enjoying the Horizon work anymore. She had enjoyed it for two years. The shift was not about Horizon itself—the problems were still interesting—but about Karan's management of the relationship, which had turned something she'd valued into a source of low-level dread.

She closed the spreadsheet. She made tea. She sat with the list for a few days, because the right response to a clear analysis was not always the first one that presented itself.

The first response was to raise her rate. Double the retainer, effectively penalise the overuse of her time through price. This was technically correct and she had seen it recommended in every freelancer guide she'd ever read. She also knew it wouldn't work, because Karan would pay the higher rate and continue the same behaviours, and she would have more money and the same problem.

The second response was to have a direct conversation about scope and communication norms. Also technically correct. She had had this conversation once, in month eight, and it had produced three weeks of improved boundaries and then a gradual regression as the pace of Horizon's internal work increased and Karan's defaults reasserted themselves.

The third response was to not renew the retainer.

She kept coming back to the third response, which was what told her it was the right one.

Chapter 3: The Call

She called Karan on a Wednesday morning, which was the time she knew he was at his desk before the day became complicated.

"I wanted to have a conversation about the retainer renewal," she said. The contract came up for annual renewal in six weeks. "I've been thinking carefully about it and I've decided not to renew."

Silence. Then: "Can I ask why?"

She had prepared for this question. Not in the sense of a script—she didn't work that way—but she had spent time being honest with herself about what was true so that she could say it truthfully.

"The scope of the engagement has expanded significantly in the past year," she said. "I'm doing substantially more hours than the retainer compensates for, and the communication pattern has started to affect the quality of work I'm doing for other clients. I should have flagged this as a conversation sooner and I didn't, which is my responsibility. But at this point I don't think a scope adjustment would fix it—I think the working dynamic has evolved in a direction that isn't sustainable for me."

Another silence.

"Is this about the Sunday calls?" he said.

"It's about a pattern that includes the Sunday calls," she said. "It's also about the revision cycles, and the timeline pressure, and the bandwidth I've been declining to use for other work. I want to be honest with you because you deserve honesty, not because I want to relitigate the past year."

Karan was quiet for a moment. She could hear his office—keyboard clicking somewhere in the background, the low murmur of an open office.

"I didn't know you felt that way," he said.

"I know. I should have said it earlier."

"Would it help to—I could change—"

"Karan." She said it gently. "I've thought about this for several weeks. I'm not calling to negotiate. I'm calling because I want to give you as much notice as possible and to make the transition smooth. I'll complete everything currently in scope before the end of the contract. I'll document the work thoroughly so whoever takes over has full context."

He accepted this. Not happily—there was an edge to the conversation for its remaining minutes—but professionally. He said he'd let her know about the transition plan. She said she'd send a summary of all current work states by end of week.

When she hung up, she felt something she identified, after a moment, as relief. Not the complicated kind she felt on the first of the month. A cleaner kind.

She emailed the climate-tech start-up the same afternoon. The project was still available. The scope was smaller than it had been when she'd declined it, but the work sounded exactly as interesting as it had the first time.

She took on the project. She also booked the Chennai flights.

The math, in November, looked different. Forty percent less total revenue, every hour actually paid for. She sat with the numbers and found she didn't feel worse.
