The Tea Auction Theft

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

Three sample tins of a record-priced Assam tea disappear from a sealed vault in Guwahati hours before the auction. Valuer Nirmala Bora insists there was no theft at all, only a puzzle of weights and tins.

Chapter 1: Eleven Grams Short

At seven o'clock on the morning of the big auction, Nirmala Bora walked into the vault at the Guwahati Tea Centre and knew at once that something was wrong, because the room smelled of too little.

Tea has a smell, and a vault holding the spring flush from Dikom estate should have been drowning in malt and honey. Instead it was thin. She counted the sample tins on the steel shelf. Twelve. There should have been fifteen.

"Three tins," said the security guard, Hemanta, white as paper. "Madam, the door was sealed. The seal was intact. I have signed the log every hour."

Nirmala was a valuer of thirty years' experience and had been chosen to certify the Dikom lot, a rare golden-tip tea expected to fetch more per kilo than a decent motorcycle. The auction would open at eleven. Three tins missing meant a failed certification, an angry estate manager and a lot withdrawn.

Mr. Chaudhury, the auction director, arrived puffing and demanded a police case. Nirmala asked for one hour first. "Nothing was forced," she said. "No dust on the shelf, no footprints on the clean floor. If this is a theft, it is a very tidy one."

She asked for the weighing log. Every tin that entered the vault was weighed on a scale checked by two clerks. The three missing tins were each marked at five hundred grams. She looked at the twelve left and lifted one, then another, feeling in her wrist what a decade at the job had taught her.

The ninth tin felt wrong. She put it on the scale. Four hundred and eighty-nine grams.

"Eleven short," she said slowly, "and sealed."

Chapter 2: The Wrong Kind of Quiet

It took Nirmala twenty minutes to open the tin without breaking the seal, and when she did she wanted to laugh and to scold someone at once. The tea inside was not golden-tip. It was ordinary ruddy leaf, bright and bitter, good for breakfast and nothing else.

"Someone switched the contents," Chaudhury said, aghast. "But the seal!"

"The seal is on the lid," said Nirmala. "They never opened the lid. They swapped whole tins, three from the middle shelf for three that look identical, and only the ones on the end were disturbed." She tapped the shelf. "And the three real tins were not taken out of this building. The guard's log shows the vault door opened for nothing but weighing, and the weighing scale is in the next room."

A gleam crept into her eye. "Hemanta, who weighed the tins yesterday evening?"

"The two clerks, madam. Dipankar and Rupjyoti. And the new boy, Kunal, carried them."

They found the clerks in the break room. Dipankar was a grey, tired man who swore he had checked every weight twice. Rupjyoti, who was younger and sleeker, said he had merely watched. Kunal, nineteen and shivering, said nothing at all.

"I want to see the cart," said Nirmala.

The little steel trolley used to move tins between the weighing room and the vault stood in a corridor. She ran her hand along its underside. Tucked into a canvas pocket sewn beneath the lower tray, so neatly that it might have been an original feature, was a folded paper bag, and in the bag lay a trace of fine golden leaf.

Chapter 3: The Heist That Wasn't

"Three tins," said Nirmala, setting the bag on the table. "Smuggled out of the vault tray by tray, beneath the trolley. A bloodless heist. A clean one. But I do not think it was for money."

Chaudhury frowned. "Then what?"

"Look at the weights, sir. The thief took the real tins and replaced them with ordinary tea in matching tins, filled to within eleven grams of the correct weight. That is the kind of care you take if you need the lot to pass inspection, not vanish. Whoever did this wanted the Dikom lot to be certified."

She turned slowly. "You, Rupjyoti. You need the certified auction to close at a high price, because the estate manager has promised you a commission on every kilo, and a bad batch would bring that down. You swapped three tins of true golden-tip for the ordinary kind, intending to bring the originals back to the auction table yourself, claiming you had found them safe."

His face drained. "How did you know?"

"Because the one thing you could not fix was the smell. You switched the tea, but you did not think about the vault. The air itself has a memory." Nirmala smiled grimly. "And because the canvas pocket is stitched with orange thread, the same thread I saw on your cuff this morning."

Rupjyoti said nothing. Kunal, the trembling boy, finally spoke. "He told me it was a game, sir. He said the tins were only to be moved, never stolen."

They found the real tins in the clerk's own locker, wrapped in a shawl. The auction opened at noon, an hour late, and the Dikom lot sold at a record price while Rupjyoti was quietly led away to explain himself to the board. Nirmala drank a cup of the golden-tip afterward, slowly, and decided the vault smelled exactly right.
