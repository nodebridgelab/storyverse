The Unsent Letters

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

After her mother's funeral in Lucknow, Zareena finds a shoebox of letters addressed to her, none of them ever posted. Reading them slowly, she learns what silence had been hiding.

Chapter 1: The Shoebox

The funeral was over by noon, and by evening the house in Aminabad had gone quiet in the way houses do when the person who held them together has gone. Zareena's cousins had left with their casseroles and their careful sentences. Her father sat on the verandah with the radio off, looking at the neem tree as though it owed him an answer.

She went upstairs to sort the wardrobe because she could not bear to sit. Behind the folded dupattas, wrapped in a faded green shawl, she found a shoebox tied with kitchen string. On the lid, in her mother's slanting Urdu-inflected English, was written: For Zari, when she is ready.

Nobody had called her Zari in eleven years. Not since the argument about Karachi, about the man she had wanted to marry, about the things her mother had said at the gate while the autorickshaw waited with its meter running. Zareena had gone anyway. The marriage had lasted three years. The silence with her mother had lasted far longer, softened only by birthday calls that both of them kept short, as if brevity were a kind of safety.

She had built a life in the years between, in Delhi, in a rented flat with a balcony of struggling tulsi plants. She told herself that a person could grow up and stop needing a mother's approval. It was only at night, when a certain cooking smell drifted up from a neighbour's kitchen, that she knew how well she had lied.

She sat on the bed and untied the string. Inside were envelopes, perhaps forty, each stamped but never posted. Each carried a date. The oldest was from the winter after she left.

Chapter 2: What Her Mother Wrote

She read them in order, by the lamp, with the ceiling fan turning slowly overhead.

The first letter was angry, and Zareena was almost glad of it. Her mother wrote about pride and shame and what the neighbours would say, and then, halfway down the page, the handwriting changed. She wrote that she had stood in Zari's empty room and found a hairclip under the bed, and that she had not been able to move it for a month.

The later letters grew gentler, as rivers do once they leave the hills. There was one about the mangoes that year, which were sweet enough to make her weep.

There was one about a pressure cooker whistle she could no longer hear without turning to say something to a girl who was not there. One about a wedding she attended and left early because a girl in a yellow dress laughed exactly like Zari. One about the day she learned of the divorce, in which she wrote only: I wanted to come. I did not know how to be a person who comes.

Near the bottom was a letter dated three months ago. It was short. She wrote that her hands had begun to shake, that she was not afraid, only sorry for the years. She wrote: I kept waiting for you to apologise and for me to apologise, like two people waiting for a train on opposite platforms, and the train never arrived because it was never going to.

Zareena put the page down on her knee and cried until her breath came ragged, the way it had not at the funeral, where everyone was watching.

Chapter 3: The Verandah

Near midnight she carried the shoebox downstairs. Her father was still on the verandah, a shawl over his shoulders.

"You found them," he said, without turning.

"You knew?"

"She wrote them at the kitchen table when she thought I was asleep. I never read one. She would not have forgiven me." He gave a small dry laugh. "She was proud, your mother. Prouder than both of us put together."

Zareena sat on the step beside his chair. The neem tree moved a little in the dark. "I should have come home sooner. Even once."

"Yes," her father said, because he had never lied to her. "And she should have posted a single letter. You were both wrong, beta. And you both loved each other so much it filled this house, and neither of you could find the door to say it."

"Did she say she forgave me?" Zareena asked.

"She said it forty times. On forty envelopes."

She leaned her head against his knee. He rested his hand on her hair, awkwardly, like a man relearning a language.

In the morning she took the last, unwritten page from the box, a clean sheet her mother had folded in with the rest, and she wrote back. It would not be posted either. She would read it aloud at the grave on Friday, under the neem's cousin that grew by the wall, and then she would begin visiting her father every month, not out of duty but because the door, at last, was open.
