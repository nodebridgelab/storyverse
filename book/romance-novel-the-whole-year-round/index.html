The Whole Year Round

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 11 chapters · Completed

Ria and Kabir meet at a New Year's party and spend the next twelve months almost-but-not-quite finding each other again.

Chapter 1: December Thirty-First

The party was on the roof of Nisha's building in Bandra, which was the kind of party where everyone knew most people and no one knew everyone, which created the agreeable social pressure of having to introduce yourself to strangers. Ria had been doing this for forty minutes with the focused cheerfulness of someone at the outer edge of their extroversion when she found herself standing near the drinks table beside a person who was doing the same thing — she could tell by the expression, which she recognized as her own: present, engaged, very slightly over it.

'Kabir,' he said, when she looked at him. Just the name, which she liked.

'Ria,' she said. 'Are you also at the stage where you've smiled at everyone you know?'

'Fifteen minutes ago,' he said. 'I'm now in the phase of looking interested in the skyline.'

The skyline was, in fact, interesting — Bombay on New Year's Eve, the lights going on forever. But she laughed because it was true. They stood at the railing and let the party happen behind them and talked — about the year ending, about the city, about the fact that they were both, in slightly different ways, newer to Bombay than they appeared. He had moved from Chandigarh three years ago for work. She had moved from Pune two years ago for work. They had in common the experience of being in Bombay long enough to love it but not long enough to have stopped being astonished by it.

He was easy to talk to in the way that certain people are — not because he performed ease but because he was genuinely curious and listened without looking for his turn to speak. She mentioned something she was reading; he asked a question about it that showed he had actually tracked what she'd said. She noticed this.

At five to midnight the crowd contracted toward the center of the roof for the countdown. Someone bumped into her — the party shifted, rearranged, did what parties do at midnight — and when the countdown was done and the city erupted and the roof erupted and she turned, he was not there. She looked. The crowd had done its midnight thing and Kabir — no surname, no number, just Kabir from Chandigarh with the easy manner and the question about her book — was not where he had been.

She looked for twenty minutes. She asked Nisha, who didn't know him — he'd come with someone else, the guest of a guest. She stood at the railing alone for a while looking at Bombay being loud and bright below her.

New year, she thought. Well.

Chapter 2: January

The city in January had a particular quality — clean-slated, optimistic in the way that things are before the year reveals itself. Ria went back to her life: her job at an urban design firm in Lower Parel, her apartment in Bandra West that was slightly too small and full of things she had been meaning to sort since she moved in, her Saturday morning routine of the market and the sea.

She thought about the party once or twice. Not obsessively — she was not someone who orbited things; she had a policy about this — but with the specific quality of an unfinished thought. A conversation that had not reached its end. She knew only: Kabir, moved from Chandigarh, worked in something she couldn't remember, easy listener, had been somewhere in Bombay since the clock struck twelve.

In mid-January she was in a meeting at a client site in Andheri when the project manager from the client side came in and introduced his team and one of them was a woman named Priya who said she was from Chandigarh originally and Ria felt something very faint and stupid flicker in her chest that she dismissed immediately. Chandigarh was a city of millions. This was absurd.

She did not mention any of this to anyone.

At the end of January there was a work dinner — her firm's partners, a visiting architect, some clients. She was seated between people she knew and she was fine. On the way out she held the lift for a man who was running for it from across the lobby, and for two seconds her heart did a theatrical and embarrassing thing because he was tall and had a particular quality to his stride, and then he turned and he was not Kabir, he was no one she knew, and she felt very silly all the way to the car.

'You are looking for someone,' her colleague Darshan said the next day, with the percipience of people who have known you for years and are intolerable for it.

'I am not,' she said.

'Who was at the party?' he said.

She told him. He said: 'It's Bombay. Millions of people. You won't find him.'

'I'm not looking,' she said.

'Right,' he said.

Chapter 3: February

The urban design conference in February was large and multi-day and held at a hotel in Powai, which Ria attended because her firm had submitted a paper and she was presenting on the second day. Conferences were their own world — name tags, coffee stations, the rhythm of panels and breaks — and she navigated this efficiently, as she navigated most things.

On the first evening there was a networking dinner. She was standing with a glass of water — she'd learned that water was more mobile than wine at these things — when she saw him.

He was across the room. He was talking to someone, profile to her, and she had only seen him once for an hour at a loud party and yet she knew immediately. The same posture. The same quality of attention directed at the person he was talking to.

She stood very still.

Then someone touched her arm — a colleague from another firm she had met at a previous conference — and drew her into a conversation, and she was a professional and she engaged with the conversation fully and when she looked again the room had shifted and she couldn't find him.

She looked for ten minutes. She could not find him. She went back to her hotel room and sat on the bed and thought: twice now. Bombay. Twice.

In the morning she looked through the conference program. Kabir was not a unique name. She looked for Kabirsomething-from-Chandigarh on the speaker list, the attendee list that was circulated. There were three Kabirs. None of them matched what little she had.

She gave her presentation. It went well. She took the train back to Bandra and stood in her too-small apartment and thought about probability and cities and how Bombay could be enormous and also oddly circulatory, how the same streets and the same restaurants cycled the same people past each other. She thought about how she was not looking and how she had definitely looked.

Chapter 4: March

Her friend Aisha got married in March, in a small ceremony in the Bandra church followed by a lunch at a restaurant nearby. Ria was in the wedding party — she stood at the front and smiled at the right moments and thought, as she always did at weddings, that ceremony was the shape we gave to things that were actually shapeless.

The lunch afterward was the better part. Round tables, good food, the easy noise of people who knew each other well. She was seated between Aisha's brother and a woman she hadn't met before, and she was having a very good conversation about the woman's work in conservation architecture when the seat across from her was taken.

She looked up.

He looked up.

Kabir. Not across a party, not glimpsed through a conference room. Directly across a table, in the bright March afternoon, with a name tag from the morning that still clung to his jacket pocket. They looked at each other for a moment that was slightly longer than polite table introductions required.

'Ria,' he said.

'Kabir,' she said.

The woman beside her looked between them. 'You know each other?'

'New Year's,' Ria said. 'Nisha's party.'

'I've been—' He stopped. 'This is the third time I've seen you.'

'Second for me,' she said. 'I might have—' She stopped too.

He looked at her steadily. He said, 'I think I saw you at the design conference in February.'

'I was there,' she said.

'I couldn't find you after.'

She said nothing. The table around them had its lunch. The afternoon was very bright.

'I have your friend Aisha's number,' he said. 'I could have asked her.' He said it in the tone of someone confessing a decision that turned out to have been wrong. 'I didn't. I don't know why.'

'I could have looked through the conference list,' she said. 'I looked. I didn't find you.'

'You looked,' he said.

'I looked,' she admitted.

They looked at each other across the wedding table in the March light, with Aisha's new husband cutting cake somewhere behind them and the city carrying on outside.

Chapter 5: April

He called her the Monday after the wedding. She had given him her number at the table with the specific relief of a problem that has found its solution and the specific anxiety of a problem that has found its solution.

He worked in architecture — sustainable construction, a firm in Lower Parel that she passed every day on the way to her own office, which, when she told him, made them both quiet for a moment. The city playing its Bombay game.

They went for coffee first, then dinner, then a Sunday walk along Carter Road that was supposed to be an hour and was three. She found, in person and with time, what she had found at the party — he was genuinely curious, he listened, he said what he thought without weaponizing it. He had an opinion about everything worth having an opinion about and held none of them more tightly than the evidence required.

She told him things she didn't usually tell people quickly: that she had moved to Bombay partly to get out from under a relationship that had ended badly, that she had made a rule about not being passive about her own life which she had not kept perfectly, that she found the city clarifying in a way that was sometimes uncomfortable.

He told her: that he had nearly gone back to Chandigarh twice and stayed because Bombay kept offering something he hadn't yet gotten hold of, that he lived alone and had started to find it too quiet, that he had thought about her after the party more than seemed proportionate given one hour of conversation.

'More than seemed proportionate,' she repeated.

'I have a policy about honesty,' he said, without apology.

She thought about her own policy — the not-being-passive one. She thought about three months of not-looking while looking. She thought about this man across a table, twice missed, third time found.

'Good policy,' she said.

Chapter 6: May to June

The months had their texture. May in Bombay, before the monsoon, had a particular heat and light — close, almost golden, the city at its most exposed. They spent it learning the shapes of each other's weeks: when she worked late and when he didn't, what they ate and what they avoided, the small negotiations of two people whose lives had been built around their own logic beginning to adjust.

His apartment was in Khar, which was fifteen minutes from her Bandra flat if you didn't count traffic, which you always had to count. They alternated. She cooked better; he made excellent coffee and was better at going to bed at a reasonable hour, which was a practical advantage they were still working out the implications of.

She met his Bombay friends in May — the architecture crowd, several people from his firm, a friend from Chandigarh who had also migrated and with whom he spoke in Punjabi at a speed she found affectionately impenetrable. He met Darshan and Aisha and the work people who were her Bombay family, and she watched him navigate these introductions with the same quality she had noticed first — present, curious, not performing.

Darshan, afterward: 'I like him. He listens.'

'I know,' she said.

'He looks at you,' Darshan said, 'like you're interesting.'

'Darshan,' she said.

'I mean it well,' he said. 'Some people look at you like you're beautiful. He looks at you like you're interesting. That's better.'

She thought about this on the way home. She thought about it the way she thought about things she was still settling with — not anxiously, just sitting with them, turning them over.

June brought the monsoon. Bombay in the rain was a different city — louder, slower, the streets doing their flooding routine, people moving with umbrellas and the specific patience of the prepared. She had always loved the monsoon. He had been unprepared for Bombay's version of it, the first year — his Chandigarh experience of rain being nothing like this — and had developed, she gathered, an annual system that involved extra supplies and a particular philosophical equanimity.

They got caught in the first heavy rain of the season, walking back from a film, and stood under an awning in Bandra while the street became a river, and he held the umbrella over both of them even though it was insufficient for the task, and she thought: this is fine. This is very fine.

Chapter 7: July

In July something happened that she had not anticipated, which was that it became hard.

Not between them. A project at her firm — a major one, heritage redevelopment in South Bombay — hit complications that required her to work the kind of hours that made weeks disappear. She was in the office at eight, often home at ten, and the work followed her even then — her mind on load-bearing walls and heritage commission requirements and the gap between what she had designed and what the site would accommodate.

He gave her space without withdrawing. This was the specific thing. He did not crowd her or require management. He texted when it seemed right and not when it didn't and he showed up on a Thursday evening with food from the place near her office because she had mentioned being too tired to cook, and he ate with her on her small balcony while she talked through the project problem and he listened and then asked the one question that made something click into place.

'You're thinking about it,' she said, surprised.

'Of course I am,' he said. 'You've been working on it for two weeks.'

She looked at him across the dinner containers. She thought about the word listen, and about what it actually meant to listen — not to wait, not to track the general shape, but to actually hold what someone was saying.

She said: 'I have a habit of managing things myself. From before. I don't always—'

'I know,' he said. He didn't say it with impatience. He said it the way you say things when you have actually been paying attention.

'I'm working on it,' she said.

'I know that too,' he said.

They sat with the city in the monsoon dark, and she thought: this is the part that is difficult to find. Not the beginning — the beginning was possible. This — the July of it, the complicated month, the part where it was easier to retreat than to stay — this was what told you something.

She reached across the dinner containers and took his hand. He turned it over and held it properly, without making a moment of it. She left it there.

Chapter 8: August

The project crisis resolved in early August, as project crises eventually do — imperfectly, with compromise and exhaustion, but resolved. She surfaced from it and found that what was there, on the other side, had not changed.

This was the kind of thing that mattered.

In August they went to Alibag for a weekend — a friend's house, three other couples, the particular pleasure of being somewhere that was not Bombay while still being close enough that it didn't count as leaving. The ferry out at seven in the morning, the island receiving them with its usual self-possessed quiet.

She and Kabir walked along the shore in the afternoon, when the others were resting, and she told him something she had been sitting with since July: 'The last relationship I was in — when things got complicated, I solved it by making more space. Manageable space. Until there was too much and it collapsed.'

'What made it complicated?' he asked.

'He needed me to be easy,' she said. 'And I'm not always easy.'

'I know that,' he said. He said it with something like pleasure, not complaint.

'You don't mind it.'

'I find it interesting,' he said. 'You're working through something or you have an opinion and it's — no. I don't mind it. I'd rather the full version.'

The shore. The afternoon. The sound of the water in its August mood. She thought about what it meant to be fully visible to someone and to have that be the thing they wanted rather than the thing they were tolerating.

'Kabir,' she said.

'Ria,' he said.

'I think I'm — I think this is—' She stopped. She was not prone to not finishing sentences. She finished it: 'I think I'm in it. This. With you.'

He stopped walking. He looked at her with the same expression he had across the wedding table in March — considered, present, as if she had said something he had been waiting for someone to say. 'I've been in it since January,' he said. 'I just didn't have anywhere to put it.'

The water did what water does. She laughed — a real one, sudden and grateful. 'Since January?'

'The conference, Ria. I looked for you in the whole building.'

She looked at him. The afternoon sun. The shore. 'You should have been faster,' she said.

'I know,' he said. 'I'm working on it.'

Chapter 9: September to October

September settled. There is a particular quality to a relationship that has found its actual level — not the beginning's careful brightness, not July's difficulty, but the thing underneath both, which is ordinary and substantial. They had this now.

She learned things about him that the early months had not yet surfaced. That he woke in the night sometimes and could not sleep and went to the kitchen rather than lying there — she learned this because she woke once and he was gone and found him sitting on the kitchen floor with tea and a book, entirely unapologetic, which made her like him more. That he had wanted to be a painter before architecture claimed him — there were sketchbooks on the shelf she had assumed were professional but were not, small studies of places, of faces, of structural details that were drawn with a different kind of attention than technical documentation. That he called his mother every Sunday but didn't always say much — the call was more presence than conversation, which she understood because her own family operated the same way.

He learned things about her too. That she moved things around — her apartment had a new configuration every two months, not from restlessness but from trying to find what worked. That she had a habit of reading the last page of a novel before she started it, which he found baffling until she explained that she read better when she wasn't anxious about endings, and then he found it illuminating in a way that went beyond books. That she had a generous furious quality when she encountered things done badly or injustly — she didn't simmer, she expressed it and moved on — and that this was one of the things he had noticed at the party and thought: interesting, that.

In October she read the last page of the year — is what she thought, alone one evening while he was at a site visit. She meant: I know how this goes. She meant: I read it early and I'm not anxious about the ending. She sat with this thought and found it settled.

He came back at eight and she told him about the project she was starting — something ambitious, a commission she'd wanted for two years. He listened. He asked the right questions. He got them dinner from the place they liked. She thought: ordinary. Yes. This.

Chapter 10: November

Her sister Priya came to visit from Pune in November, which Ria had been anticipating with the specific combination of gladness and assessment that a beloved sibling's visit entailed. Priya was the kind of person who had opinions about everything and the confidence to voice them, which Ria had grown up with and found both annoying and useful.

Kabir met Priya on the Saturday of her visit — they had dinner, all three, at the restaurant in Bandra where Ria and Kabir went so often it had become theirs by default. Priya was direct and Kabir was direct and they discovered an overlap of opinions about infrastructure and city planning that Ria had not predicted, and the dinner went an hour longer than planned.

In the cab back to Ria's apartment, Priya sat in the back seat with her while Kabir sat up front, and Priya looked at Ria with the full force of a sister who has been watching and said nothing out loud. Ria looked back at her with her full force. Priya looked at Kabir's profile. Looked back at Ria. Made a small satisfied face.

Ria considered this evidence.

The next morning, early, Priya and Ria had chai on the balcony while Kabir was still asleep and Priya said, without preamble: 'He's good.' She meant it the way she said meaningful things — simple, no performance.

'I know,' Ria said.

'You're happy,' Priya said. 'You look — easy. Not simple. But easy.'

Ria thought about the word. She thought about last July and the space she hadn't made and the project that had not collapsed them. She thought about a man in a kitchen at midnight with tea and a book, unapologetic. She thought about sketchbooks that were not professional. 'Yes,' she said. 'I think I am.'

Priya drank her chai. Looked at the Bandra morning. 'Good,' she said. 'That's all I needed to know.'

Chapter 11: December

The year came around to itself.

Nisha announced the party early — she was, as always, on the roof in Bandra, the same roof, the same date. Ria and Kabir were both invited, independently and then together, and there was something in this fact — the same roof, twelve months on — that she found appropriate without being able to fully say why.

They arrived together, which was different from before. She had arrived alone last year, an hour into the party, making herself talk to strangers. This year she arrived at nine with Kabir and they were immediately absorbed into the crowd, and she was aware that coming with someone and coming alone were two different ways of being in the same place.

She saw Nisha, who hugged her and said 'finally' in a way that suggested she had always been managing something, which Ria decided not to follow up on. She saw Darshan, who saw Kabir and made a deeply restrained face of approval. She moved through the party that was the same party and was entirely different from the inside.

At ten she found herself at the railing again. She had not planned this — the body returned to where things had happened. The city below was doing its New Year's Eve thing, all that light. Kabir found her in three minutes because he knew by now where she went when parties became a lot.

He stood beside her at the railing. 'Same spot,' he said.

'I didn't plan it,' she said.

'Neither did I last year,' he said. He was looking at the city. 'I went to get a drink and ended up at the railing and you were there.'

'I was escaping the crowd,' she said.

'I know. Same reason.' He paused. 'I thought: there's a person who's doing the same thing I'm doing.'

She looked at the city. Twelve months of it. The circuit of it — party to conference to wedding to here, the city circling them back around to itself. She thought: Bombay, she had said, kept offering something. She thought: here it was.

'Don't lose me in the midnight crowd this year,' she said.

He took her hand at the railing. 'I don't plan to,' he said.

Behind them, someone started the countdown. The city prepared its noise. She held on, and the year turned, and she was not worried about the ending.
