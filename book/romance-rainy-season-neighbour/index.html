Rainy Season Neighbour

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

She moves in during the monsoon; he appears at her door with solutions to problems she hasn't mentioned yet.

Chapter 1: First Floor, Second from Left

Kavya moved into the flat on the first of July, which was possibly the worst day possible to move anywhere in Pune because it had been raining since the previous Thursday and the movers were in a bad mood and everything that could get damp did.

She was on the second floor. The building was an old one in Kothrud — pale yellow walls, iron-grille windows, a staircase that echoed — and the landlord had told her it was a quiet building with good neighbours, which was the kind of thing landlords always said. She had moved from Hyderabad for work and knew exactly no one in the city, which she was choosing to experience as an adventure rather than the moderately terrifying thing it actually was.

She had been in the flat for three hours when there was a knock at her door. She opened it to find a man — late twenties, perhaps thirty, holding an umbrella that was still dripping — and he said, 'Flat below. Arjun. The water is coming through your balcony drain into my kitchen.' He paused. 'I have a plunger if you need it. The drain gets blocked every monsoon.'

She had not yet noticed this problem. She had not been on the balcony. She had been unpacking mugs.

'Oh,' she said. 'I'm sorry, I didn't — how did you know it was my drain?'

'The building has one drainage stack,' he said. 'When yours blocks, mine overflows.' He was very matter-of-fact about this. He held out the plunger with the manner of someone who had made this trip before. 'I can show you where the cleanout is.'

She took the plunger. She introduced herself. He showed her the cleanout. He fixed the drain in ten minutes with the focused competence of someone who had strong opinions about clogged drains, and she was left standing on her rain-wet balcony with a plunger, a working drain, and the specific feeling of having been efficiently rescued by someone she hadn't known existed until seven minutes ago.

'Thank you,' she said.

'It happens every year,' he said. He looked out at the rain for a moment — it was very heavy, the kind that turned the lane below into a river. 'It will happen again in August. The plunger is yours. Keep it.'

He went downstairs. She went back to her mugs. She thought about the way he had looked at the rain — as though it were old and familiar and fine.

Chapter 2: Appearing

The second time was four days later, when he knocked to say that the building's electricity meter for her flat sometimes tripped when the monsoon was heavy and that he had taken the liberty of labelling the relevant switch in the common electrical box so she'd know which to flip. She had not had a power cut. She was mildly baffled.

The third time was the following week, during a power cut. He appeared with a small torch, an extension cord, and a portable power bank with a USB-C cable, which he offered without any apparent embarrassment. She took the power bank and said, 'How did you know I'd need this?'

'You have a laptop on your table,' he said. 'Through the window.'

She looked at the window. The window faced the lane. From the lane, her second-floor window was visible. From his kitchen, presumably — she did a quick spatial calculation — also visible. 'You can see my flat from your kitchen?' she said.

'The light is on most evenings,' he said, not quite answering. 'I don't — I'm not watching. I just notice the light.'

She looked at him. He had the expression of a man who was aware that this had come out slightly wrong and was not sure how to correct it.

'I work late,' she said, offering him an exit.

'I know,' he said, and then caught himself. 'The light tells me.'

She held the power bank. 'Do you do this for all the tenants?' she asked. 'Bring things they haven't asked for?'

He considered this. 'The aunty on the third floor — I bring her newspaper up when it's raining. The couple on the ground floor, I told them about the loose tile before it became a problem.' He paused. 'The last person in your flat left after two months. She couldn't manage the monsoon.'

Kavya thought about this. She thought about a man who paid attention to things, who showed up before problems became problems, who noticed light in windows.

'I'm not leaving,' she said. 'I like the rain.' She looked past him at the wet staircase. 'Would you like some chai? I just made it.'

A pause. He seemed genuinely surprised. 'Okay,' he said.

Chapter 3: What He Knew

By August, the pattern had clarified itself. He didn't come every day — not even every week. But when he did appear, he always had a specific and practical reason, and the reason was always accurate. He brought a tube of sealant for a window she had been meaning to look at. He mentioned that the vegetable vendor on the corner was trustworthy on Tuesdays, not on Thursdays. He told her the name of the electrician who knew the building's wiring without being asked because she had mentioned, just once, that a socket seemed slow.

She had started keeping tea ready.

This was something she acknowledged to herself in August, sitting on the balcony with the rain making its steady noise. She didn't always hear him coming. But she kept water on the stove in the evenings and she didn't examine why.

She learned things about him in the spaces between practical information. He worked in urban planning — which explained, she thought, the attention to infrastructure. He had grown up in the building, moved out, moved back when his parents relocated to Nashik. He was thirty-one. He read environmental reports for pleasure, which she found unexpectedly charming. He made very good coffee and brought her some once in a steel tumbler the morning after a particularly heavy night of rain, when he said he had thought the noise might have kept her awake.

It had. She hadn't mentioned it.

In September she came home from the office to find a small piece of paper tucked under her door. On it, in careful handwriting: The lane floods after heavy rain — park on the main road if it starts before 6. — A. She stood in the corridor holding the note and felt something she recognized as significant shift quietly in her chest.

She went downstairs and knocked on his door. He opened it and she said, without preamble, 'You know all these things about me. What I need, what time I get home, that I work late, that the rain keeps me up.' She looked at him. 'I don't know almost anything about you.'

'You know I make good coffee,' he said.

'That's not enough,' she said.

He was quiet for a moment. The rain was its steady self in the lane outside. Then he said, 'I know a good place for dinner. In Kothrud, close enough to walk. If you wanted to fix that.'

The lane was not flooded. She had checked. 'Now?' she said.

'I'll get my jacket,' he said.
