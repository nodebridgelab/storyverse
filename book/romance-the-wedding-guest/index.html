The Wedding Guest

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

Strangers at a wedding table spend the whole evening very carefully not falling for each other.

Chapter 1: Table Eleven

The seating plan had them both at Table Eleven, which was neither close enough to the main stage to be prestigious nor far enough to feel like a punishment — it was, Nisha decided, the table for people the family liked but had not quite figured out where to place. She was the bride's college friend. She didn't know who the man across from her was.

The wedding was in Jaipur, in a haveli that had been hired for the occasion, and it was the kind of wedding where money and effort had been applied in every direction — flowers hanging from every archway, chandeliers, a live band playing Kishore Kumar numbers at a volume that made conversation both necessary and difficult. Nisha had arrived in the city that afternoon and knew no one else at the table.

The man was already seated when she arrived. He was in a navy sherwani, reading something on his phone that he put away immediately when she sat down, which she noted because it was unusual at weddings. Most people kept their phones out all evening like a defensive measure.

'Nisha,' she said, offering her name without the usual preamble.

'Rohan,' he said. 'Groom's cousin.' He looked at the table with the expression of someone doing social geometry. 'Table Eleven.'

'I know,' she said.

'Not an insult,' he said. 'I asked to be put somewhere quiet.'

This was, she thought, a surprisingly honest thing to say at a wedding.

The other guests at the table settled in over the next twenty minutes — a pair of aunts who knew each other, two young cousins who were clearly friends and focused entirely on each other. This left Nisha and Rohan in the natural situation of two strangers with no one else to talk to.

The band played. The lights were very beautiful on the old stone walls. A waiter came with drinks.

'So,' Rohan said, with the tone of a man who was going to do this properly. 'What do you do?'

'Architect,' she said. 'Bangalore. You?'

'Policy research,' he said. 'Delhi.' A pause. 'Your first Jaipur wedding?'

'Is it that obvious?'

'You've looked at the ceiling three times in ten minutes.'

She had. The ceiling was plastered with tiny mirrors. 'In my defence,' she said, 'the ceiling is exceptional.'

Chapter 2: The Long Dinner

The first course arrived at nine — this was a wedding, it ran on wedding time — and they were well into a conversation about urban architecture and its relationship to public policy, which was not what either of them had expected to be talking about but had the comfortable momentum of a conversation that had found its own level.

He was interesting. She kept noticing this despite her intention not to particularly notice anything about him. He had opinions that he held firmly but without aggression, and when she disagreed with him — which she did, twice, on the question of heritage conservation versus residential need — he listened with actual attention rather than the performance of attention that people often substituted.

She was also aware that he was good-looking in a way that she was choosing not to factor in. This was a wedding, she was in Jaipur for two days, she had a full life in Bangalore. These were all true things.

'The band has played Ek Ladki Ko Dekha twice now,' she said, during a pause in which the groom was escorted to the stage for some ritual she didn't know.

'They'll play it at least once more,' he said. 'Weddings have a playlist that never changes.'

'Do you go to many weddings?'

'My family is large and relentlessly matrimonial,' he said. 'I have been to nine weddings in three years. I have been seated at the difficult table six times.'

She laughed at that. 'The difficult table?'

'Not family enough for the inner circle. Not distant enough to be given the row of chairs in the back. Eleven is always the difficult table.'

She looked at him across the table — at the way the mirror-work ceiling scattered light across everything. She thought: this is exactly the kind of conversation she would not allow herself to have if she were being sensible. She had been sensible for two years, since Arjun, and it had served her perfectly well.

'I like Table Eleven,' she said.

'So do I,' he said. 'Tonight particularly.'

A small silence. The band played something slower. A few couples moved toward the open courtyard that was serving as a dance floor. One of the aunts nudged the other. Nisha looked at her hands, at the mehendi on them — her own, from this morning — and thought about the two-day visit and the flight back to Bangalore on Sunday.

'The fort,' she said abruptly. 'Amber Fort. I was going to go tomorrow morning before the flight. The light is supposed to be good in the early morning.'

'It is,' he said. He said it as though he knew.

Chapter 3: Sunday Morning

She had not invited him. She had mentioned the fort and he had said the light was good and that had been that — the conversation had moved on, the main course had arrived, the bride's mother had come by the table to apologize for the seating with a warmth that suggested she wasn't actually sorry, and eventually the evening had wound down into its late phases where the music softened and the elders sat and the young wandered.

He had said goodbye at the door of the haveli. She had said goodbye. It had been entirely normal. She had gone to bed.

She was at the fort at seven in the morning because the light really was supposed to be good. She had her camera and her coffee from the hotel and the specific contentment of a person who travels alone and is fine with it.

He was there. At the gate. With a coffee from somewhere.

They looked at each other across the entrance courtyard. He looked the same kind of unsurprised that she felt.

'The light,' he said.

'Yes,' she said.

They walked through the fort in the early morning, when the tour groups hadn't arrived yet and the stone was warm in the angled sun and the view over the lake was the colour of water and sky doing something cooperative. He knew things about the fort — the construction dates, the water systems, the geometric principles of the lattice screens — that he mentioned without turning it into a lecture. She photographed the things that interested her: the geometry of the shadows, the place where the mirror work in the Sheesh Mahal made a single lamp into a galaxy.

He stood next to her while she photographed. He didn't talk. This was the thing about him, she thought, pressing the shutter — he knew when to be quiet.

Afterward, they sat in the courtyard with the last of their coffees. Her flight was at two. He had a train at four.

'Bangalore and Delhi are not the same city,' she said.

'No,' he agreed.

A pause. Below them, a tour bus pulled in. The morning was becoming the day.

'I have a work trip to Delhi in October,' she said. It was true. She had not planned to mention it. 'For a heritage project consultation.'

He said nothing for a moment. Then: 'I know good places for dinner in Delhi. Not Table Eleven tier. Better.'

She looked at him — the same way she had at the table, the ceiling light scattered everywhere, except now it was actual morning and easier to see. 'Send me the list,' she said.

'Or,' he said, 'I could show you.'

She took out her phone. He took out his. They exchanged numbers with the straight-faced practicality of people who knew exactly what they were doing and had decided to do it anyway.
