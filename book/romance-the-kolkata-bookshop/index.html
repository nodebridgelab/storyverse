The Kolkata Bookshop

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

Every Saturday she visits his crumbling bookshop; every week her books are already set aside before she arrives.

Chapter 1: College Street

The shop had no proper sign, which Piya had always thought was either deeply confident or deeply eccentric. It was on a lane off College Street — the kind of lane that Kolkata manufactures effortlessly, narrow and crooked and smelling of old paper — and it existed, as far as she could tell, in a state of permanent near-collapse that had somehow persisted for decades. The shelves were built into the walls, the ceiling was water-stained in patterns that resembled maps of places that didn't exist, and the floor had a particular quality of knowing exactly where you stood.

Piya had found it by accident eighteen months ago — she had been cutting through the lane in the rain and ducked in and picked up, for twenty rupees, a 1974 edition of a Tagore translation she had been looking for since university. Since then she had come every Saturday.

The owner was a man named Biren, who appeared to be somewhere in his late thirties, though the shop's atmosphere made everyone inside seem slightly out of time. He was always there when she arrived — behind the desk, reading, cataloguing, writing in a ledger whose purpose she had never established. He was tall and slightly severe-looking until he smiled, which happened infrequently and with the quality of something that had not been wasted.

She had been coming for two months before she realized that books were appearing on the desk when she arrived. Not left randomly — set aside. A first-edition Nabarun Bhattacharya that she had mentioned once in passing. A collection of women writers from Bengal that she had been trying to find. She had said nothing. She had bought them. She had gone home and sat with the books and the faint discomfort of being known by someone she hadn't formally introduced herself to.

The following Saturday she arrived and said: 'You put those books aside for me.' It wasn't a question.

He looked up from the ledger. 'You were looking for them,' he said.

'How did you know what I was looking for?'

He considered this. 'You tell me,' he said. 'When you browse. You talk to yourself.'

She opened her mouth and closed it. She had not known she did this. She felt simultaneously embarrassed and fascinated. 'I'll try to stop,' she said.

'Please don't,' he said, and returned to his ledger.

Chapter 2: Saturdays

She did not stop. She had tried, for two Saturdays, and found that she needed to narrate to herself to think, which was apparently her private system. She accepted this and accepted also that it meant Biren knew things about what she was looking for, which led to her arriving each week to find something on the desk she hadn't quite realized she wanted.

The shop became a different shape over time. She noticed more of it — the way it extended back into a second room she had not seen at first, smaller, darker, which contained what he called the serious inventory, by which he seemed to mean the things he hadn't priced yet or wasn't sure he wanted to sell. She was invited into this room in April, which she took as a mark of some kind. She found three things in there that she bought on the spot.

'How do you find these?' she asked. She was holding a 1961 edition of Bibhutibhushan Bandyopadhyay, original dust jacket intact.

'Estates,' he said. 'Old families, old libraries. People who don't know what they have.'

'And do you know what you have?'

He looked at the book in her hands. 'I know what is worth keeping and what can be let go. Sometimes those are the same thing.' He said it about the book. She was not sure if it was entirely about the book.

In June she came on a Saturday when it was raining so heavily the lane was ankle-deep and she had waded in and arrived wet to the knees, and he had produced, from somewhere behind the desk, a towel and a cup of tea with the complete unstartled manner of someone who had been expecting exactly this. She accepted both.

'You have a towel in a bookshop,' she said.

'The lane floods in the monsoon,' he said. 'People arrive wet.'

'And you have tea for all of them?' she said.

A small pause. 'I have tea for Saturdays,' he said, which was not quite the same as what she had asked.

She drank the tea. The rain did its Kolkata thing outside. She browsed and narrated and he read and catalogued, and she thought that this — this specific Saturday morning, this specific room smelling of old paper and tea, this specific silence that was not a silence — was one of the things she had not expected and could not adequately explain.

Chapter 3: The Closed Sign

In October, she arrived to find the shop closed. Not the casual closed of a late morning — the actual shutter pulled halfway down, a paper sign that said CLOSED, which was so unusual she stood in the lane for a full minute staring at it.

She left and came back the following Saturday, and the shutter was up. She went in and Biren was at the desk and she said, before anything else, 'You were closed last week.'

'Family matter in Shantiniketan,' he said. 'I should have put a sign earlier. I'm sorry.'

'I was—' She stopped. She had been, she realized, going to say worried. She said instead: 'I came by.'

He looked at her. The severity of his expression was doing what it did, which was to make the moments when it shifted more legible. 'I'm sorry,' he said again. 'I should have—' He stopped too. 'I thought of calling. I don't have your number.'

They looked at each other across the desk. In eighteen months she had never given him her number. He had never asked. This had been a system that worked. She thought about why it had worked, and what it would mean to change it.

On the desk, as always, there was a book set aside. She looked at it. It was a small thing — a chapbook, actually, handmade, the kind of limited-run poetry collection that sometimes made its way into shops like this. She picked it up. Inside the front cover, in pencil so light it was barely there, was a note in careful handwriting: Piya — this came in on Wednesday. I thought it was yours.

She had not told him her name. She had told him things — in narrating to herself while browsing, she had apparently mentioned her own name at some point, which she could believe. She stood with the chapbook and the pencilled note and felt something very clear and complete arrive in her chest.

'Biren,' she said. 'It's Piya. In case you needed — if you wanted to—'

'I know your name,' he said, very carefully.

'A number is different,' she said.

He came around the desk. He had never done this — always remained on his side of it. He stood in front of her and held out his phone with the same manner he had when she was buying something: deliberate, exact, without flourish. She entered her number. She handed it back.

'Thank you,' he said. He meant, she thought, more than her number. She meant the same when she said: 'Thank you for saving the books.'

She bought the chapbook. She walked out into the October Kolkata afternoon, where the light was doing what only Kolkata light did, and she thought about a crumbling shop on a crooked lane and a man who had learned her name from the way she talked to herself, and she walked home slowly, in no particular hurry.
