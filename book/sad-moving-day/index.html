Moving Day

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

Two best friends spend one last day packing up the apartment they shared for seven years before one of them marries.

Chapter 1: The System

They had a system, like they always did for everything. Tanvi made the labels. Priya taped the boxes. This was how it had always been — Tanvi's handwriting was neater and Priya's patience with tape and cardboard was inexhaustible in a way that Tanvi's was not. They had packed Priya's old life into boxes before, the move from Bhopal to Bombay seven years ago, the two of them sweating in Priya's mother's house in the summer heat and laughing at the sheer quantity of clothes Priya owned.

Today was different, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so.

The apartment was a two-bedroom on the third floor of a building in Andheri that had unreliable hot water, a ceiling fan in the living room that wobbled, and a landlord who communicated exclusively through his wife. They had moved in together when Priya got the Bombay job and Tanvi, who had already been here for a year, said: why are you looking at PGs, just come here, there is a room. It was supposed to be temporary. It was supposed to be one year, maybe two. Seven years had happened instead.

Priya was getting married on Saturday. She was moving to Pune. The groom was Sanket, whom both of them liked genuinely and unreservedly, which meant there was nothing to argue about and nowhere to put the strange feeling of this particular afternoon.

Tanvi was not moving. She was staying in the apartment, taking over the full rent, which she could afford now. She had already found someone to take Priya's room — her colleague Farah, who seemed nice. This was all practical and fine and the right set of decisions.

Tanvi labelled a box: PRIYA — BOOKS. She handed it to Priya, who taped the flaps without looking at the label. They worked efficiently. They were very good at this.

Chapter 2: The Things They Found

At two in the afternoon they found the pickle jar.

It was at the back of the highest kitchen shelf, behind a box of oats neither of them had eaten and a bottle of vinegar of uncertain age. The pickle jar was empty but still had the faded hand-written label from their first Diwali in the apartment — a batch of mango pickle that Priya's mother had sent, labelled in her mother's handwriting: FOR MY IDIOT DAUGHTERS (she referred to Tanvi as a daughter and had for years).

They both looked at it.

Keep it, Priya said finally. It's yours too.

They found other things. A photograph pinned to the inside of a cabinet door that they had forgotten was there, from a trip to Lonavala in 2019, the two of them and three other friends who were now scattered across different cities. A pen that Tanvi had accused Priya of stealing for three months, which turned out to have been in a kitchen drawer under a spare set of keys to an apartment they had moved out of four years ago. A note in Tanvi's handwriting on a Post-it stuck to the refrigerator door from the inside, where a former tenant must have put it and they had never opened this particular door panel before: REMEMBER: BUY CORIANDER.

They laughed at that one. The kind of laughing that is close to something else.

When did we stop buying coriander regularly? Priya said.

I don't know, Tanvi said. When we started ordering food more.

They added this to the general archaeology of seven years: the years of cooking together and arguing about whose turn it was to clean and watching each other through bad jobs and worse heartbreaks and the slow accumulation of habits so embedded that Tanvi could not currently imagine — was actively not imagining — what it would feel like to eat dinner here without Priya's noise.

Chapter 3: The Last Hour

By six o'clock the boxes were stacked in the living room. The moving van was coming in the morning, but the physical work was done.

They sat on the floor of Priya's empty room because all the furniture had already been moved or sold or given away, and the room was just walls and light now, and the marks on the wall where Priya's shelves had been, and the slightly uneven paint in the corner where Tanvi had tried to help repaint two years ago and had, as Priya often reminded her, done a terrible job.

Priya was getting married on Saturday. Sanket was a good person who made her genuinely happy. Pune was forty minutes by bus from Bombay on a good day. None of these facts resolved the feeling of sitting on the floor of an empty room at the end of seven years.

Do you remember, Priya said, and then she stopped.

What?

When we moved in. How long it took us to figure out the hot water. We thought it was broken and we called the landlord's wife three times and she kept telling us there was nothing wrong with it and then eventually she came up and turned the knob a different way. She looked at us like we were absolutely hopeless.

Tanvi said: I think about that every time I use the shower.

They sat with this for a while.

Tanvi said: you'll come back. You'll come all the time. You know that, right?

I know, Priya said.

And I'll come to Pune.

I know.

So this is not — it's not a goodbye.

No, Priya said. She leaned her head back against the wall. No, I know. It's just — it's still something, isn't it. It's still a kind of something.

Yes, Tanvi said. It is.

They sat on the floor of the empty room until it was dark, not saying much, letting the something be what it was. When they finally got up, Tanvi put the pickle jar — empty, labelled, irreplaceable — carefully in her own bag. She did not offer to share it again. Priya did not ask.
