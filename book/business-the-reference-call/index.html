The Reference Call

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

A founder picks up an investor's reference call and must decide how much truth costs him.

Chapter 1: The Number

Arjun was eating a late lunch at his desk—rice from a steel tiffin, the lid propped against his monitor—when the call came in from a Mumbai number he didn't recognise. He almost let it go to voicemail. The past month had been the kind where every unknown number felt like a creditor or a journalist, and he had learned to treat his phone like a small, dangerous animal.

He answered anyway.

"Arjun? This is Divya Menon, calling from Sequoia." She said it the way people say the name of a hospital—matter-of-fact, giving him time to adjust. "I'm doing a reference check on Kiran Rao and his team over at GrowthStack. He mentioned you as someone who had worked with him at Flipspark. Would you have fifteen minutes?"

The rice went cold immediately in some psychological sense. He stared at the tiffin lid.

Kiran. They had been co-founders at Flipspark eighteen months ago. The company was dead now, dissolved in the Registrar's records with all the dignity of a used tissue. Arjun had left eight months before the end, and he and Kiran had parted ways with the specific politeness that means neither person ever wants to speak of it again.

"Yes," Arjun said. "I know Kiran."

"Great. I'll just record this for our notes—is that okay?"

He said yes. He pushed the tiffin to the side of the desk.

Divya began with the easy questions. How long had he known Kiran. What was the nature of their working relationship. How did Kiran handle pressure. Arjun answered the first two honestly and the third carefully. Kiran handled pressure by redistributing it. That was the honest answer. The answer he gave was: "He sets very high expectations for himself and the people around him."

Then she asked about the pivot.

"GrowthStack's current model is quite different from what Flipspark was doing," she said. "Did you go through a major pivot while you were there? How did Kiran lead that?"

Arjun looked out the window. The afternoon light was hitting the building across the road at an angle that made it look almost golden. He thought about what had actually happened. The pivot hadn't been led. It had been survived, and not by everyone. They had burned through fourteen months of runway chasing an enterprise SaaS angle that Kiran had been convinced about despite data suggesting otherwise. When the angel round finally dried up, they had pivoted—but it was less a decision and more a capitulation, and three engineers had quit in the two weeks that followed because no one had told them the truth about the cash position.

Arjun had been one of the people who hadn't told them.

"The pivot was significant," he said. "There were some hard months."

"Were there communication issues with the team around that time?"

He was quiet for one second longer than he should have been.

Chapter 2: What He Said

"There were communication gaps," Arjun said finally. "Things moved fast and not everyone had full context at the right time."

It was true. It was also the version of events that could live comfortably on a LinkedIn post. He knew this as he said it. Divya made a small sound that might have been a note being typed or might have been scepticism.

"Were those gaps on Kiran's end, or more of a company-wide thing?"

Arjun stood up from his chair and walked to the window. Below, an auto-rickshaw was trying to reverse out of a space that was clearly too small. The driver kept inching back and forward.

"Look," he said, and then stopped.

The thing about reference calls was that everyone in the ecosystem knew how they worked. You said something warm, you flagged one or two manageable concerns to seem credible, and you protected the relationship. Kiran would almost certainly be giving Arjun's number to investors if his own company ever raised again. That was just how it functioned. The whole system ran on people being sixty percent honest and one hundred percent collegial.

But Divya had asked a specific question. And Sequoia was about to put money—real money, the kind that changed company trajectories—into Kiran's next venture, based in part on what people like Arjun said in calls like this one.

Arjun thought about the three engineers who had quit. Rohit, who had moved back to Pune and was still freelancing because he hadn't wanted to explain on interviews why he'd left a start-up after eight months. Shalini, who had told Arjun at the farewell dinner that she'd found out about the cash situation from a contractor, not from Kiran. Pradeep, who simply hadn't come into the office one day and had sent a message on Slack that read: 'I think it's time. Thanks for everything.'

"The communication gaps were more on Kiran's end," Arjun said. "He made decisions about company direction and cash position that he didn't share with the team until very late. I think he believed he was protecting people from anxiety. But the effect was that people felt misled when things came out."

Silence.

"That's useful," Divya said, and she sounded like she meant it.

"He is genuinely brilliant at product," Arjun added, because it was also true. "His instinct for what users actually want, not what they say they want—that's real. But the team management and the communication, that's a learning curve he was still on when I left."

"And in your assessment, is that curve something he's continuing to work on, or—"

"I don't know," Arjun said. "I haven't spoken to him since Flipspark. I can only tell you what I observed."

Divya thanked him. The call lasted another four minutes—product questions, market observations, the kind of thing that felt like a palate cleanser after the harder part. When she hung up, Arjun stood at the window for a while.

The auto-rickshaw had gotten free. The driver was pulling into traffic now, the little vehicle tilting on the road's camber.

Chapter 3: The Cost

He didn't hear from Kiran for eleven days.

When the message came, it was a WhatsApp, which was worse than a call somehow. The preview showed enough: 'Heard you spoke to Sequoia. Would be good to—' and then the message cut off at the edge of the screen. Arjun didn't open it immediately. He left it unread for two hours, which accomplished nothing except making him check his phone more often.

When he finally opened it, the full message read: 'Heard you spoke to Sequoia. Would be good to connect and hear what you told them. The round is on hold. Just want to understand the situation.'

Arjun typed three different responses and deleted all of them.

The first was defensive: 'I told them the truth, which I think is what everyone deserves before committing capital.' The second was apologetic, which was worse: 'I hope I represented things fairly.' The third was a question back, which was a coward's move.

He put the phone face-down and went back to the customer call he was supposed to be on. Afterward, walking to the coffee machine in the small kitchen that smelled permanently of someone else's lunch, he thought about what the right version of himself would do.

The right version would call Kiran. Would say: I told them there were communication issues around the pivot. I said you were good at product. I said I didn't know where you are now with team management because I haven't seen you work since we split.

He called Kiran.

Kiran picked up after two rings, which meant he'd been waiting.

"Arjun." No warmth, no performance.

"I told them the truth about the pivot. About how the communication with the team went. I also said you were strong at product." He said it all in one go, no hedge.

A pause. "You could have called me first."

"She told me it was a reference check. You'd already given her my number."

Another pause. "Fair. I just—" Kiran stopped. Started again. "Was it specific?"

"I didn't name anyone. I said the team didn't have full information about cash position until late, and that it felt like protection but landed as concealment."

"Ouch."

"Yes."

"Is that how you actually saw it?"

"That is how it actually was," Arjun said. He kept his voice even. "You know that."

The silence this time was longer. Outside the office window, Bengaluru was doing what it always did in the late afternoon—turning into a slow bronze light over traffic. Somewhere a dog was barking, then stopped.

"Yeah," Kiran said. "Yeah, I know."

They didn't fix anything in that call. Kiran didn't thank him, and Arjun didn't apologise. But they stayed on the line for another ten minutes, talking about the market, about what GrowthStack was building, about a former colleague who had just joined a Series B company. By the end of it something had shifted—not resolved, but shifted.

Arjun didn't know if the round went through. He didn't find out for two more months, when a press announcement showed up in his feed: GrowthStack had closed a seed round, not from Sequoia, but from a smaller Mumbai firm. Kiran's photo was in the article. He looked tired and determined, the way founders always did in those photos.

Arjun read the article once and didn't share it. He hoped, in a plain way without any drama to it, that Kiran had learned something. He suspected they both had.
