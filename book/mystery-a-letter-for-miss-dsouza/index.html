A Letter for Miss D'Souza

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

A yellowed letter arrives forty years late at a Mangaluru post office. Retired postmaster Gopal Shenoy has until Sunday to find a woman who may no longer want to be found.

Chapter 1: The Forty-Year Delay

The letter slid out of a mailbag marked for the dead letter shelf, and Gopal Shenoy, who had retired from the Mangaluru head post office six years ago and only came back to help with the monthly sorting, almost dropped it into the discard box. Almost. Then he saw the stamp.

It carried the old five-paise Gandhi portrait, a stamp withdrawn before his daughter was born. The envelope was cream gone to tea colour, and the address was written in a firm, slanting hand. Miss Rosalind D'Souza, c/o the Convent Lane Dispensary, Bendur, Mangaluru.

"Someone has found this behind a cupboard," said the young clerk, Sunita. "They post these things sometimes. It is a hobby for them."

"A hobby with a postmark," Gopal said, tilting the envelope to the light. "Look. March nineteen eighty-four. It was posted. It simply never arrived."

"Forty years, Shenoy sir. She will be dead, or married to someone in Dubai."

"Perhaps." Gopal turned the letter over. The flap was still sealed. "But a letter is a promise, and the post office makes the promise. Give me until Sunday."

Sunita rolled her eyes, but she gave him the key to the old directory room.

The Convent Lane Dispensary was long closed, a shop selling mobile covers now. Gopal walked the lane with his stick, asking the sellers about a Miss D'Souza from 1984. A tea stall owner remembered a nurse by that name who had taught the neighbourhood children to bandage their knees. "She left," he said. "The sisters said her father fell ill, in Udupi."

Udupi was an hour away. Gopal went home and sat with the envelope and his wife's old magnifying glass, and noticed what he had missed in the office. In the top left corner, in pencil so faint it was almost a rumour, was a single word. Sender: Anand.

Chapter 2: The Pencil Word

Sunita was right about one thing, he decided on Saturday. A letter forty years old could not be delivered like a letter of today. Everything about it was strange now, and the old Rosalind might have buried the memory.

Still, there was a way to look. The hospital in Udupi where her father had been treated kept registers of the nursing school, and an elderly records clerk there agreed, after tea and a long conversation about pensions, to search the nineteen eighty-four list.

"D'Souza, Rosalind," he said at last, tracing a finger. "Left the nursing programme in April that year. She was engaged to a boy, it says here. A marriage note, but the name is smudged."

Gopal sat very still. "Could it be Anand?"

"It could be Anand, or Arvind, or Aneesh. Too wet, this register." The clerk shrugged apologetically. "But here is her forwarding address, and it is a village near Karkala."

By the time the bus reached Karkala the afternoon was gold. The village had one lane, one temple, and one church whose priest, a stout man with a broad moustache, knew everyone. He looked at the envelope for a long time.

"Rosalind D'Souza is Rosalind Pereira now," he said. "She lives behind the bakery. Her husband Anand Pereira was a postman himself before he died. Very quiet man."

Gopal felt something drop inside him, a small cold stone. A postman. A letter in his own hand, never delivered, to the woman he later married. It made no sense, unless it did.

"Did you know them well?"

"Forty years I have been here, Mr. Shenoy. I married them." The priest gave a gentle smile. "Come. She makes the best coffee in the district."

Chapter 3: Delivered

Rosalind Pereira was seventy-one, small and straight-backed, with spectacles on a chain and flour on her sleeve. She did not seem surprised to see a stranger at her door with a stick and an envelope. She seemed, if anything, resigned, like a woman who had been waiting for a late bus.

"Anand wrote me many letters," she said, when Gopal explained. "This one I never received. He told me, when we were first married, that he had once written to me after a quarrel and been too proud to ask whether it had arrived. We were both too proud. I left Mangaluru without knowing if he had forgiven me, and he followed me to Udupi to say that he had."

"Then the letter was unnecessary," Gopal said, and he felt oddly foolish.

"No. It was not." She held out her hand. "May I?"

She did not hurry. She slit the flap with a hairpin from her bun, and read standing at the window while the light turned from gold to rose. Once she laughed softly. Once she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth.

"He says," she said finally, "that he is sorry for what he said about my brother, and that he will wait outside the dispensary every evening until I come out, and that if I do not come he will go on waiting anyway. He was twenty-three. He signed it with a drawing of a bicycle."

The priest had slipped away. The kettle was singing in the kitchen.

"I am sorry it took forty years," Gopal said.

"The post office kept its promise," said Rosalind. "It only took the long road." She folded the letter into its envelope and tucked it inside her blouse, close to her heart. "Will you take coffee, Mr. Shenoy? Anand would have wanted to give you a drawing of a bicycle as well."

Gopal stayed for an hour, and when he caught the evening bus home he was smiling at nothing, a retired postmaster on the Sunday evening of his life.
