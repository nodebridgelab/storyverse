The First Hire

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

A solo founder hires his first employee and realises, with a start, that the company now has a culture.

Chapter 1: Onboarding

There was no onboarding document.

Varun had not thought about this until Tanveer arrived at nine-fifteen on a Monday morning and sat down across from him in the two-person office in HSR Layout—which was technically a room above a printing shop—with an expectant expression that was both professional and slightly helpless.

Varun had been building FieldTrack, a logistics tracking tool for SME distributors, for fourteen months by himself. In those fourteen months he had developed extremely precise opinions about every aspect of the product and almost no capacity for explaining any of them to another person, because there had been no other person. He had a customer base of eleven companies. He had a codebase that he understood completely and had documented not at all. He had a daily routine that involved working from seven-thirty until whenever he stopped, eating at his desk, and communicating with customers via WhatsApp in a style that was extremely casual and apparently highly effective.

None of this was written down anywhere.

"So," he said to Tanveer. "Let me show you the product."

Tanveer was twenty-six, came from a mid-sized logistics company, and had taken the FieldTrack job for a ten-thousand-rupee pay cut because he believed the product was solving a real problem and he wanted to be in on something from the beginning. He had said this in the interview with the kind of sincerity that Varun had initially been suspicious of and had since decided was genuine. He was, by Varun's estimation, significantly better at customer communication than Varun was, and probably better at the kind of structured thinking that Varun had been avoiding because he found it boring.

Varun spent an hour walking him through the product. Then another hour on the customer base. Then he explained the WhatsApp communication style, which he described as 'casual but reliable,' and which Tanveer listened to with an expression that was entirely neutral and which Varun could not read.

"Can I ask," Tanveer said, when the walk-through finished, "what do you want me to do today? Specifically."

Varun had not thought about this either. He had thought about Tanveer's salary, his probation period, his broad role as 'customer success and operations.' He had not thought about Monday.

"What do you think needs doing?" he said, which was a deflection masquerading as a management philosophy.

Tanveer looked at him for a moment. Then he opened a notebook—an actual paper notebook, which was somehow reassuring—and said: "Can I spend today documenting what you just walked me through? So we have it written down."

"Yes," Varun said. "Please."

By the end of the day, Tanveer had produced twelve pages of notes that were more coherent than anything Varun had in his own head about how the business worked.

Chapter 2: Culture by Default

The thing about being a solo founder, Varun was discovering, was that you got to be inconsistent without consequences.

He was a late replier to some emails and an immediate replier to others, based on a system that made sense to him and that he had never had to explain. He worked late on Thursdays for no particular reason and took Friday afternoons off when he could. He sometimes spent entire days on technical problems that were interesting rather than urgent, because he was accountable to himself and his own interest was a legitimate input. He communicated with customers in a way that mixed formal updates with personal enquiries—one customer's daughter had been sick and Varun had followed up on that before the product conversation, which had produced a loyalty that no SLA could have generated.

All of this was his culture. He just hadn't known it was a culture because there had been no one else to see it as a pattern.

Now there was Tanveer, and Tanveer was watching.

Not in a judgmental way—Varun didn't feel observed in an uncomfortable sense. But he noticed that Tanveer was quietly building a model of how things worked here, and that model was going to become the model of what FieldTrack was as a company. When Tanveer replied to his first customer email, he used a tone that was professional and warm but not as informal as Varun's. Varun read the reply and thought: that's a better email than mine. He also thought: this is now the tone we have.

He had a conversation with Tanveer in the third week about the WhatsApp communication style. He said: "I've been very casual with customers. I'm not sure if that's the right register for where we're going."

Tanveer said: "It's working. Eleven customers, no churn, three of them have referred someone. I think the casual approach is a feature, not a gap. I'd keep it."

"But you're not as casual as I am," Varun said.

"I'm building toward it," Tanveer said. "I don't know these people yet."

This was exactly the right answer and Varun found it slightly disorienting, because the right answer had come from someone other than himself.

At the end of the first month, Tanveer proposed a weekly check-in—thirty minutes, Mondays at ten, no agenda document required but a list of three things each of them was working on that week. Varun agreed without thinking about it much. After the first two check-ins, he realised that the rhythm was making him more organised, not because he'd decided to be more organised but because he was now accountable for saying what he was doing in advance.

This was what another person in the room did to you. It wasn't management. It was just visibility, in both directions.

Chapter 3: What They Agreed

In the third month, a customer called Shukla & Sons, which was their largest account by revenue, called Varun directly to complain.

The complaint was about a feature that Tanveer had promised was coming in the next release. The feature was not, in Varun's current plan, coming in the next release. It was on a list. It might make the release after that.

Varun handled the customer call—Shukla Sr. was agitated but also fundamentally reasonable, and the conversation ended with a commitment to a specific timeline that Varun knew was tight but achievable. When he got off the call, he had the first conversation with Tanveer that was not entirely comfortable.

"You told them next release," he said.

"I said 'we're targeting next release,'" Tanveer said. He was not defensive—he was being precise. "Which was true when I said it. I flagged it in the notes from that call two weeks ago."

Varun looked it up. Tanveer had flagged it. He had not read the notes closely enough to see the flag.

"Okay," Varun said. "My fault on the follow-through. But I need to know before we make any timeline commitments to customers. Not because you've done it wrong—you were technically correct—but because if it gets to a customer before I know about it, I can't manage the expectation if something changes."

"That makes sense," Tanveer said. "Do you want a separate flag for anything timing-related?"

"Yes. And cc me on the email."

"Done."

It took five minutes. No one was upset in a lasting way. The thing that struck Varun afterward was how different this was from how he'd expected his first difficult management conversation to go—how he had imagined it would be a scene of some kind, a moment of authority asserted, a lesson learned dramatically.

Instead it was just two people figuring out a process gap. It was exactly as hard as it needed to be and no harder.

By month four, FieldTrack had added three customers, all from referrals. Two of them had specifically mentioned talking to Tanveer as the thing that made them decide to sign. Varun looked at the numbers and felt two things simultaneously: genuine pride, and the realisation that the company was no longer only his.

This was not a loss. It was a little unsettling and also entirely the point. He had hired someone because he wanted the company to be more than one person could be, and then it had become that, and now he was going to have to keep learning how to be the person who made space for that.

He booked a lunch for the two of them at the place below the printing shop, which was a small south Indian place that they'd both been separately going to every day and had somehow never gone to together. He told Tanveer the first three months' numbers. Tanveer said they were good. Varun said they were good enough. They argued, mildly, about what 'good enough' meant. It was the best conversation they'd had.
