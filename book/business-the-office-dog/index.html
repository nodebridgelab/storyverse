The Office Dog

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

A scraggly stray named Biscuit wanders into a Bengaluru agency and becomes its true centre of gravity.

Chapter 1: Biscuit

The dog arrived on a Tuesday in February, which was not notable except that it was the day of the quarterly billing review and everyone was in a mood.

Suresh, who handled operations for Lighthouse Creative—a nine-person brand and digital agency in Indiranagar—was the first one to notice the dog sitting outside the glass door, looking in with the focused patience of someone waiting for a delayed flight. It was a medium-sized brown dog of no particular breed, with an ear that folded forward and a general expression of mild enquiry. It was not thin enough to be starving but had the look of an animal accustomed to solving its own problems.

Suresh opened the door to shoo it away and the dog walked in.

This was, in retrospect, the defining moment for what the company became over the next year. At the time it was simply a nuisance. Meghna, the founder, was on a call in her glass office and looked up at the commotion with the expression of someone who has allocated zero cognitive bandwidth to this situation. Pawan from design made a sound that was half alarm and half recognition. Rashida from accounts actually moved her chair back.

The dog walked to the centre of the open floor, turned around once in a small circle, and lay down.

They named it Biscuit, provisionally, meaning to decide something more considered later. No one ever did.

For the first week there was disagreement about the arrangement. Meghna said that a client-facing agency with a dog wandering around during meetings was not professional. Suresh pointed out that Biscuit had already slept through two client calls without incident. Rashida, who had been the most alarmed on day one, had by day three developed a habit of feeding Biscuit small pieces of her breakfast before the others arrived. This was not mentioned in the discussions about the dog's future, but it was noticed.

The vote—there was an actual informal vote—was seven in favour of keeping Biscuit, one abstention (Meghna, who didn't vote either way, which everyone correctly interpreted as permission), and one strong in-principle objection from Pawan that converted into a yes when he spent a Thursday afternoon working late alone with the dog sleeping under his desk and reported the experience as "not terrible."

Suresh bought a dog bed, food, and a water bowl. He put them near the back window where the afternoon sun came in. By the end of the second week, Biscuit had moved the bed to a position under Rashida's desk and this arrangement became permanent.

Chapter 2: What the Dog Changed

The first thing anyone noticed was that people arrived earlier.

This was not a dramatic shift. They were not a team that had problems with punctuality exactly, but there was the standard drift of office mornings—people filtering in between nine and ten, the first real conversation happening sometime around the coffee machine, the day genuinely beginning at ten-thirty for most of them. After Biscuit, the drift narrowed. People who wanted to see the dog before the day became properly busy started coming in at nine, sometimes before. Rashida, who had always been an eight-thirty person, now regularly arrived at eight and took Biscuit for a short walk around the block, which she did not tell anyone about for three weeks and which then became well known because Pawan saw them from the road and put it on the company WhatsApp group.

The second thing was the client reaction, which surprised Meghna most of all.

The first client to meet Biscuit was a mid-sized jewellery brand whose marketing head, Fatima, had come in for a brand strategy presentation. Biscuit had wandered into the conference room midway through and sat down next to Fatima's chair with complete assurance. Meghna had begun to apologise and Fatima had said, with genuine warmth: "Don't you dare move him." The meeting ran forty minutes over its scheduled time, and not because of the deck. Fatima had later told her team that Lighthouse Creative was 'a good culture' company, and two referrals had followed in three months.

This happened again, in variations, with other clients. Not every client was a dog person—one had a polite but firm conversation about allergies that resulted in Biscuit being walked by Suresh during that specific account's site visits—but the dog created something that Meghna couldn't have designed intentionally: a reason for clients to feel differently about the office than other offices they visited.

The third thing was internal, and harder to measure.

The agency, like most agencies, had the rhythmic stress of deadlines and client demands that created a constant background noise of frustration. People being short with each other. Conversations that started productively and turned into territory disputes. The specific tension that came from everyone working on creative output under pressure, where everyone was simultaneously the expert and the victim of someone else's opinion.

Biscuit did not solve any of this. But the dog provided something that meetings and offsites had failed to provide: a reason to stop. When Pawan and Anita were in the middle of a feedback loop that was going badly, and Biscuit came over and pushed his nose against Pawan's knee, Pawan bent down to pet the dog and the conversation had a pause in it that let both of them breathe. This happened in different forms across the team, over months, often unnoticed, always useful.

Meghna watched it and said nothing for a long time, because saying it would have made it into a policy, and that was clearly not what it was.

Chapter 3: The Gravity

In August, the agency was approached by a holding company that wanted to acquire a majority stake. The process was serious—NDA, financial review, three rounds of meetings with the buyers' team, a valuation exercise that took two weeks and was conducted partly in spreadsheets and partly in whispered conversations between Meghna and Suresh at the coffee machine.

The buyers liked the client list and the talent. They had concerns about scalability and process documentation. Their culture assessment—done via a consultant who spent two days in the office asking everyone structured questions—concluded that Lighthouse had a "high-trust, relationship-led culture" and that "systematising this for a scaled entity would present challenges."

The consultant had met Biscuit and had written, in the actual report, in a footnote: 'Informally, the team culture appears to centre around a shared emotional anchor (an office dog), which is an unusual but functionally effective bonding mechanism in a small team setting.'

Meghna read this footnote three times. She found it funny and also, somehow, exactly right.

The acquisition talks collapsed in October over equity terms. The holding company wanted sixty percent; Meghna wasn't willing to go above forty-nine. They parted on cordial terms with the understanding that the door was open. Meghna felt more relieved than disappointed, which told her something about what she actually wanted.

Biscuit had been present for exactly one of the acquisition meetings—the first one, before the seriousness of the process had fully set in. He had spent most of the meeting asleep under the table and had, at one point, snored loud enough that the lead buyer had laughed. The laughter had been real and had, briefly, made the conversation feel like something between people rather than a transaction.

Meghna thought about this sometimes.

By December, the team had grown to twelve. Four of the new people were hired in the second half of the year, and all four had, at various points in the hiring process, asked about the dog. Not as a deciding factor—they were professionals—but as a detail they noticed, a data point about what kind of place this was.

Biscuit was four years old by Suresh's estimate, which was difficult to confirm with any precision. He had filled out from the lean, provisional-looking animal who had walked in on the billing review day and now moved through the office with the authority of tenure. He slept under Rashida's desk and took walks with Suresh in the morning and allowed Pawan to use him as a footrest when the design reviews ran long and Pawan's back hurt.

There were hard months still. Agency work didn't stop being agency work because there was a dog in the room. Clients were still difficult. Timelines were still impossible. Meghna still lay awake at two in the morning thinking about cashflow.

But the dog was there in the morning when people came in, and that was its own kind of answer to something no one had known they were asking.
