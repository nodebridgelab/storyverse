The Stepwell Cipher

by STORYVERSE Originals · Mystery & Adventure · 3 chapters · Completed

Isha Rathore finds a rhyme folded inside her great-grandfather's letters, and it seems to describe a hidden thing in a ruined Jaipur stepwell. Counting arches by lantern light, she works out the one clue the family had missed for a century.

Chapter 1: The Rhyme in the Envelope

The letters had been tied in a saffron cloth and forgotten in the bottom of a tin trunk, and Isha Rathore, home from college for the monsoon holidays, found them while hunting for an old photograph.

Her great-grandfather Hari Singh had been a clerk to the royal treasury in Jaipur, a careful man who wrote four-page letters about nothing and signed them with a flourish. Isha read three, yawning. The fourth had a folded paper tucked inside, and on the paper was a rhyme.

Seven arches facing the morning,

Count from the one that holds no warning.

Where the lamp-niche meets the stone of three,

Look under what the water cannot see.

"Dadi," she called. "What is this?"

Her grandmother came shuffling in from the kitchen, wiping her hands. She read the rhyme and snorted. "Your great-grandfather loved riddles. He used to say the family had a treasure, and we laughed at him, because we lost the haveli in nineteen fifty."

"Where would arches face the morning?"

"A stepwell, I suppose." Dadi frowned. "There was a ruined one near the old water gate, beyond the Amer road. Surya Kund. Children are told to stay away. Too deep, too many snakes."

Isha was nineteen and had never been told to stay away from anything for long. By Saturday she had borrowed her cousin Dev's torch and tied a rope around her waist, which Dev insisted on holding.

Surya Kund was a square well of pale sandstone, its steps dropping in neat triangular flights down to green water. The rainy season had filled it to the third landing. Arches ran along the eastern wall like a row of dark mouths, and Isha began to count.

Chapter 2: Seven Arches

There were nine arches, not seven.

She counted them three times. Dev, peering over the edge, said it was a bad sign. Isha sat on the cool step and read the rhyme again.

"Seven arches facing the morning," she said aloud. "Maybe two of them don't count. Count from the one that holds no warning."

"Warning?" said Dev. "A sign?"

"A carving." She went back along the wall, shining the torch. Three of the arches had small stone figures above them, a snake, a bird and an elephant. A fourth had a dark stain where something had once been bolted to the stone. The rest were plain.

"A warning would be the snake," said Dev.

"It might be." Isha stared at the arch under the snake, then at the one beside it. And then she noticed a thing her great-grandfather would have loved. The arch under the stain held a faded painted word in old Hindi, almost eaten away: Sawdhan. Beware.

"That is the warning. The one that holds a warning, not the one that holds none." She stood. "The rhyme says count from the one that holds no warning. So I start at the arch beside the warning, the next one along."

She counted seven arches from there, going south, and found herself standing in front of an arch with a small lamp niche cut into its side, exactly at shoulder height. Below it, set into the floor of the landing, was a stone slab carved with three petals.

"Stone of three," said Isha, and her voice shook a little.

Chapter 3: What the Water Cannot See

They knelt, the two of them, and Dev worked a pocketknife into the crack around the slab. It came up with a groan and a breath of damp, cold air. Beneath was a shallow stone cavity, dry as a bone, and in it sat a flat tin box wrapped in oilcloth.

"What if it's gold?" whispered Dev.

"What if it's snakes?" said Isha, and lifted the lid.

It was not gold, and it was not snakes. Inside lay a bundle of folded papers, brown at the edges, and a second smaller envelope, with her great-grandfather's flourish across the front. She opened the envelope first.

My dear descendants, it began. If you read this, you are as stubborn as I was. You will think I have hidden riches. I have hidden something better. When the new government came, they told us the haveli had no deed, and we lost it. But I kept the deed. Here it is, with the stamp of the old treasury and three witnesses. I could not trust a bank or a cupboard. I trusted the water, which keeps everything.

Below the letter lay the deed itself, stamped and signed, in crisp black ink that the dry air had preserved for a century. A property in the old city, a courtyard house with a well and a neem tree.

Isha read it twice and began to laugh, helplessly, with tears running down her face. "The haveli," she managed. "Dev, it is still ours. Or it would have been."

"Is it still there?" asked Dev.

"I do not know. But Dadi will want to see this." She wrapped the papers in the oilcloth, and they climbed the long flight of steps back into the light. At the top she paused and looked down at the arches, nine of them, facing the sun that was only now coming over the hill. "Seven, if you count the right way," she said, and went to find her grandmother.
