The Acquisition Offer

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

Forty-eight hours, a number that changes everything, and a founder who cannot decide what she owes herself.

Chapter 1: The Number

The email arrived at nine-twelve on a Thursday morning and Deepa read it twice at her desk before she walked to the bathroom to read it a third time in private.

It was from Sanjay Kapoor, Chief Strategy Officer at Nexus Commerce, a publicly listed e-commerce infrastructure company. Nexus had been a customer for eighteen months. She knew Sanjay slightly—they had met twice at industry events and he had been on a panel she'd attended at a Nasscom event in February. The email was direct. Nexus was interested in acquiring CartLogic, the inventory management tool Deepa had built over five years. The valuation they had in mind was twenty-two crore rupees. They were prepared to move quickly if she was interested. They needed an indication in forty-eight hours.

Deepa stood at the bathroom mirror for a while.

Twenty-two crore. Her personal stake in CartLogic was sixty-four percent, accounting for the angel round she'd closed in year two. That was fourteen crore rupees. After tax, after the legal costs of a transaction, after the small adjustments that lawyers always found reasons to make, something in the range of ten to eleven crore would reach her personal account.

She was thirty-seven years old. Her parents were in a rental in Nagpur. Her daughter was four. Her husband ran a small architecture practice that had good months and difficult ones. They owned no property. She had been drawing a salary from CartLogic for three years that was comfortable by Nagpur standards and modest by Bengaluru ones.

She walked back to her desk and closed the email. She opened it again.

She told no one for three hours, which was unusual for her—she was a talker, a founder who processed decisions out loud, whose team had learned to read the stage of a problem by how many times she had walked into the main room to think out loud about it. This one she held.

At lunchtime she called Prashant, her co-founder who held eighteen percent. He was in Chennai for a customer visit. She told him on a call, standing on the office terrace where it was loud from the street below.

He said: "What do you want to do?"

She said: "I don't know yet."

He said: "Then let's figure it out."

Chapter 2: What She Wanted

In the evening she made a list—not a pros and cons list, which she found reductive, but a list of what she actually wanted, which was harder and different.

She wanted to not worry about the company's accounts payable in the way she currently worried about them, which was to say with the specific, low-grade physical tension that lived just below her left shoulder blade and had been there for about two years. She wanted her parents to move to Bengaluru, which required money she didn't currently have in a predictable form. She wanted a house.

She also wanted CartLogic to become the thing she had spent five years imagining it becoming, which was not the same thing as it being a product line inside a large company. The Nexus acquisition, by the nature of these things, would mean integration. A roadmap decided by people who had priorities she didn't currently share. A team that would be folded into Nexus's engineering and product structure, meaning some of them would stay and some wouldn't, and she wouldn't have much say in which was which.

She thought about Ritu, who had been her first product hire and who had grown into a genuinely exceptional PM. She thought about Shiv and Aakash from engineering, who had been with her since year two. She thought about what it would feel like to tell them 'we've been acquired' and to watch them do the math on their own futures.

Then she thought about what it would feel like to say no to fourteen crore, or eleven crore, or whatever the actual post-tax number was, and to go back to the office on Friday and worry about the accounts payable.

She called her mother.

Her mother listened carefully and said: "You built something real. Whatever you decide, that's yours."

This was not useful advice in an operational sense and was exactly what she needed to hear.

She called her lawyer at nine PM, which he answered because she paid him to answer at nine PM.

She asked about the forty-eight-hour timeline. He said it was a negotiating tactic and she had more time than that if she pushed back professionally. She asked about the valuation. He said CartLogic's revenue multiples would support a higher number, and that twenty-two crore was a reasonable opening position for an acquirer to take. He advised her to counter.

She asked: "What's the realistic ceiling?"

He thought for a moment. "If the product data room comes out clean and they're genuinely strategic about it—maybe twenty-eight, twenty-nine. Thirty is a stretch."

She did the math again with the new number. The difference was significant. Eleven crore versus fourteen. Houses looked different at those numbers. Her parents' situation looked different.

Chapter 3: Forty-Eight Hours

She sent Sanjay an email at eleven o'clock that night.

It said: Thank you for the expression of interest. I'd like to understand more about the strategic rationale before indicating a direction. I'm also asking my advisors to review the valuation parameters—preliminary read is that we see the business somewhat differently. Can we schedule a call for tomorrow afternoon?

She spent Friday morning with Prashant, who had taken a six AM flight back from Chennai. They talked for four hours. He asked her the question she had been asking herself: 'Is this about the money or the company?'

She said: 'Both. I don't think separating them is honest.'

He said: 'Good answer.'

The call with Sanjay was at three. He was warmer than his email had been, which suggested he wanted this. She asked about integration plans. He was honest: the product roadmap would be absorbed into Nexus's category management strategy, which meant some features would be deprioritised and some would be accelerated. The team would go through a ninety-day integration review, which was the phrasing they used for 'we'll keep the people we need.'

She asked about her own role.

He said: 'We'd want you in a category leadership position for two years. After that, your earn-out vests and the arrangement is yours to evaluate.'

Two years was a long time to work for someone else's strategy. She knew this as he said it. She also knew that two years with an earn-out attached to it looked a specific way in financial terms.

She asked for twenty-eight crore.

Sanjay said he'd take it back to his committee.

He came back at six with twenty-five five. She accepted.

She told Prashant first, then called Ritu and Shiv and Aakash individually. The calls were hard—not because anyone was angry, but because they were real. Ritu cried a little, which she apologised for and which was fine. Shiv asked specific questions about the integration review, which Deepa answered as honestly as she could. Aakash said: 'You've been fair to us for five years. I trust this decision.'

She didn't feel good exactly. She felt like herself at the end of a long, serious day, which was close enough.

The transaction closed four months later after the expected legal complexity. The number that arrived in her account was fifteen point two crore after everything, which was different from her various calculations and also more than enough.

She took two weeks off before starting at Nexus. She flew her parents to Bengaluru. They looked at three apartments. They found one her mother said yes to immediately, which was the right signal. They made an offer.

CartLogic's interface still looked like CartLogic. She checked it sometimes, late at night, the same way you'd look at a photograph of something that happened and is complete and still, in some quiet way, yours.
