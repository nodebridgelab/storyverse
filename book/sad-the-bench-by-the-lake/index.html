The Bench by the Lake

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

Two elderly widowers find each other every morning on the same park bench without ever deciding to.

Chapter 1: The Arrangement

The bench was not a special bench. It was one of four wooden benches on the eastern path around Sunder Lake in the colony park, painted green the way all the benches were painted green, with a small plaque that said it had been donated in 1997 by someone whose name had become illegible through weather. It faced the water and caught the morning sun.

Mohan Krishnamurthy had been sitting on this bench every morning since his wife Sumitra died, which was eleven months ago. He came at seven, sometimes seven-fifteen if he was slow with his morning tea. He sat for between forty-five minutes and an hour, watching the light on the water and the birds and the various walkers and the one dog whose owner let it run off leash in what was technically a no-dogs area, which Mohan found mildly irritating but not enough to say anything about.

He came because Sumitra used to walk here. In the last few years of her life when walking had become slow and somewhat effortful, she had walked this path with her friend Geeta, slowly, and Mohan had sometimes met them at this bench for tea from a thermos. He did not have a thermos. He sat without tea. But the bench was hers in some way that he could not rationalize and did not try to.

Balraj Sood appeared in October.

Mohan noticed him because he also sat on the bench — not asked, just sat, at the far end, as if arriving at his own unrelated conclusion about where to be in the mornings. He was perhaps five or ten years older than Mohan, with white hair and the careful movements of someone whose knees had opinions. He brought a newspaper that he read for a while and then set on the bench beside him and seemed to forget about.

They did not speak on the first day. Or the second. Or the third.

By the end of the first week they had developed the specific comfort of two people who have agreed, without words, on an arrangement.

Chapter 2: What They Talked About

The first conversation happened in the third week, in November, when the gardeners came to do something noisy to the path nearby and Mohan said, without thinking: they do this every season and never fix the actual problem.

Balraj lowered his newspaper. He looked at the gardeners. He said: what is the actual problem?

Mohan pointed. The drainage. That area floods whenever it rains heavily. They trim around it instead of fixing the drainage.

Balraj looked at the drainage situation with the expression of a man who has had opinions about civic maintenance his entire life and has been waiting for an occasion. He said: someone must have filed a complaint.

I filed a complaint, Mohan said, two years ago.

Balraj laughed. Not a polite laugh. A real one, the kind that comes from recognizing something.

After that they talked most mornings. Not for the whole hour, and not about large things. They talked about the colony, which was changing — new apartment buildings going up at the eastern edge, families they didn't recognize. They talked about the dog and its owner, who remained a point of mild shared grievance. They talked about the news, when the news felt worth talking about, which was increasingly rare.

Mohan learned that Balraj had been an engineer, retired, and that his wife had died two years ago after a long illness, and that his son was in Canada and called every Sunday with the kind of cheerful efficiency that Balraj understood as love and also sometimes found difficult.

Balraj learned that Mohan had been in bank administration for thirty years, that he had a daughter in Hyderabad and a son in the same city who tried to coordinate visits but mostly visited separately, and that Sumitra had made a particular kind of karanji at Diwali that Mohan had been unable to recreate despite three attempts.

Neither of them talked about being alone. They were both perfectly aware of being alone. It did not require discussion.

Chapter 3: The Rain Morning

In February there was an unusual rain — not monsoon, just a February rain that came without warning and thoroughly, as if the sky had made an error in scheduling.

Mohan went to the park anyway. He was not sure why. He stood under the large peepal tree on the eastern path for a while, watching the rain on the lake, which was beautiful in a way he could not exactly justify since he was also getting wet.

Balraj arrived in a raincoat. He stood under the peepal tree without comment. They watched the rain together.

After a while Balraj said: my wife used to walk in the rain.

Mohan looked at him.

She said she had spent sixty years trying to stay dry and it was overrated, Balraj said. This was in the last year, when she was not well. She walked in our neighbourhood whenever it rained. The neighbours thought she had gone eccentric. Maybe she had.

Mohan thought about this. He thought about Sumitra at this bench, in her slow years, with Geeta, telling him things he hadn't always listened to carefully enough.

Sumitra would have liked this rain, he said. She liked unusual things. Things that were out of place.

They stood under the tree until the rain slowed and then stopped in the way February rains do, as if they had said what they had to say. The lake was very still afterward, the kind of still that only follows rain.

They walked to the bench and sat down. The bench was wet, but neither of them moved to leave. Balraj put his newspaper — completely ruined — on the far end of the bench and left it there. Mohan did not say anything about the newspaper. He watched the light coming back across the lake, the clouds breaking, that particular white and gold of a morning after rain.

He was seventy-one years old. He had been a widower for eleven months and three days. He was sitting on a bench by a lake in the early morning with a man he had known for four months and did not know well and knew, in the way you sometimes know things, better than he knew most people he had known for decades.

The bench dried slowly in the returning sun. The dog came back, off its lead as always. Somewhere across the lake a bird called and was answered. Mohan and Balraj sat together in the ordinary morning, which was not nothing, which was, in fact, everything they had.
