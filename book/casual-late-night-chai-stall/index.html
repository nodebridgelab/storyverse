Late Night Chai Stall

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

At 2 AM outside a Bengaluru software park, Raju's chai stall hears the truths no open-plan office ever could.

Chapter 1: Raju and His Regulars

Raju set up his stall at half past ten, when the first shift was ending and the second shift was settling in, and he packed it by four AM when the night bled into the grey hour that wasn't quite morning yet. He had been doing this for seven years, first near the BPO on the outer ring road, then — after a complicated business with a municipality notice — here, on the pavement outside the back gate of Sapphire Tech Park, Bengaluru.

The stall was three elements: a gas burner, a large aluminium vessel, and a plank balanced between two plastic crates that served as the counter. He had a folding stool he did not sit on because he was always moving — stirring, pouring, washing glasses, cutting ginger. He had learned over seven years to read what kind of chai someone needed from how they walked toward his stall. The fast walk meant give it quick, I have ten minutes. The slow walk meant they needed to stand here for a while.

His regulars were mostly from the software park — the night-shift people, the QA teams who tested things in other time zones, the support people who talked to customers in the middle of the night who were in time zones where it was afternoon. They came alone or in pairs. Rarely in groups — groups gathered inside, in the cafeteria, which was brighter and louder. The stall drew people who needed the particular privacy of the street: open air, low light, a stranger who would make chai and not ask questions.

This was the thing about the stall that Raju had understood without being told. The not-asking. People came to him because he was not their colleague, not their manager, not anyone who would carry what they said back inside. He was the chai man. He remembered orders but not conversations, by a social agreement so universal it required no articulation.

He was wrong about this last part, actually. He remembered conversations. He was just good about who he told.

The people he remembered most: Priya the senior QA lead who came every night at 1 AM and stood with her chai facing the road rather than the building, like she was deciding something. Roshan the young developer who talked to his mother on the phone every break, voice dropping to something younger when she answered. Devika and Santhosh who came together most nights and didn't talk to each other much at the stall, which told a story all its own.

Chapter 2: What People Said at 2 AM

Priya came on a Tuesday and ordered two chais, which she hadn't done before. Raju made them without comment. She stood with one in each hand — she wasn't waiting for anyone, just holding both, which was stranger still.

"Resign kar dun kya," she said. Not to him exactly. To the road.

He had been making chai for long enough to know that this was not a question requiring an answer. He stirred his vessel.

"Seven years," she said. "I have been QA lead for three. Before that I was senior QA. Before that I was junior QA." She looked at the second chai, the one she wasn't drinking. "I keep thinking next year will be different and I keep being wrong."

Raju poured a fresh glass for the man who had just walked up — short-circuit, quick walk, two minutes — and gave it to him and took the twelve rupees and turned back to the vessel.

"The thing is," Priya said, "I'm good at it. I don't hate it. I just don't love it enough to give it this many nights."

She drank the second chai herself, after all. Then left.

Roshan came at 2 AM on a Friday and did not call his mother. He stood with his chai and Raju noticed — in the peripheral way of a man who watches people without watching people — that he looked lighter than usual. When asked if it was the usual adrak wali, he said no, make it elaichi, and said it with the air of someone who had made a decision.

"I put in my papers today," he said. "I'm going back. My amma is alone and I've been here two years and she never complains and that's the problem."

Raju made the elaichi chai. The cardamom smell rose clean in the night air.

"She'll complain that you left the good job," Raju said. He didn't do this often — speak — but sometimes a thing needed saying.

"Yes," Roshan said. "That's how I'll know she's happy."

Devika came alone one night — the first time in months that Santhosh wasn't with her. She drank two chais, standing very still, and Raju did not ask. She offered nothing. But at the end she said, "He got promoted. He's going to Singapore for two years." A pause. "I'm happy for him. I'm saying that because it's true and because it doesn't sound like it when I say it."

"Dono sach ho sakte hain," Raju said. Both can be true. He had heard this phrase from his wife once and had kept it for the right moment.

She looked at him. "Yes," she said. "They can."

Chapter 3: The Stall in Morning Light

There was a man — Raju didn't know his name, never asked — who came every night at three and ordered one chai and drank it in complete silence and left. He had done this for four months. He was not the only silent regular but he was the most consistent. Some nights he looked tired in the normal way of people who work nights. Some nights he looked tired in the other way, the one that is not about sleep.

One Thursday, three-fifteen AM, he came and ordered his chai and then, surprising both of them, said: "Kitne saal se ho yahan?" How many years have you been here?

"Saat saal," Raju said. Seven years.

The man absorbed this. "Wahi jagah pe?" Same spot?

"Nahi, pehle ring road ke paas tha. Wahan se shift karna pada." No, I was near the ring road first. Had to move from there.

"But you stayed in this work."

"Haan."

The man drank his chai. "My father thought this job would make me happy," he said. Not the night-shift job, Raju understood — the broader thing. The city, the software, the life built for someone else's expectations. "I thought so too. I thought I just needed more time to feel it."

Raju had heard many versions of this at this stall. The city was full of people who had come for the salaries and stayed for the salaries and were still waiting for the other part to arrive.

"Chai se khush ho?" he asked. Do you like chai? It was a small question, almost a joke, but not entirely.

The man looked at his glass. "Haan," he said, with something like surprise. "This chai I like."

"Toh yahan aate rehna." So keep coming here. It was the most Raju had said to anyone in one conversation in weeks.

When three AM gave way to four, Raju began packing. This was his favourite part of the night — not because it was over but because it was complete. The vessel cooled. The burner was cleaned. The plank was lifted off its crates. The crates were stacked. He loaded everything onto his handcart.

The sky was still dark but making promises. A delivery truck passed. A dog trotted by with purpose. The software park behind him was lit from inside — it never went fully dark — and as he wheeled his cart toward the bus stop where he caught the 4:15 to his own neighbourhood, he passed the gate where his people had been coming all night, one by one, to stand under a fluorescent tube and say the things that would otherwise go unsaid.

He thought, not for the first time, that his was the better job. The nights were long, the margins were thin, and his knees were troubling him this winter. But he spent every night at the place where people came when they had something real to carry, and they gave it to him for the duration of a chai, and they walked back lighter. He didn't know if that counted as meaning. But it felt like it might.
