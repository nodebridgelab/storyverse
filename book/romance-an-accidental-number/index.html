An Accidental Number

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

A mistyped number starts a six-month conversation that neither of them can quite bring themselves to end.

Chapter 1: Wrong Turn

The first message said: Didi are you coming for dinner or not. Maa has been asking since five.

Rohini received this at 7:48 PM on a Thursday in March. She was in Hyderabad. She had no brother, no maa waiting, no dinner. She looked at the number — it was not in her contacts — and typed back: I think you have the wrong number.

A long pause. Then: Oh god. I'm so sorry. My sister's new number and I clearly wrote it wrong. I'm very sorry to have bothered you.

She looked at this for a moment. Then she typed: Hope didi shows up for dinner.

Another pause. Then: She will. She's always late but she always comes. Sorry again.

Rohini put her phone down and ate her own dinner — alone, rice and dal, a Thursday evening — and thought nothing more about it.

At nine-thirty, a message from the unknown number: She came. Dinner was good. Maa made biryani. (Why am I telling a stranger this. Ignore me.)

Rohini read this. She typed: I'm glad the biryani was good. Goodnights.

She saved the number as Wrong Number and went to sleep.

In the morning there was nothing. She checked once and then felt faintly silly for checking and put her phone in her bag. By noon she had forgotten it.

At noon the following Thursday: I think I owe you an introduction since you know about the biryani. I'm Karan. From Lucknow. My sister's number is saved correctly now.

Rohini read this on her lunch break. She thought about not responding — she was thirty-two, she had a job and a routine and no particular need for conversations with wrong numbers. She typed back: Rohini. From Hyderabad originally but living here now. Does your maa make biryani every week?

The reply was immediate: Only on special occasions. But she treats every week like a special occasion. That's the thing.

Rohini read this twice and thought something warm about it. She typed: That sounds like a good thing.

Chapter 2: Routine

By May they were talking every few days, which neither of them had planned. It had arrived naturally the way some things did — one message, then another, then a pattern that they had both accommodated without discussing.

He was a civil servant in Lucknow — district administration, a job he described as ninety percent paperwork and ten percent being the person everyone calls when something breaks in some municipal way. She was a product manager at a software company, which she described as forty percent meetings, forty percent writing things other people would change, and twenty percent actually solving problems.

They talked about their cities. He described Lucknow with the particular love of someone who grew up there and was still discovering it — the old city, the architecture, the way the food was different from anywhere else. She described Hyderabad the way she had come to love it — as an adult city, not inherited but chosen. They talked about their work, their families (his large and centripetal; hers smaller and scattered), the things they were reading or watching or thinking about.

They had never spoken by voice. This was a fact they both noticed. She had thought about calling once — she had looked at his number — and then not called, without fully knowing why. She thought it was something about how the written conversation had its own texture, its own pace, and a voice would change it. He must have had the same thought. He had never called either.

In June he sent a photograph. He said: This is the old market near my office. Thought you might like it. The photograph was beautiful — evening light on an old street, the kind of image that made Hyderabad feel very far away.

She sent a photograph back: the view from her office window, late afternoon, the city spread out in its particular way. She said: This is what I see when the meetings are going badly. He said: I would look at this too.

This was the kind of thing they said. Just barely, just within the boundary. She had begun to notice when they were near the boundary, and to notice also that neither of them crossed it. It was, she thought, like a conversation that had built itself a room and was living comfortably in it and was not ready to open the door.

Chapter 3: September

In September, he sent a message on a Monday afternoon that was different from the usual: I'm going to be in Hyderabad next month. Work thing — a national training program. I'll be there four days. He sent it and then, before she could reply, sent another: This is a strange message to send. You can pretend I didn't send it.

She read both messages. She put her phone down. She picked it up. She typed: Why would I pretend?

His reply was slower than usual. Because we haven't met. Because you might not want to. Because I realized after I sent it that I've been talking to you for six months and I don't actually know what you look like and it's strange but it also doesn't feel strange and I'm not sure what to do with that.

Rohini sat in her office with this message for a long time. She was thirty-two. She had been in one relationship that had ended quietly three years ago and had not been in one since, not for lack of opportunity but for lack of the particular feeling that she'd come to recognize as necessary. She thought about six months of messages, about the photograph of the Lucknow market, about the fact that she had started checking her phone at times that were not her usual times.

She typed: I have a favourite tea stall near my office. They know me there, which means they'll bring tea immediately and not hover. We could go there. If that feels right.

A pause. Then: Yes. That feels right.

She sent him the name. He saved it — she knew he had because he said: Good. I've saved it. She put her phone down and felt something uncomplicated and bright, the way October sometimes felt after a long monsoon.

He arrived on a Tuesday. They met outside the tea stall at four-thirty, and she recognized him from nothing — from a voice she hadn't heard, from a face she hadn't seen, from six months of messages — and then she recognized him because he was looking at her the way she was looking at him, with the expression of someone who had known and is now confirming, and he said, 'Rohini,' and she said, 'Karan,' and they went in and the tea came immediately and they talked and the city did its late-afternoon thing around them and neither of them mentioned that they had nearly not sent any message at all.
