The Plant Lady

by STORYVERSE Originals · Casual · 3 chapters · Completed

Retired schoolteacher Mrs. Sharma's balcony jungle quietly becomes the soul of Sunrise Apartments.

Chapter 1: The Green Balcony

Mrs. Kamla Sharma had been retired for six years, and in those six years her balcony had become something else entirely. What had started as two pots of tulsi and a scraggly money plant during her last semester of teaching had expanded, season by season, into a dense, breathing world of its own. There were tomato vines climbing a bamboo trellis she had built herself, using instructions from a YouTube video her nephew had shown her. There was a row of marigolds so orange they seemed to glow at dusk. There were ferns she misted every morning, mint that she harvested twice a week, and a jasmine creeper whose smell drifted into the corridor on warm evenings and made people pause outside their own doors.

She lived in flat 4B of Sunrise Apartments in Pune, fourth floor, west-facing. Her husband had passed five years ago, her son was in Bengaluru, her daughter was in Canada. The building had forty-two flats and she knew perhaps twelve people by name. This had never bothered her in the way people assumed it should. She had her plants, her morning Doordarshan, her weekly phone calls. She had enough.

What she had not expected was the morning Mr. Desai from 4D stopped in the corridor and said, without any preamble, "Your jasmine is something else, Sharmaji."

She had looked up from her watering can. Mr. Desai was a retired bank officer who wore pressed trousers even on Sundays and had, in four years of living three doors apart, said perhaps eleven words to her total. She counted. Good morning, twice. Lift broken again, once. And a handful of nods that barely qualified as communication.

"Thank you," she said, because what else do you say.

"My wife used to grow jasmine," he said. He did not explain the past tense. He did not need to. He looked at the creeper for a moment, then nodded, and walked to the lift.

She thought about that for the rest of the morning. She thought about it while she repotted the aloe vera and while she ate her lunch alone at the kitchen table. There was something in the way he had stopped — not out of politeness but out of something involuntary, the way you stop when a song you recognize from a long time ago comes on the radio in a shop.

The next morning she cut a small sprig of jasmine and left it outside 4D in a little steel bowl she would not miss. She put no note. There was nothing to say that the sprig didn't say better.

She did not know if he found it, because she did not watch the corridor. But two days later she found the steel bowl back outside her door, washed clean and dry.

Chapter 2: The Roof Committee

The WhatsApp group for Sunrise Apartments was called "Sunrise Residents Important Only" and was used almost exclusively for complaints about parking, questions about the society maintenance bill, and the occasional photo of a suspicious wire that someone had noticed near the water tank. Mrs. Sharma was in the group the way she was in many things — present but quiet.

She was therefore surprised when, one Tuesday morning in March, a message appeared from someone called Rohan Mehta — 2A, she believed, young man, worked in IT — saying: "Hey everyone has anyone noticed the plant situation on the 4th floor corridor? Asking because I walked up yesterday instead of taking the lift and honestly it was the best decision I've made this week. The jasmine smell alone."

She stared at her phone. Forty-three people were in the group. Twelve of them responded within the hour. There were three laughing emojis, one heart, and several messages along the lines of yes someone mentioned this, and whose plants are those actually.

"Those are Mrs. Sharma's plants. 4B. She's been growing them for years," typed someone she didn't know.

She did not type anything. She put her phone face-down and went to water the ferns.

But something had shifted. Over the next week, she noticed people she had never seen before walking past her balcony door — which she kept open in the mornings — a little slowly. A young woman with a toddler on her hip stopped one day and said, pointing, "Amol, see, tomato!" with the delight of someone who had not expected to find a tomato on the fourth floor of an apartment building. Mrs. Sharma invited them in and gave the toddler a cherry tomato to hold. He carried it around like a treasure for ten minutes before eating it.

It was around this time that Mrs. Patil from 3A came up with a proposal. She knocked on 4B one evening and stood at the door with the slightly nervous energy of someone who had rehearsed what they were going to say. She was the secretary of the residents' welfare committee. She had, she explained, been thinking about the rooftop. It was currently used for water tanks and old scooter parts belonging to no one traceable. But it was large. And sunny.

"We thought," Mrs. Patil said carefully, "that if you were interested — only if, no pressure at all — we could create a proper terrace garden. The society would fund the pots and the soil. And maybe you could teach some people. Whoever wanted to learn."

Mrs. Sharma looked at her for a moment. She thought about thirty-three years of teaching mathematics to children who mostly did not want to learn mathematics. She thought about the particular pleasure of the moment when something clicked for a student who had been struggling.

"Which Sunday works?" she said.

Chapter 3: What Grows Between People

The first rooftop session had nine people, which was more than Mrs. Sharma had expected and fewer than Mrs. Patil had hoped for. They stood in the morning sun with their chai and their uncertainty, and Mrs. Sharma looked at them — young couple from the ground floor, two teenage boys whose mother had sent them, Mr. Desai with his pressed trousers, a grandmother from 1C who said she used to grow vegetables in her village and had missed it for forty years, Rohan Mehta with his phone already out to take notes — and felt something she recognized from her teaching days.

She started with soil. People always wanted to start with seeds, she had learned, but seeds are the last thing. You start with what you cannot see. She passed around a handful of compost and asked them to smell it. Rohan Mehta looked briefly uncertain about this, then did it anyway. The grandmother from 1C smiled immediately, the way you smile at something familiar that has been lost for a long time.

"Good earth smells alive," Mrs. Sharma said. "That's how you know."

They worked for two hours. By the end there were twelve pots in the ground, a rough plan for what would go where, a rota written in Rohan's neat app, and a loose agreement to meet every other Sunday. The grandmother from 1C, whose name turned out to be Mrs. Gokhale, had taken charge of the corner where they would grow methi and coriander and had opinions about everything, delivered in a mix of Marathi and Hindi that somehow everyone followed.

Mr. Desai had said perhaps forty words across the whole morning, which was still a record for him. He had, though, quietly untangled two plants that had knotted together in their nursery bags and repotted them without being asked, his large retired-banker hands surprisingly careful.

On the way back down the stairs — the lift was broken again — he walked beside Mrs. Sharma. "My wife grew jasmine in our old house in Nashik," he said. "We moved here for the grandchildren. But the grandchildren went to the US." He said this without particular sadness, just as fact. "Good morning, Sharmaji."

"Good morning, Desaiji," she said.

Her balcony was as it always was when she got back — the marigolds burning orange in the late morning light, the mint smelling sharp when she brushed it with her hand, the jasmine waiting for the warmth of the afternoon to release its full strength into the corridor.

She sat with her tea and did not feel less alone, exactly. But she felt the specific not-aloneness of someone whose particular knowledge had turned out to be wanted. This, she thought, was not a small thing. It had never been a small thing, teaching. She had always known that. What she had not expected was that retiring from the classroom was not the same as retiring from knowing how to teach.

She picked up her phone and typed into the group for the first time in six months: "Next Sunday 8 AM. Bring your own chai."

Fifteen people liked it before she put the phone down.
