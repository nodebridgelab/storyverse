The Last All-Hands

by STORYVERSE Originals · Business · 3 chapters · Completed

A CEO stands before his team on their last day and tries to find words that are actually true.

Chapter 1: What to Say

Rahul spent the morning of the last all-hands writing and deleting the same paragraph fifteen times.

The meeting was at two o'clock. It was eleven-fifteen. He was sitting in the company's small conference room—the one with the whiteboard that still had the Q3 OKRs from six months ago on it, because no one had wanted to erase them and he hadn't wanted to ask anyone to—and trying to figure out what you said to twenty-three people whose jobs you had just ended.

He had read the articles. Every founder who'd gone through a shutdown had eventually written about it on Medium or LinkedIn, usually two years later when the pain had converted into lesson. They all said the same things: be honest, be direct, don't make it about yourself, thank the team, acknowledge the difficulty without dramatising it. Good advice. The kind of advice that was easy to give and genuinely hard to do in a room full of people who had believed you when you told them, nine months ago, that the Series A was close.

The Series A had not been close. It had been never, as it turned out, though Rahul had not known that nine months ago and had not been lying. He had been wrong. That was different and also harder in some ways, because lies could be explained and wrongness just sat there.

He opened a new document and typed: 'Thank you for being here.' Then deleted it. Everyone was here because they had to be, because he had sent a calendar invite marked 'mandatory all-hands' at nine in the morning with no description, which was the kind of invite that everyone in tech now knew meant one thing. Half the team had probably already updated their LinkedIn profiles.

He typed: 'I want to start by saying I'm sorry.' He looked at that for a while.

That was true. He was sorry. He was sorry in a deep, tired way that had been building since the last investor call, six weeks ago, when the term sheet they'd been expecting had come back with conditions that made the company mathematically unworkable—a down round that would have wiped out the founding team and most of the angel investors, in exchange for eighteen months of runway that he'd calculated would only get them to the same cliff from a different angle.

He had spent three weeks trying to find another path. He had called every contact in the network. He had spoken to two acquirers who had offered him soft compliments and no term sheets. He had explored a merger that would have saved twelve jobs and ended eleven others, which was a calculation he couldn't resolve.

So he had made the decision. Wound down. Paid out what he could—two weeks of severance per year of service, which was what the lawyers said and which felt entirely inadequate. Sent the letters. Made the individual calls before the group one, which the articles all said was the right thing, and it was, but it also meant that by the time the all-hands arrived everyone already knew and the meeting was a formality and somehow that made it harder.

At one fifty-five he picked up the printout he'd made of the paragraph he'd finally finished writing. He walked out to the main floor.

Chapter 2: The Room

They were all there. Twenty-three people in a room that had been designed for thirty, which meant there were enough chairs but still the particular density of a gathering where no one is quite sure where to look. Some people were on laptops. He didn't mind that. He understood the need to have something to look at that wasn't him.

Ankita from engineering was sitting in the back corner, which was where she always sat, and she had her hands folded in front of her on the table in the way that she had when she was concentrating very hard on being calm. Dev from product had clearly been crying before he came in—his eyes had that particular redness—and had the slightly rigid posture of someone who was not going to cry again. Meena, who handled customer success and had been with the company for just eight months, looked mostly confused, which was fair: she had come in on the upswing and was leaving in a direction she hadn't anticipated.

Rahul stood at the front of the room and looked at them for a moment before he said anything. He didn't do it for effect. He did it because he genuinely needed to see them all, needed to make himself understand that these were specific people in specific circumstances and not an abstraction.

"I'm not going to use a deck," he said. "I don't have slides. I wrote some things down and I'm going to read some of it and maybe not all of it, depending on how it goes."

Some people nodded. Someone in the middle coughed.

"The company is closing. You know that by now—I've spoken to most of you individually over the past two days, and I'm sorry that process took as long as it did, but I wanted to make sure those conversations happened before this one. I didn't want anyone finding out in this room."

He looked at his paper.

"I'm not going to spend a lot of time explaining what went wrong with the business, because that conversation is available for anyone who wants to have it with me one-on-one, and I'll make time for every person in this room if you want that. What I want to say here, to everyone, is simpler and also harder."

He paused. The air conditioning was loud in the silence.

"What I want to say is that I made decisions that I believed were right, and some of them were wrong, and the combination of those things is the reason we're here today. I don't think there's a version of this where I blame the market or the investor environment or our timing, though all of those things are real and all of them contributed. The truth is that I made the call to hold the team together through the last fundraise when the signals were telling me to cut costs. I thought we could get to close. We couldn't. That cost six people their jobs earlier than it should have, and it cost all of you more time than you'll get back."

Dev had looked down at the table. Ankita's hands were still folded.

Chapter 3: After

"I want to thank you," Rahul said. "Not in the way that's a polite ending to a speech. I want to try to be specific."

He looked down at the paper, then back up.

"Ankita—you took what was genuinely a mess of technical debt and built something that actually scaled. The architecture you put in place in year two is the thing that let us serve twenty times the load without a single outage. That's real work and it's permanent even though the company isn't."

Ankita looked at the table. She nodded once.

"Dev—the product decisions you made in the back half of last year when we didn't have a clear market direction were the best calls made in this company during that period. You shipped things when we had no roadmap and almost no budget and you made them good. That's harder than it sounds."

He went through seven more people. Not everyone—the room was too large for that without it becoming a ceremony, which wasn't what this was—but the ones whose specific contributions he could name accurately and without sentimentality. He had thought carefully about who to include and what to say, because he had watched enough closing meetings go wrong by becoming about the founder's feelings rather than the team's work.

When he finished the specific acknowledgements, he set the paper down.

"The severance details are in the email that went out at noon. Your final paychecks process on Friday. The reference letters are being drafted by myself and the leadership team and will be ready by end of this week—if you need a personalised reference call for any specific role, my calendar is open and I mean that without any time limit. If it's three months from now, call me."

He looked at the room.

"I know some of you are angry. That's correct. I know some of you are scared about what comes next, and I wish I could fix that, and I can't. What I can tell you is that the work you did here was real, and people who know this space will know what it means on your CV. We weren't successful as a company but we were not a bad company, and there's a difference."

He paused one more time.

"That's all I had prepared. I'd like to stay in this room for as long as anyone wants to talk. There's no other place I need to be today."

The room was quiet for a moment. Then Dev, who had been staring at the table for most of the meeting, looked up.

"Can we order food?" he asked. "The biryani place nearby does lunch until three."

Something in the room loosened. Someone laughed—not a big laugh, the kind that just releases pressure.

"Yeah," Rahul said. "Let's do that."

They ordered enough for thirty people and ate it in the conference room with the Q3 OKRs still on the whiteboard behind them. Rahul ate more than he expected to. The conversations were real—some hard, some funny, some both. Ankita told him about a bug she'd always meant to fix and never got to, and they laughed about it together. Dev said he was going to take two weeks before he looked for anything.

The last person left at five-thirty. Rahul stayed and sat in the empty room for a while. He didn't feel okay. He felt like himself, which was the closest thing to okay that the day allowed.
