Monsoon Tenant

by STORYVERSE Originals · Sad & Healing · 3 chapters · Completed

In Kochi, retired schoolteacher Varghese rents out his late wife's sewing room to a young nursing student, and finds that grief and a stranger's kindness can share the same small house.

Chapter 1: The Sewing Room

The rain in Kochi did not fall so much as take up residence. By the third week of June, Varghese had stopped noticing it, the way one stops noticing a clock that has ticked for forty years.

He had taught geography at the St. Antony's school for thirty-four years, and he still woke at five to a classroom no one was waiting in. The mornings were the worst. Leelamma used to hum at the stove, off-key, and he used to complain about it.

His wife Leelamma had been gone fourteen months. Her sewing room at the back of the house still held her machine under its cloth cover, her tin of buttons, a half-finished blouse pinned to a dress form in the corner. He had not opened the shutters since she died. His son in Dubai phoned every Sunday and said, Appa, you cannot live like a museum caretaker. Rent the room. Get a person in the house.

So he put up a card at the church gate, and on a grey Tuesday a girl named Reshmi came, dripping, with a steel trunk and a stethoscope looped around her neck like a pendant. She was twenty, in her second year at the nursing college down the road. She looked at the machine and the buttons and said, "I can move all this, Uncle, if it bothers you."

"No," he heard himself say. "Leave them. Only, don't touch the blouse."

She didn't. For two weeks she moved around that room as softly as a visitor in a temple.

He found small kindnesses appearing in the house without being asked. A rinsed cup. The newspaper folded to the crossword. She never commented on the photographs of Leelamma on the wall, and he came to understand this was her way of respecting them.

Chapter 2: Buttons

The trouble came on a night when the power failed. Varghese was sitting in the dark of the front room, listening to the water in the gutters, when he heard a small sound from behind the sewing room door. Not crying exactly. The sound a person makes when she is trying not to.

He knocked. Reshmi opened it with her eyes red. A torch lit the room from below, throwing the dress form's shadow up the wall.

She had failed a practical exam. Her father, a fisherman in Alappuzha, had sold his second boat to pay her fees. "I keep thinking about the boat," she said. "Every time my hands shake."

Varghese stood in the doorway a long minute. Then, without quite deciding to, he crossed the room and lifted the cover from the machine. It was the first time in fourteen months. Underneath, the black iron gleamed from Leelamma's care.

"My wife used to say a steady hand is a trained hand, not a born one," he said. "She taught half the girls in this lane to sew. Sit."

He didn't know how to sew. But he remembered how Leelamma sat, how she fed the cloth. He showed Reshmi from memory, clumsy and wrong in places, and she laughed through her tears at his mistakes. She sewed buttons onto a square of cloth until her fingers stopped trembling. Outside, the rain kept up its long argument with the roof.

Near midnight the power returned all at once, the bulb flaring, and they both flinched and laughed. Her square of buttons was crooked, and she said it looked like a drunken caterpillar. He said Leelamma would have called it a good start.

Chapter 3: Open Shutters

Reshmi passed the repeat exam in August. She came home with the result slip and put it into Varghese's hands before she told her own father.

That evening he opened the shutters. Light, wet and silver, fell across the machine and the pinned blouse. He had wondered for a year whether it would hurt to see the room clearly. It did, a little. It also smelled of cloth and rain and ordinary afternoons, and it did not feel like a tomb. It felt like a place where someone had been happy.

He took the blouse off the dress form at last, carefully, pin by pin, and folded it into the drawer with the buttons. "She would want it finished," he told Reshmi, "but not by us. Some things are allowed to stay half-done."

Reshmi stayed three more years. On Sundays, when his son called, Varghese held the phone toward the sound of the girl humming in the next room and said, "Listen. The house is full." And the rain went on falling, and for the first time it sounded like company.
