Second Attempt

by STORYVERSE Originals · Romance · 3 chapters · Completed

Two people who swore off love meet at a school sports day and spend the afternoon inventing reasons to stay.

Chapter 1: The Relay Race

Sunita had not planned to stay for the whole day. She had come to watch Anika run the 100-metre sprint — her daughter had been training for weeks, had worn out two pairs of shoes — and then she had planned to slip away before the prize-giving, which she had heard could go on until four. She was thirty-eight. She had been divorced for two years. She had things to do.

The sprint was over by ten-thirty. Anika got third, which she received with the intense private disappointment of a nine-year-old who had genuinely expected first, and Sunita was crouching at the edge of the track offering consolation when she became aware that a man was doing the same thing to a boy approximately Anika's age in the next lane.

'Third is really very good,' Sunita said to her daughter.

'Third is bronze,' said the man to his son, apparently following the same script. They glanced at each other across their children.

'I keep telling him practice is the whole point,' the man said, straightening up. He was tall, a little rumpled, wearing the specific exhausted expression of someone who had been cheerful for several hours out of duty. 'He disagrees.'

'Mine thinks results are the whole point,' Sunita said. 'We're having the same argument from opposite directions.'

He smiled at that — a real one, which arrived slowly. 'Vikram,' he said, holding out his hand. 'And this sulking is Rohan.'

'Sunita. The other sulking is Anika.'

The children eyed each other with the wary assessment of the young. Anika said, 'You run fast.' Rohan said, 'So do you.' This appeared to resolve something. They wandered off together in the direction of the juice stall without further ceremony.

Sunita looked after them. 'Well,' she said.

'I was going to go after the relay,' Vikram said.

'I was going to go after the sprint.'

'The relay is at eleven-fifteen.'

'The prize-giving is at three.'

They stood there on the edge of the track in the November sun while children ran past them with batons and ribbons and the school's PA system crackled Hindi film songs between announcements. It occurred to Sunita that she had forgotten what it felt like to stand next to someone and feel no particular urgency to leave.

Chapter 2: Excuses

The relay was at eleven-fifteen. Neither of them left.

They sat on the metal folding chairs along the far side of the field — Anika and Rohan had migrated to the same row of seats with the specific efficiency of children who had decided their parents needed management. Sunita was aware of this. She suspected Vikram was too. She chose not to address it.

He was a civil engineer, she learned, though he said it the way people said things they had stopped finding interesting — factually, without flourish. She was in educational publishing, which surprised him in a way that made her feel pleasantly seen. They talked about Pune, where both of them lived, in the way that people who have both arrived in a city from elsewhere talk — noting the things they'd expected, the things that had caught them off guard, the parts they had learned to love.

At noon there was a lunch break. The school had set up a small canteen — idli, vada, tea, Rooh Afza for the children. Rohan wanted Rooh Afza. Anika wanted Rooh Afza. The children went to get Rooh Afza. Vikram and Sunita went to get tea and found themselves standing side by side holding paper cups while the school fielded someone's parents' complaint about the lack of parking.

'Do you do this often?' she asked. 'The whole-day sports thing.'

'Rohan's been asking me to come to more school events,' Vikram said. 'I work too much. It's — I've been trying to be better about it. Since his mother—' He stopped. 'I lost my wife three years ago,' he said, in the careful way people say things that are hard but that they have had practice saying.

'I'm sorry,' Sunita said, meaning it completely.

'Your—' he began, and then caught himself.

'Divorce,' she said. 'Two years. Mutual, in the end. Also not easy in the end.'

He nodded. Not with pity, which she appreciated. With something more like recognition.

'It changes how you look at things,' he said.

'It changes what you think you want,' she agreed.

The tea was gone. The children came back. The afternoon session started. She thought: the prize-giving is at three. She thought: that gives me three hours. She thought: what am I going to do with that information? She kept sitting.

Chapter 3: After the Prizes

The prize-giving was not at three. It was at three-forty, which was announced at two-fifty in a tone that suggested this had always been the plan, and by that point Sunita had stopped inventing reasons and simply accepted that she was going to be here until the end.

Anika won second in the obstacle course, which she accepted with slightly better grace this time — Rohan had coached her on the philosophy of participation during the lunch break, which Sunita found both funny and a little heartbreaking. Rohan won the spelling bee, which was not a sports event but which the school had inexplicably included, and he was so startled by his own victory that he stood on the podium for a long moment before remembering to come down.

Vikram laughed — a full, unguarded laugh — and beside her Sunita felt something she had not felt in a long time, which was the particular delight of watching someone laugh at their child with uncomplicated love.

Afterward, the field was full of families folding chairs and collecting forgotten water bottles, and the children ran to find each other to say goodbye, and Sunita found herself standing with Vikram in the thinning crowd.

'This was a good day,' he said. He said it with a small amount of surprise, the way people say things they hadn't expected to mean.

'Yes,' she said.

A pause. The school's PA system played one final crackly announcement. Somewhere, a child shrieked with the pure joy of being done.

'There's a school fete next month,' he said. 'Rohan's class is running a stall.' He looked at the program in his hand rather than at her. 'I'll probably have to come for that too.'

Sunita looked at the program in his hand. She had one of her own, slightly crumpled. On it, in her own handwriting from earlier in the afternoon, she had written the time of the relay, and underneath it, without entirely meaning to, the name of the restaurant they had walked past on the way in, which she had mentioned offhandedly because she'd heard the dosa was good.

'I might bring Anika to that,' she said. 'If she's not busy.'

'Good,' he said. He looked up then. 'Good.'

Anika and Rohan came running back. The children said goodbye to each other with the brisk efficiency of the young, already planning to see each other again. Sunita and Vikram said goodbye to each other with the slightly more careful manner of people who are aware that they're beginning something, even if neither of them has used that word yet.

She drove home through the Pune traffic with Anika talking steadily about Rohan and the spelling bee and whether they could come to the fete, and Sunita drove and listened and thought: second attempts, she had decided after the divorce, were for people who believed in them. She had not thought she did. She thought, now, that she might have been wrong.
